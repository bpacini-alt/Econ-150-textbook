Unit 4 · When Competition Breaks Down

Imperfect Competition II:
Oligopoly, Cartels, and Collusion

When your best move depends on theirs

Chapter 24~ 50 min read

In 1975, Pepsi started running blind taste tests. People sipped from two unmarked cups and picked the one they liked better. More of them picked Pepsi, and Pepsi turned the results into a national ad campaign called the Pepsi Challenge.

Coke was still the best-selling soda, but its lead was shrinking. Coca-Cola ran its own secret taste tests and got the same answer: in a quick sip, people liked a sweeter cola. So the company did something it hadn't done in 99 years. On April 23, 1985, it changed the formula. The new Coke was sweeter, more like Pepsi.

Pepsi declared victory. Its CEO said the two companies had been going at it eyeball to eyeball, and “the other guy just blinked.” Coke drinkers were furious. The company got as many as 8,000 angry calls a day and about 40,000 letters, and one was addressed to “Chief Dodo, The Coca-Cola Company.” On July 11, 1985, 79 days after the switch, Coca-Cola brought the original back as Coca-Cola Classic. By the end of the year, Coke had kept its place as the best-selling soda.

You can't make sense of New Coke by looking at Coca-Cola alone. Coke changed its product because of what Pepsi did, and Pepsi's next ads were about what Coke had done. That is life in a market with only a few big sellers, where every move is watched and every firm has to guess what the others will do next.

The big picture
When only a few firms share a market, none can choose its price in isolation — each one's best move depends on what the others will do. The outcome is set by interlocking incentives, not by a price the model reads off a curve. The same incentive that tempts rivals to cooperate is also the one that keeps pulling cooperation apart.

Major concept 1

Oligopoly

A Few Firms, Watching Each Other

An oligopoly is a market run by a few firms: a handful of airlines on a route, a few wireless carriers, Coca-Cola and Pepsi. In the United States, three companies (Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, and Keurig Dr Pepper) make most of the soda in the cooler. What makes it its own structure is what that small number does to each firm's decisions: mutual interdependence. When there are only a few sellers, each one is big enough that its move visibly moves the others. Drop your price and rivals feel it; raise it and they notice. You cannot pretend you are alone.

That single feature sets oligopoly apart from every structure so far. A monopolist could ignore rivals because it had none. A perfectly competitive firm could ignore rivals because there were too many to matter — one farmer's choice is a drop in the ocean. The oligopolist sits in the uncomfortable middle: few enough that each rival matters, too few to disappear into a crowd. So before it can choose anything, it has to ask a question the other structures never faced: what will they do if I do this?

Why do some markets end up with only a few firms? Usually because something makes entry hard. These are the barriers to entry from Chapter 22, a bit weaker than a monopolist's but still real: large economies of scale (an airline or a cell network costs billions to build), control of key resources, patents, and government licenses. Soda shows a few of them at once. A new cola would need bottling and delivery networks that reach every store, plus a brand that can sit next to names built with billions of dollars of advertising every year. In Chapter 23, advertising couldn't keep rivals out of a market where anyone could open next door. At this scale it can help: just getting noticed next to Coke and Pepsi costs a fortune. Because barriers like these keep new firms out, an oligopolist can earn economic profit that lasts into the long run, the kind of profit that entry would compete away in monopolistic competition. The products themselves can be nearly identical, like steel or gasoline, or clearly different, like cars, phones, and soft drinks. What every oligopoly shares is a small number of firms that can't ignore one another.

Major concept 2

Strategic Behavior

Your Best Move Depends on Theirs

This book has run on one idea since the first chapter: people respond to incentives. Oligopoly is where that idea folds back on itself. Your incentive now depends on your rival's incentive, which depends on yours. You can't read your best move off a cost curve, because the payoff to cutting your price is enormous if the rival holds steady and ruinous if the rival cuts too. The decision is strategic: it has to account for a thinking opponent who is, at this very moment, accounting for you.

Key aphorism

The key lesson of game theory is to put yourself in the other player’s shoes.

Avinash Dixit and Barry Nalebuff, The Art of Strategy (2008), Chapter 1. Reason from the other player’s incentives and likely responses as well as your own.

Price fights work the same way on a street corner. Two gas stations face each other across an intersection, and both post $3.40. Each owner knows that dropping alone to $3.30 would pull the cars across the street. But the next morning the rival sees the empty pumps and drops to $3.25. By the weekend both are at $3.05, busier than ever and earning less than when they started. Neither owner did anything foolish. Each made a sensible move at every step, and together they ended up where neither wanted to be.

Holding the high price together is comfortable and profitable. But each owner can see a private prize: undercut while the other holds, and capture the whole street. That temptation comes from the way their incentives are set up, and both owners see the same prize. So both reach for it, and the comfortable outcome falls apart into a price war. To see why sensible moves can lead somewhere neither player wants, we need a tool built for choices that depend on each other.

Why a new tool
Supply-and-demand curves assume a firm reacts to a price. In an oligopoly a firm reacts to another firm's decision — and that firm is reacting back. For interlocking choices like these, economists reach for game theory and its central picture, the payoff matrix.
Quick checkWhy can't one gas station owner find her best price just from her own costs and demand?▸
Because the profit from any price depends on what the station across the street charges, and that owner is deciding at the same time while thinking about what she will do. She has to predict her rival's response, which makes the choice strategic.

Major concept 3

The Prisoner's Dilemma

Why Rational Choices Can Leave Everyone Worse Off

A payoff matrix lays out every combination of choices and what each player gets. Let's build one for the two gas stations and call them Firm A and Firm B. Each has two choices: hold the high price (cooperate) or cut it (cheat). Two choices each gives four possible outcomes. The first step is to find Firm A's best response to each thing B might do. Suppose B holds the high price. Then A earns $10 by holding and $14 by cutting, so cutting is better. Now suppose B cuts. A earns $2 by holding and $5 by cutting, so cutting is better again. Cutting wins for A no matter what B does, which makes it A's dominant strategy. Next we do the same for B. The game is symmetric, so B's numbers work the same way, and cutting is B's dominant strategy too.

Finally, we put the two together. Both firms cut, so they land in the bottom-right cell with $5 each. That cell is a Nash equilibrium: given that the other firm is cutting, neither can do better by changing its own choice. Yet both would earn $10 in the top-left cell if they both held. When each player's sensible choice leads to an outcome that is worse for both, economists call it a prisoner's dilemma. It is the most important idea in this chapter, and it reaches well past gas stations. Arms races, doping in sports, climate negotiations, and overfishing (the tragedy of the commons from Chapter 18) all share its shape.

Worked exampleSolving the game in three steps
  1. Find A's best response to each of B's moves. B holds → A cuts ($14 > $10). B cuts → A cuts ($5 > $2). A's dominant strategy: cut.
  2. Find B's best response to each of A's moves. By symmetry, B's dominant strategy: cut.
  3. The cell where both dominant strategies meet is the Nash equilibrium. Both cut → ($5, $5).
Nash equilibrium: both cut, $5 each. The cooperative ($10, $10) is better for both but unreachable when each can privately gain by cheating.
Why “prisoner's”?
In 1950, two mathematicians at the RAND Corporation, Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher, built a game with exactly this shape. Albert Tucker, a Princeton mathematician, turned it into a story about two suspects, and the name stuck. Police arrest two partners in crime and question them in separate rooms. If both stay silent, each serves a short sentence on a minor charge. If one confesses and the other stays silent, the one who talked goes free and the other gets a long sentence. If both confess, both serve fairly long sentences. Whatever the partner does, each suspect does better by confessing. So both confess, and both end up worse off than if they'd kept quiet. Swap “confess” for “cut the price,” and you have the two gas stations.

The same shape shows up when firms compete with advertising instead of prices. Coca-Cola and PepsiCo each spend billions of dollars a year advertising their products. Suppose the ads mostly cancel each other out, and both companies cut back. Each would save a fortune and keep about the same customers. But if one cuts back while the other keeps advertising, the quiet one loses customers. So advertising heavily is each firm's dominant strategy, and both end up spending billions partly just to stay even. That is a prisoner's dilemma too.

Quick checkCoke and Pepsi might both earn more if each cut its ad budget in half. Why don't they?▸
Each firm does better by advertising no matter what the other does. If the rival cuts back, advertising wins customers from it. If the rival keeps advertising, advertising protects your own customers. Advertising is a dominant strategy for both, so they land in the costly outcome, just like the price-cutting gas stations.

Not every game is that tidy. Sometimes only one player has a dominant strategy. Then you find the Nash equilibrium in a different order: start with the player who has a dominant strategy, then ask what the other player's best response to it is. Worked exampleWhen only one firm has a dominant strategy
  1. Check Main Street. If Corner delivers, Main Street earns $9 by delivering and $6 by not. If Corner doesn't, Main Street earns $8 by delivering and $5 by not. Delivering wins both times, so it is Main Street's dominant strategy.
  2. Check Corner. If Main Street delivers, Corner earns $4 by delivering and $5 by not, so it shouldn't. If Main Street doesn't deliver, Corner earns $7 by delivering and $6 by not, so it should. Corner's best move depends on Main Street, so Corner has no dominant strategy.
  3. Put them together. Main Street will deliver no matter what. Corner's best response to that is not to deliver.
Nash equilibrium: Main Street delivers and Corner doesn't ($5 for Corner, $8 for Main Street). Neither can do better by switching alone, even though only one of them has a dominant strategy.
What we're leaving out, on purpose
Economists model oligopoly many ways — Cournot (firms pick quantities), Bertrand (firms pick prices), the kinked-demand curve, sequential games with a first mover. Each captures a different slice of reality. At this level the payoff matrix carries the essential logic without the machinery; the others are refinements you would meet in a later course.
Retrieval practiceIn your own words, why is the prisoner's dilemma outcome a Nash equilibrium even though both players dislike it?▸

Because at that outcome, neither player can do better by changing strategy alone. Given that your rival is cutting, you still earn more by cutting than by holding. Both would prefer to cooperate, but neither can move there unilaterally without being exploited — so the bad outcome is stable.

Retrieval practiceWhat is the word for a strategy that is best for a player no matter what the other player does?▸

A dominant strategy.

Misconception“The Nash equilibrium is the best outcome for the players.”▸

A Nash equilibrium is a stable outcome: no player can do better by switching alone. It can still be worse for everyone than another cell. In the pricing game, both firms prefer ($10, $10) to the Nash equilibrium of ($5, $5). The prisoner's dilemma is the classic case where the stable outcome and the best shared outcome come apart.

Major concept 4

Cartels and Collusion

Why cartels rarely last

If competition drives firms to the bad $5 cell, the obvious fix — for the firms, not for consumers — is to agree to sit in the good $10 cell. That agreement is collusion, and a formal version is a cartel: rivals coordinating to behave like a single monopolist, splitting the monopoly profit. In most places this is illegal precisely because it recreates monopoly harm without monopoly's occasional justifications. In the United States, the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 makes price-fixing agreements a crime.

Key aphorism

People of the same trade seldom meet together … but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public.

Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, I.x, Part II (excerpt). Sellers can gain by coordinating high prices while buyers lose.

But notice what the payoff matrix already told us: the cooperative cell is not a Nash equilibrium. The instant both firms agree to hold the high price, each one faces the same private temptation — cut a little, capture the street, jump from $10 to $14 while the partner is still playing nice. Every member of a cartel has, built into the deal, a personal incentive to break it. That is why cartels are chronically unstable. The incentive that makes the deal attractive is the same one that breaks it.

So why do some cartels last? Because the gas-station story repeats. When the same firms meet day after day, cheating today invites retaliation tomorrow. A simple strategy — tit-for-tat: cooperate, then do whatever your rival just did — turns the long-run incentive around. Cheat once and you trade a single big day for a lasting price war. When the future matters enough, the threat of retaliation can hold the high price together. This is why the answer to “does oligopoly end in cooperation or in a price war?” is it depends on the game: on how many firms there are, how visible their moves are, how patient they are, and how often they meet. Oligopoly has no single predicted outcome, and that is itself an important result.

OPEC shows both sides of this. It is a cartel of oil-producing countries, not companies, so no antitrust court can break it up, and it has been meeting since 1960. It has also lived with constant cheating, as members pump more than their quotas. In 1985 Saudi Arabia got tired of holding back while others cheated and raised its own output, and by 1986 oil prices had fallen by more than half.

It happened again in 2020. Since 2016, OPEC and a group of outside producers led by Russia, together called OPEC+, had held prices up by agreeing to pump less. In early March 2020, with a new virus shutting down travel and the world needing less oil every week, Saudi Arabia pushed for deeper cuts. On March 6, Russia said no. Two days later, Saudi Arabia did the opposite of cutting: it slashed its prices and announced it would pump far more oil in April. Prices had their biggest one-day drop since 1991. The pandemic did most of the damage, but the price war piled on, and on April 20 a contract for U.S. crude settled below minus $37 a barrel. Sellers paid buyers to take it. Within weeks, with prices collapsing for everyone, the same countries were back at the table. They agreed to deep cuts in April and agreed in June to keep cutting. The repeated game had pulled them back together.

Some economists go further and argue that, because of all the cheating, OPEC raises prices much less than its reputation suggests.

Collusion comes in two forms. Explicit collusion is an actual agreement: a meeting, a phone call, a signed price list. In the United States and most other countries it is illegal. Tacit (or implicit) collusion has no agreement at all. Firms settle into a high price because each expects the others to answer any cut, and each watches and matches on its own. Since no deal is made, tacit coordination is usually within the law, which is part of why regulators find it hard to police. Economists call a high-price outcome that firms manage to keep up a cooperative equilibrium, and the both-cut outcome of a one-time game a noncooperative equilibrium.

Key aphorism

The biggest threat to the cartel is the cartel.

Each member can gain by undercutting an agreement that everyone else honors. Repeated dealings and retaliation can restrain that temptation, but the temptation remains.

Quick checkA cartel sets a high price and every member agrees. What is each member's immediate incentive, and what does it imply for the cartel's stability?▸
Each member's immediate incentive is to cheat — cut price slightly and capture extra sales while the others hold. Since every member faces that same incentive, cartels tend to be unstable and break down, unless repeated interaction and the threat of retaliation make cheating unprofitable over time.

AP Micro practice

The moves you can now make

Reading a 2×2 game is the core AP oligopoly skill — dominant strategy, Nash equilibrium, and the dilemma. Try each before revealing.

Identify

In a 2×2 game, Firm X earns more by advertising than by not advertising whether or not Firm Y advertises. What do we call X's “advertise” choice, and how did you know?

Reveal answer▸

A dominant strategy — it is X's best choice regardless of what Y does. You confirm it by checking X's payoff against both of Y's moves and seeing that “advertise” wins in each.

Reasoning

If both firms have a dominant strategy, where is the Nash equilibrium, and why is it stable?

Reveal answer▸

At the cell where both dominant strategies meet. It is stable because neither firm can improve by switching alone — each is already doing its best given the other's choice, so no one has a reason to move.

Application

Two firms collude to hold a high price. Explain why the agreement is fragile in a one-time game but can survive if the firms interact repeatedly.

Reveal answer▸

In a one-shot game each firm's dominant strategy is to cheat, so the high price unravels immediately. With repetition, cheating today triggers retaliation tomorrow; if firms are patient enough, the long-run loss from a price war outweighs the one-time gain from cheating, so cooperation can hold.

Market thesis

When a few firms share a market, the outcome is decided by interlocking incentives rather than by any single price — and the very incentive that makes cooperation tempting is the one that keeps pulling it apart.

If economists ruled the world…

every cartel would collapse on its members' own incentive to cheat — and the ones that didn't would tell us something is quietly holding them together, and send us looking for what.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Oligopoly is defined by mutual interdependence: each firm's best move depends on its rivals'.
  • In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, cutting is the dominant strategy and the Nash equilibrium — worse for both than cooperating.
  • Repeated play and credible retaliation can sustain cooperation that a single round cannot.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Where is the line between lawful matching of a rival and illegal collusion?
  • If outcomes are genuinely indeterminate, should regulators lean toward more scrutiny or less?
  • When is the cooperation we'd applaud between friends the cooperation we'd prosecute between firms?
Takeaways
In your life

In any relationship you'll be in again tomorrow, your willingness to retaliate is what makes cooperation possible. Spend that credibility carelessly and you fall back into the outcome nobody wanted.

In your work

If you're matching a rival's every price move, you're stuck in the dilemma. The lawful way out isn't to coordinate prices — it's to compete on something they can't instantly copy, so you're no longer playing the same losing game.

Key aphorisms
  1. The key lesson of game theory is to put yourself in the other player’s shoes.
  2. People of the same trade seldom meet together … but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public.
  3. The biggest threat to the cartel is the cartel.
