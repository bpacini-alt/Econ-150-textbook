Unit 4 · When Competition Breaks Down

Market Power I: Monopoly

When big becomes power — and what power can do

Chapter 21~ 55 min read

In the late nineteenth century, John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil into a corporate empire that controlled nearly 90 percent of America's oil refining capacity. He did it with two strategies that worked together. He bought out rival refineries across the country, an approach economists call horizontal integration. At the same time, he acquired the barrel factories, chemical plants, pipelines, and rail tank cars needed to bring oil to market, a strategy known as vertical integration. Rockefeller soon ran into a thorny legal problem, though. State laws limited a corporation chartered in one state from owning stock in companies located in another. In 1882, a Standard Oil lawyer named Samuel Dodd devised a clever workaround. The shareholders of forty separate companies handed their stock and voting rights to a small board of nine “trustees,” in exchange for certificates entitling them to a share of the combined profits. The corporate trust was born, and it gave Rockefeller centralized control over an entire industry.

The arrangement proved so effective at coordinating prices and locking out rivals that other industries quickly copied it, giving rise to the Sugar Trust, the Whiskey Trust, and the Beef Trust. When Congress passed the Sherman Act in 1890 to rein in the trusts, the fight against them gave the whole field its name. That's why competition law is still called antitrust law, even though the nineteenth-century legal trust has long since vanished.

By the time the U.S. Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil broken into thirty-four independent companies in 1911, Standard Oil had become the textbook definition of monopoly, with all its interesting questions. When does a company shift from simply being big to holding genuine market power? What is the difference between merely holding market power and actively harming consumers? Standard Oil's sheer scale drove down the retail price of kerosene by making refining far more efficient. Yet its dominance also gave it the power to crush upstart rivals, dictate terms to railroads, and restrict competition whenever it chose. Unlike the competitive firms we have studied so far, which must accept whatever price the market hands them, a monopoly stands alone, without close substitutes. In this chapter, we examine how monopolies are defined and measured, how they decide what to produce and what to charge, and how economists weigh the trade-offs between a firm's scale, its market power, and the well-being of consumers.

The big picture
A monopoly can choose its price, but buyers still decide how much they will buy at that price. If a monopoly can price discriminate, it will charge each customer the most that customer will pay and keep all the gains. If it has to charge everyone the same price, it sells less than a competitive market would, and some trades that would have helped both sides never happen. Those lost trades are the main economic cost of monopoly.

Major concept 1

Market Structures

How many sellers, how alike their products are, and how hard it is to get in

For most of this course, we have studied markets that work like the wheat market in Chapter 10: many buyers and many sellers, a product buyers see as identical from one seller to the next, easy entry and exit, and good information. In a market like that, no single firm can change the price. A farmer who asks a dollar more than the going price sells nothing.

Unit 3 took some of those assumptions away, one at a time. When a trade spilled costs onto people outside it, we got externalities. When nobody could be kept off a shared pasture, we got the tragedy of the commons. When one side of a trade knew more than the other, we got adverse selection and moral hazard. Through all of it, one assumption stayed in place: lots of firms competing for the same customers.

Now we drop that assumption. We shift from perfect competition, with its idealized assumptions, to imperfect competition, where firms can build up power, capture gains, and harm consumers. Economists sort markets into four market structures, mostly by asking three questions. How many firms sell in the market? Are their products the same or different? And how hard is it for a new firm to get in?

StructureSellersProductEntryControl over priceExamples
Perfect competitionManyIdenticalEasyNone; each firm is a price takerWheat, soybeans, eggs sold to a wholesaler
Monopolistic competitionManyDifferent from seller to sellerEasyA littleRestaurants, hair salons, coffee shops
OligopolyA fewIdentical or differentHardSome, but each firm has to watch its rivalsAirlines on a route, cell phone carriers, Coke and Pepsi
MonopolyOneNo close substitutesBlockedA lot, but still limited by demandA city's water system, a drug under patent, Standard Oil at its peak

Some famous cases. True monopolies are rarer than the word suggests, but history has a few besides Standard Oil. For most of the twentieth century, De Beers controlled around 80 percent of the world's rough diamonds, until new mines in Russia, Australia, and Canada wore that share down. Today the U.S. Postal Service has a legal monopoly on delivering ordinary letters. Oligopolies are easier to find: a few airlines fly most routes, and a few carriers sell nearly all cell phone plans.

Placing a real market. Perfect competition and monopoly are both highly stylized versions of reality: rare in real life, common in textbooks. Each one still teaches you how markets behave under different conditions, even though most real markets sit somewhere between the two extremes. Most firms are in between, and Chapters 23 and 24 cover those. To place a market, start by counting the sellers buyers can actually turn to.

One warning before you start sorting. A big market share, a famous brand, or high profits can't tell you on their own whether a firm is a monopoly. Nor can they tell you whether a large firm is actually harming consumers. In this chapter, we move from everyday intuitions about bigness and badness toward precise definitions of monopoly, market power, and harm.

Major concept 2

Monopolies

One seller, no close substitutes, and the power to set a price

A monopoly is a single seller of a good with no close substitutes. Both parts of that definition matter. Only one company makes Cheerios, but Cheerios isn't a monopoly, because a shopper who thinks the price is too high can grab Corn Flakes or the store brand. The test is always the same: if this seller raises its price, what can buyers turn to?

Market power is the ability to influence price. A firm in perfect competition has none. It faces a horizontal demand curve for its own output, so it takes the market price as given. A monopolist faces the entire market demand curve, which slopes down. That makes it a price maker (some books say price setter): it can raise its price and keep some of its customers. It can't raise its price and keep all of them. A monopolist can choose its price or its quantity, but not both. Once it picks one, demand decides the other.

Key aphorism

What holds the monopolist accountable? The demand curve.

Monopolists have much more price-setting power than firms in competition, but even that power is limited: the consumer can ultimately walk away.

Where the harm comes from. Having market power isn't the same as using it to hurt buyers. When a monopoly does cause economic harm, the harm usually comes from restricted output. To keep its price up, a monopolist sells less than a competitive market would, and some trades that would have helped both buyer and seller never happen. Section 4 shows exactly how that works.

Measuring market power. How do economists and courts decide whether a firm really is a monopoly? First they define the market: the product and the place. Then they look at how sales are divided among the firms in it. Two common measures:

MeasureWhat it isRough rule of thumb
Market shareOne firm's share of all sales in the market.Courts rarely find monopoly power below 50 percent. A share of 65 to 70 percent or more is usually strong evidence of it.
Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI)Square each firm's market share (in percent) and add them up. One firm with 100 percent gives 10,000; 100 firms with 1 percent each give 100.Federal merger guidelines call a market with an HHI above 1,800 highly concentrated.

Standard Oil, with about 90 percent of refining, would have had an HHI of at least 8,100 (90 × 90) from its own share alone.

Good to know, not on the test
You won't be tested on the HHI or these thresholds in this course, and there's no practice for them. The point is simply that market power can be measured carefully and with numbers. Economists, regulators, and courts don't just eyeball whether a firm seems too big.

Big isn't the same as powerful, and powerful isn't the same as harmful. A national pizza chain can be huge and still have little market power, because hungry customers have dozens of close substitutes. Going the other way, a firm with real market power doesn't always leave buyers worse off. A city's water company has enormous power, but if regulators keep its price close to its cost, the city may be better served by one water company than by two (Chapter 22 explains why). A firm whose only advantage is a product nobody else can match yet may charge more than it costs to make, and its customers can still be better off than they were before it existed. The question this chapter keeps asking is narrower: is the firm selling less than a competitive market would, so that worthwhile trades don't happen?

Reading the graphs. You can often tell what kind of firm a graph describes by looking at two things: the demand curve the firm faces and the shape of its costs. A perfectly competitive firm's demand curve is a horizontal line at the market price. The firm can sell as much as it wants at that price, so the price line is also its demand and its marginal revenue. A monopoly faces the whole market's downward-sloping demand curve, with a marginal revenue curve below it (Section 4.1 explains why), and its average total cost curve is usually U-shaped. A natural monopoly is a market where one firm can serve everyone at a lower average cost than two or more firms could. On a graph, it has the same downward-sloping demand, plus an average total cost curve that is still falling where it crosses demand. Chapter 22 explains why a natural monopoly can be useful.

Major concept 3

The Price-Discriminating Monopoly

Charging each buyer what they are willing to pay

A firm with market power has a pricing choice to make. It can post one price for everyone, the way a grocery store prices milk. Or, if it can tell its customers apart, it can charge different customers different prices. These are two kinds of monopoly, and we'll start with the second, because its logic is simpler.

Price discrimination means charging different buyers different prices for reasons not fully explained by cost differences. Imagine two scenarios. In the first, two customers ship containers of the same weight, and they are charged different prices. Why? One is shipping to the next city over and the other around the world, so the cost of the service really is different. In the second, I welcome students to my lecture hall but give each one an individual tuition bill, based on my appraisal of their ability to pay. Two students sitting in the same lecture hall, hearing the same professor, and taking the same exam should probably pay the same, rather than one being targeted to pay more. That feels intuitively wrong to us: it is a real form of discrimination, and it advantages the seller.

Notice, though, that the mechanics don't change much if you replace a professor's personal avarice with a financial aid office. One student pays close to the sticker price, and another pays far less. The gap comes from sorting: who can pay, who is likely to enroll, and who needs a discount to show up. That is why price discrimination often hides behind friendlier words like discount, scholarship, coupon, or loyalty reward. As a rule, price discrimination is possible only when sellers know something about buyers' willingness to pay, including their ability to pay, or when they can get buyers to reveal it. In short, if sellers know how much money you have and how much you want a good, they can price discriminate.

The extreme case: perfect price discrimination. Imagine a seller who knows exactly what each buyer would pay and charges each buyer exactly that. Economists call this perfect, or first-degree, price discrimination. It is the easiest place to start.

Worked exampleA college that knows what every student will pay

A college has four applicants. Enrolling one more student costs the college $8,000 (its marginal cost).

StudentWillingness to pay (one year)
A$40,000
B$30,000
C$20,000
D$10,000
  1. Charge each student their willingness to pay. A pays $40,000, B pays $30,000, C pays $20,000, and D pays $10,000.
  2. Compare each student with marginal cost. Every student will pay more than the $8,000 a seat costs, so the college admits all four. A fifth applicant willing to pay only $6,000 would be turned away, because that seat would cost more than it brings in.
  3. Add up the surplus. The college's producer surplus is ($40,000 − $8,000) + ($30,000 − $8,000) + ($20,000 − $8,000) + ($10,000 − $8,000) = $68,000. Each student pays their full willingness to pay, so consumer surplus is zero.
All four students enroll. The college keeps $68,000 of surplus, and the students keep none.

Price discrimination can lead to efficient outcomes. Look at what admitting student D does to the college's revenue. D pays $10,000, and nobody else's price changes. So the extra revenue from D, which is D's marginal revenue, is just D's price. Under perfect price discrimination, marginal revenue equals price, and the demand curve is also the marginal revenue curve. The college keeps enrolling students as long as the next one will pay more than a seat costs, and it stops where demand meets marginal cost. A competitive market stops at the same point.

Fig 21.2Perfect price discrimination. Each unit sells for its buyer's willingness to pay, so demand is also MR. The firm sells every unit worth more than it costs, out to Qe, and keeps the whole area between demand and MC.

So perfect price discrimination produces the efficient quantity, with no deadweight loss. Every trade worth making gets made. What changes is who gets the gains. All of the surplus goes to the seller, and buyers get none.

Keep in mind that this is a theoretical result. No real seller knows every buyer's exact willingness to pay, so real price discrimination gets only part of the way there. You may not love the idea of losing out to big firms, but when economists judge efficiency, they are agnostic about who ends up with the gains. They focus only on the size of the pie. One way to make that feel less strange: imagine a world where your prices are higher, but the company's stock does better, and in some cases you can buy shares of that stock and reap the rewards yourself. We'll come back to this tradeoff in Section 4.3.

Real price discrimination. Perfect price discrimination is mostly a thought experiment, because no seller can read every buyer's mind. Real sellers sort buyers more roughly. Three things have to hold before they can do it at all.

ConditionWhat it meansExample
Market powerThe seller can influence price, not just accept it.A differentiated college, an airline on a route.
SortingThe seller can tell buyers or groups apart by willingness to pay.Aid forms, age, timing, a coupon that rewards searching.
Limited resaleLow-price buyers cannot resell to high-price buyers.A discounted seat or ticket is tied to one person.

Elasticity is escape power. The rule underneath all of it: charge higher prices to groups with less elastic demand and lower prices to groups with more elastic demand. Buyers with good substitutes, flexible timing, or lower incomes can walk away more easily, so they are charged less. Buyers with urgent needs and few substitutes are easier to charge more. A business traveler whose meeting is on Tuesday has less elastic demand than a vacationer who can shift dates, drive, or skip the trip. A coupon-clipper reveals price sensitivity by the very act of clipping.

Economists sort price discrimination by how precisely the seller can read willingness to pay.

TypeHow price differsCommon examples
First degree (perfect)Each buyer pays exactly their willingness to pay.A seller with perfect information; haggling over a used car comes close.
Second degreePrices depend on the characteristics of the purchase: how much a buyer buys, which version, or which package. Buyers sort themselves by what they choose.Bulk discounts; basic, pro, and enterprise software tiers.
Third degreeIdentifiable groups pay different prices.Student and senior discounts, in-state tuition, prices that differ by location.

Not every price difference counts. When a price difference comes from a cost difference, it isn't price discrimination. A car insurer charges a 17-year-old driver more than a 40-year-old because young drivers have more accidents, so insuring them costs more. An electric company that charges more on the hottest afternoons may be covering the higher cost of running extra power plants at peak times. A bigger package that costs more because it holds more product isn't price discrimination either, unless the pricing is built to sort buyers by what they would pay.

Is it legal? Mostly, yes. Student discounts, coupons, and bulk pricing are everywhere. The closest real thing to perfect price discrimination is personalized pricing, sometimes called surveillance pricing, where a company uses data about you, such as your location, browsing, or purchase history, to set the price you see. As of late 2026, no federal law bans it outright, and the Federal Trade Commission has said it doesn't have the authority to prohibit personalized pricing in every case. A few states, starting with Maryland, Connecticut, and New Jersey, now ban it for groceries, and New York requires companies to say when personal data helped set a price. Expect these rules to keep changing.

Major concept 4

The Single-Price Monopoly

One price for everyone, and what it costs

Most sellers can't do what the college did. A grocery store doesn't know what you would pay for milk, and even if it did, a shopper who got a cheap gallon could resell it to one who didn't. So most firms with market power post one price for everyone. That one change, a single price instead of many, is what makes monopoly costly. This section follows it through in three parts: what it does to marginal revenue (4.1), how the firm picks its quantity and price (4.2), and what it costs everyone else (4.3).

4.1 Why Marginal Revenue Lies Below Demand

To sell one more, the firm cuts the price on all of them

Demand tells the monopolist the price a buyer pays. Marginal revenue (MR) answers a different question: how much does total revenue change when the firm sells one more unit? Under perfect price discrimination, the answer was the new buyer's price. A single-price monopolist doesn't get that. To sell one more unit, it has to lower the price on every unit it sells, including the ones it could have sold at the higher price.

At one unit, price is $10 and total revenue is $10. To sell two units, the firm lowers the price to $9 for both, so total revenue becomes $18. Buyers pay $9 per unit, but the firm's revenue rises by only $8. The new unit adds $9, and the $1 price cut on the first unit subtracts $1. So MR = $9 − $1 = $8, while the price is $9.

QuantityPriceTotal revenueMarginal revenue
1$10$10$10
2$9$18$8
3$8$24$6
4$7$28$4

This is the same tug-of-war you saw in Chapter 15's total revenue test. A price cut has a quantity effect (the firm sells more units) and a price effect (it gets less for each unit it was already selling). Marginal revenue adds the two together: the price of the new unit minus the revenue lost on the old ones.

When the jump is more than one unit. Demand schedules often move in bigger steps, from 100 units to 200, say. Then you find marginal revenue per unit by dividing: MR = change in total revenue ÷ change in quantity.

QuantityPriceTotal revenueMR per unit
100$10$1,000—
200$8$1,600($1,600 − $1,000) ÷ 100 = $6
300$6$1,800($1,800 − $1,600) ÷ 100 = $2
400$4$1,600($1,600 − $1,800) ÷ 100 = −$2

Going from 100 to 200 units raises total revenue by $600, which is $6 for each of the 100 extra units. Now watch what happens as the price keeps falling. Total revenue rises, levels off, and then falls. Going from 300 to 400 units, the firm sells 100 more units and takes in $200 less, so marginal revenue is negative. Total revenue is highest where marginal revenue reaches zero. A monopolist never chooses to produce where marginal revenue is negative, because those extra units cost money to make and bring in less revenue than before. In Chapter 15's terms, marginal revenue is positive only where demand is elastic, so a monopolist always ends up on the elastic part of its demand curve.

The same arithmetic works for a price increase. If this firm raised its price from $4 to $6, it would sell 100 fewer units, and its total revenue would rise from $1,600 to $1,800.

The MR line on a graph. When demand is a straight line, the MR line starts at the same point on the price axis and falls twice as steeply. It hits zero at half the quantity where demand hits zero. If demand reaches zero at 120 units, MR reaches zero at 60.

Major concept 4 · The single-price monopoly

4.2 Quantity First, Then Price

Quantity: Where MR = MC. Price? As Much as You Can Get Away With.

Picture the owner of the only charter boat on a mountain lake, sitting at her kitchen table on a Sunday night and planning the week. She has two decisions to make: how many tours to run, and what to charge for them. Most of us would start with the price. She starts with the quantity, using the same marginal reasoning every firm uses. As long as one more tour adds more to her revenue than it adds to her costs, she runs it. When the next tour would add less revenue than cost, she stops. That stopping point is the monopoly quantity, Qm, where MR = MC.

Only then does she turn to the price. She already knows how many tours she wants to sell, so the question is simple: what is the most her customers will pay for that many? The demand curve answers it. On the graph, go straight up from Qm to the demand curve and read across to the price axis. That price is Pm. It sits above both MR and MC, and nothing pulls it down to either one. Buyers will pay Pm for Qm tours, so that is what she charges.

Students tend to take one of two wrong turns on this graph. The first is reading the price at the spot where MR crosses MC. That height is the extra revenue from the last unit, not the price anyone pays. The second is stopping where demand crosses MC. That point is the efficient quantity, Qe, the amount a competitive market would produce. The monopolist stops short of it, and Section 4.3 shows what that costs.

Fig 21.3Single-price monopoly. Find Qm where MR = MC, go up to demand for Pm, compare with ATC for profit, and find Qe where D = MC.
AP watch: monopoly quantity comes from MR = MC. Monopoly price comes from demand.

The same graph also tells you whether the monopolist makes money. Find average total cost (ATC) at Qm and compare it with Pm. Economic profit is (Pm − ATC) × Qm. MR = MC picks the best quantity the firm has, but it doesn't promise a profit. If ATC at Qm is above Pm, the best the firm can do is a smaller loss.

One more consequence: a monopolist has no supply curve. A competitive firm's supply curve tells you how much it will sell at each market price. A monopolist doesn't take a price from the market. It picks its quantity from MR and MC and its price from demand, so there is no single list of prices and quantities to draw.

Worked exampleQuantity first, then price: from a demand schedule

Back to the charter boat. Each extra tour costs her $60 to run (fuel, a deckhand, cleanup), so MC = $60 for every tour. Here is what her customers will pay.

Tours per dayPrice per tourTotal revenueMarginal revenue
1$200$200$200
2$180$360$160
3$160$480$120
4$140$560$80
5$120$600$40
6$100$600$0
  1. Find total revenue. Multiply price by quantity at each row. Four tours at $140 bring in $560.
  2. Find marginal revenue. MR is the change in total revenue. Going from three tours to four raises revenue from $480 to $560, so the fourth tour's MR is $80.
  3. Compare MR with MC, one tour at a time. The fourth tour adds $80 in revenue and $60 in cost, so it raises profit by $20. Run it. The fifth tour adds only $40 in revenue but still costs $60, so it would lower profit by $20. Stop at four.
  4. Read the price from demand. Customers will pay $140 a tour when four tours are offered, so she charges $140.
Qm = 4 tours a day at Pm = $140. She does not charge $80 (the MR of the last tour) or $60 (its MC). Those numbers decide how many tours to run; demand decides the price.
Worked exampleReading a monopoly graph, start to finish

Quiz graphs usually look like this one: a straight demand curve, an MR curve that falls twice as fast, and a flat MC line. Here MC is constant at $4, and average total cost at 40 units is $7.

  1. Find the quantity. Look for the point where MR crosses MC. It sits at 40 units, so Qm = 40.
  2. Find the price. Go straight up from 40 units to the demand curve, then across to the price axis. Pm = $8. The $4 where MR meets MC is not the price.
  3. Find the profit. At 40 units, ATC is $7. The firm earns $8 − $7 = $1 on each unit, so profit = $1 × 40 = $40, the green rectangle.
  4. Compare with the efficient point. Demand meets MC at 80 units, where the price would be $4. The monopolist sells 40 fewer units and charges $4 more. Each of those 40 missing units was worth more to some buyer than the $4 it would cost to make. The red triangle adds up that lost value: ½ × (80 − 40) × ($8 − $4) = $80. Section 4.3 explains why this is a real loss and not just money changing hands.
Qm = 40 units, Pm = $8, profit = $40. The efficient quantity is 80 units, and the deadweight loss is $80.

Major concept 4 · The single-price monopoly

4.3 What a Single Price Costs

Monopolies Produce Too Little; the DWL Is That Some Trades Never Happen

Monopoly creates inefficiency because output is restricted below the socially efficient quantity. The efficient benchmark is where price equals marginal cost, or where demand intersects MC. At that point, every unit whose value exceeds its marginal cost is produced.

A single-price monopolist instead produces where MR = MC. Since MR lies below demand, that quantity is smaller than the efficient quantity. Price is higher, output is lower, and some consumer surplus is transferred to the producer as profit. But that transfer is not the social cost. A transfer just moves surplus from one pocket to another; the economy still has it. The real loss, the deadweight loss, is the surplus that disappears because some mutually beneficial trades, units whose value exceeds their marginal cost, never happen. Nobody captures that value. It is gone.

Key aphorism

The problem with monopoly isn’t bigness; it’s restricted output.

The deadweight loss comes from mutually beneficial trades that never happen. A transfer of surplus from buyers to the firm is a separate issue.

AreaHow to find it on the monopoly graph
Consumer surplusAbove Pm and below demand, up to Qm.
Producer surplusBelow Pm and above MC over the units sold (revenue minus variable cost).
Social surplusConsumer surplus plus producer surplus: the total gain from the trades that happen.
Economic profit(Pm − ATC at Qm) × Qm, shown as the profit rectangle when Pm > ATC.
Deadweight lossThe lost-surplus triangle between Qm and Qe, below demand and above MC.

Producer surplus subtracts variable costs; economic profit also subtracts fixed costs. On the graph, economic profit is the rectangle (Pm − ATC) × Qm.

Worked exampleWhat buyers lose when a market becomes a monopoly

Take a market where demand runs from $12 down to zero at 120 units, and marginal cost is constant at $4. Suppose the market starts out competitive, and then one firm takes it over.

  1. Under competition. Price equals MC, so P = $4 and Q = 80. Consumer surplus is the whole triangle above $4 and below demand, areas A + B + C: ½ × 80 × ($12 − $4) = $320.
  2. Under monopoly. The firm sells 40 units at $8. Consumer surplus shrinks to area A: ½ × 40 × ($12 − $8) = $80. Buyers lose $320 − $80 = $240.
  3. Find the transfer. Area B is the rectangle between $4 and $8 out to 40 units: $4 × 40 = $160. Buyers still make those 40 trades. They just pay $4 more on each one, and that money goes to the firm as producer surplus.
  4. Find the deadweight loss. Area C is the rest: ½ × (80 − 40) × ($8 − $4) = $80. Those are the 40 trades that no longer happen, and nobody gets that $80.
Consumer surplus falls by $240. Of that, $160 (area B) moves to the firm and $80 (area C) disappears. Social surplus falls from $320 to $240.

Back to price discrimination. Now compare the single-price monopolist with the price discriminator from Concept 3. The single-price firm charges one high price, so it shuts out every buyer whose willingness to pay sits above marginal cost but below that price. Those were trades worth making, and losing them is the deadweight loss.

A firm that can price discriminate doesn't have to shut those buyers out. It can sell to them at a lower price without cutting the price for everyone else. Output rises, and deadweight loss shrinks. With perfect price discrimination, output reaches Qe and the deadweight loss disappears.

Fig 21.4A single-price monopolist restricts output and leaves a deadweight-loss triangle (left). A price-discriminating firm serves additional buyers at different prices, pushing output toward Qe and shrinking the deadweight loss — while capturing the surplus as profit (right).

Run the college from Concept 3 both ways. If it has to charge everyone the same price, its best choice is $30,000. Students A and B enroll, and producer surplus is ($30,000 − $8,000) × 2 = $44,000. (At $40,000, only A enrolls, for $32,000. At $20,000, three enroll, for $36,000.) Student A keeps $10,000 of consumer surplus. Students C and D are priced out, even though each would pay more than the $8,000 a seat costs, so $12,000 + $2,000 = $14,000 of surplus is lost. Under perfect price discrimination, all four enroll, the deadweight loss is gone, and the college's surplus rises to $68,000. But student A's $10,000 goes to the college too.

So is price discrimination good? The total gain grows, but the seller keeps the new gain and some of the buyers' old gain as well. Price discrimination can be efficient and unpopular at the same time. That is why monopoly isn't a simple villain story. The same power that destroys surplus when the firm restricts output can win it back when the firm sorts buyers, while changing who ends up with it.

CaseBack to Standard Oil

The chapter opened with two questions about Standard Oil. When does a company go from simply being big to holding real market power? And what is the difference between holding market power and harming consumers?

Was it a monopoly? By the measures in Concept 2, yes. With roughly 90 percent of American refining, its HHI from its own share alone was over 8,100, far past any threshold courts or regulators use. Oil producers and buyers had few other places to turn, and Standard's pipelines and its secret rebate deals with railroads made it hard for a new refiner to get oil to market. It was big, and it was powerful.

Did it harm consumers? This is the harder question, and economists still argue about it. Kerosene got much cheaper during Standard's rise, because its huge refineries really were more efficient. By that measure, buyers gained. But the test from Section 4.3 asks something different: compared with a competitive industry using the same technology, did Standard sell less and charge more? Its power let it squeeze railroads for rebates its rivals couldn't get, and it was accused of cutting prices in one local market at a time until rivals gave up. Tactics like those protect a firm's power, not its efficiency.

The honest answer is that it did both. Standard Oil's scale made oil products cheaper, and its market power let it shut out rivals and restrict competition when it chose. Being big wasn't the problem. Power that didn't have to answer to competitors was. Chapter 22 asks where power like that comes from, and what, if anything, should be done about it.

AP Micro practice

The moves you can now make

Three application checks — revenue, the graph, and the welfare turn. Try each before revealing.

Revenue table

A monopolist can sell 200 units at $8 each or 300 units at $6 each. What is the marginal revenue per unit of the extra 100 units?

Reveal answer▸

TR at 200 units = $1,600. TR at 300 units = $1,800. MR per unit = ($1,800 − $1,600) ÷ 100 = $2. The extra units sell for $6, but the $2 price cut applies to the first 200 units as well, so MR falls far below price.

Graph move

On a monopoly graph, how do you find Qm and Pm, and where is the deadweight loss?

Reveal answer▸

Find Qm where MR = MC. Go straight up from Qm to the demand curve to read Pm. Deadweight loss is the triangle between Qm and Qe (where D = MC), bounded above by demand and below by MC.

Welfare reasoning

A monopolist begins charging a lower price to a more elastic group while holding a higher price for a less elastic group. State one efficiency effect and one distributional effect.

Reveal answer▸

Efficiency: output may rise because buyers who were priced out under one price now buy, reducing deadweight loss.

Distribution: consumer surplus may fall, because the firm captures more of each buyer's willingness to pay as producer surplus.

Market thesis

A monopoly's deepest cost isn't the money it moves from buyers into profit. It is the trades that never happen when one price shuts out buyers who would have paid more than the cost. A firm that can price discriminate wins those trades back, but it keeps the gains.

If economists ruled the world…

they would not punish bigness as such. They would attack the barriers that let firms trap consumers and restrict output. The target is protected power, not success.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • A firm can be big without having much market power, and it can have market power without harming consumers. When monopoly does cause harm, it usually comes from restricted output.
  • For a single-price monopolist, MR lies below demand, so output is restricted and price is raised.
  • Deadweight loss is surplus destroyed, not transferred.
  • Price discrimination can raise output and shrink that loss while capturing surplus as profit.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • When is sorting buyers by willingness to pay fair, and when is it exploitation?
  • If price discrimination raises output, should we still mind that the firm keeps the surplus?
  • Which market power is earned reward, and which is entrenched rent?
Takeaways
In your life

When you're not offered the discount, you may be the inelastic buyer — the one with no easy alternative. Before you pay, find out what your alternatives actually are.

In your work

If you have pricing power, sorting customers by willingness to pay can grow the market, not just the margin — but only if you can keep the cheap version from being resold to the buyer who'd have paid full price.

Key aphorisms
  1. What holds the monopolist accountable? The demand curve.
  2. The problem with monopoly isn’t bigness; it’s restricted output.
