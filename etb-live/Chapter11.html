Try to name a company that has earned large profits for more than a hundred years without some kind of protection: a government license, a patent, a trade barrier, an exclusive franchise, a network nobody else can copy. It sounds easy. Big old companies are everywhere, and famous brands last for generations. But big profits that last are much harder to find than big companies, because profit attracts attention.

When one firm finds an easy way to make money, other firms notice. They copy it, enter the market, undercut the price, and improve on the original. What started as a brilliant opportunity becomes ordinary. The market doesn't let an easy profit sit quietly in a corner; it points at it.

Chapter 10 showed what a firm does with the plant it has, this month. But give it time and everything is on the table: it can build a bigger plant, sell the one it has, or leave the industry, and so can everyone else. So what happens to a profit once every rival is free to chase it? And what is left for the firm that found it first?

The big picture
Competition is the consumer's ally. In the long run, nothing is fixed: firms choose how big to build, enter markets where profit beckons, and leave markets that lose money. Free entry pushes economic profit toward zero and hands much of the gain to buyers, which is hard on firms and good for everyone else. Firms that want lasting profit have two choices: keep creating new value, or block the door. By the end you should be able to read the long-run average cost curve, trace the long-run adjustment on side-by-side graphs, trace a demand or cost shock through the short run and the long run, state the long-run equilibrium condition and explain why it is efficient, tell profit that was earned from profit that is protected, and sort barriers to entry by where they came from.

Major concept 1 · Nothing is fixed

The Long Run and Economies of Scale

For two chapters, something has stayed fixed: the oven, the lease, the building. That is what made it the short run. The long run is long enough that every input can change: sign a bigger lease, buy a second oven, build a new kitchen, or sell the whole thing. Neither is a set number of months. A food truck's long run might be a few weeks; a chip factory's is years. The long run is however long it takes to change the slowest input.

One consequence follows directly: in the long run, there are no fixed costs. The rent you're locked into today becomes, at renewal, one option among several. That is the formal version of the "cost as opportunity" reading from Chapter 9: given enough time, a firm can rethink any cost it now treats as fixed.

Choosing a plant. Suppose the workshop is planning ahead and can choose among four sizes of shop. Once built, each has its own fixed cost and its own bottleneck, so each has its own short-run ATC curve — its own U. The small shop is the workshop from Chapter 9, which does best at 4 units. Bigger shops cost more to run at low output but can handle more before they jam. For whatever output it plans to produce, the firm picks the shop with the lowest average cost. Traced across every output, those lowest costs form the long-run average total cost curve (LRATC). Its shape has three parts.

Economies of scale. Where LRATC falls, bigger is cheaper per unit: the firm has economies of scale. A bigger operation can split work into narrower specialties, run larger and faster machines, and buy inputs in bulk. Costco stocks only about 4,000 items, so it buys each in enormous volume and negotiates with suppliers in a way a corner store never could. Where up-front costs are huge and each extra copy is cheap, the effect is dramatic: a drug may cost a billion dollars to develop and pennies to manufacture, and the same holds for a film, a video game, or a semiconductor fab.

Constant returns to scale. Where LRATC is flat, doubling every input roughly doubles output, so cost per unit holds steady and a firm can grow without getting cheaper or costlier per unit. The lowest output at which LRATC reaches its minimum is the minimum efficient scale — 8 units in the table. When that scale is small relative to the market, as for restaurants, salons, and contractors, small and large firms survive side by side.

Diseconomies of scale. Where LRATC rises, the firm has grown past its best size and bigger is costlier per unit: diseconomies of scale. The bottleneck returns in a new form. In the long run you can buy another oven, but you can't easily buy more of the founder's attention. As a firm adds layers, information passes through more hands, decisions wait on more approvals, and more of the payroll goes to coordinating the work rather than doing it.

Keep the two time horizons separate. A rising short-run curve comes from piling variable inputs onto a fixed one; a rising long-run curve comes from an organization too large to coordinate. Both raise costs, for different reasons. Your exam checks whether you can tell them apart.

Misconception "Any rising cost is diseconomies of scale." ▸

Diseconomies of scale is a long-run idea: every input has grown, and the organization itself has become hard to coordinate. A short-run MC curve that rises because workers crowd a fixed kitchen is diminishing marginal returns.

And a cost curve that shifts up because an input got pricier is neither one: that is a cost shock (Concept 4). Ask which time frame you are in, and whether the firm is moving along a curve or the curve itself is moving.

Reflection · why big firms often have an edge

In college, my business strategy professor asked us why Walmart so clearly dominates its market. Our answer: "because… Walmart is big?" It's tempting to think large firms dominate because they're powerful. Often the causation runs the other way. Walmart didn't start big; it revolutionized its supply chain, and the cost advantage that produced is what let it grow. Amazon's warehouses and a chipmaker's fab work the same way: when minimum efficient scale is enormous, smaller rivals face a higher cost per unit, and only a few firms can survive. Whether that scale passes savings to customers or walls out competitors is the question Concept 6 starts to answer.

In the short run at least one input is fixed; in the long run every input can change. Neither is a set length of time. LRATC falls where there are economies of scale (specialization, bigger machines, bulk buying, huge up-front costs spread over huge output), is flat under constant returns (minimum efficient scale is where the flat part begins), and rises under diseconomies of scale (coordination costs of a too-large organization). Major concept 2 · Profit is a flare

Entry, Exit, and the Long-Run Adjustment

In the short run, profit is a scoreboard: it tells the firm whether it is beating its next-best alternative. In the long run, profit is also a flare. Everyone else can see it, and in the long run they can act on it.

Entry is what happens when profit draws in competitors: new firms start up and existing ones expand, so market supply rises. Exit is what happens when losses push firms out: they close, sell their equipment, and move their money and people elsewhere, so market supply falls. Exit is not the same as the short-run shutdown from Chapter 10. A firm that shuts down still owes its fixed costs and can reopen next week; a firm that exits has escaped them for good.

Trace the profit case on two graphs at once. On the firm's graph, price is above ATC, so there is a profit rectangle. That profit draws in new firms, which shifts the market supply curve to the right. The market price falls, and since each firm takes that price as given, its horizontal price line falls too. Each step down shrinks the profit rectangle. Entry keeps going until the rectangle is gone: price has fallen to the bottom of ATC, and economic profit is zero.

Losses run the same story backward. Firms exit, market supply shifts left, the price rises, and the remaining firms' losses shrink until price is back at the bottom of ATC. Easy profits come and go for the same reason: anything easy to earn is easy to copy.

Fig 11.2 Profit invites entry. New firms shift market supply from S₁ to S₂, and the price falls from P₁ to P₂. Each firm's price line falls with it, and the profit rectangle shrinks until P₂ sits at the bottom of ATC.
What to notice: the firm produces less after entry (q₂ is left of q₁), even though the market produces more. More firms, each one smaller.
Fig 11.3 Losses push firms out. Exit shifts market supply from S₁ to S₂, and the price rises from P₁ to P₂. The surviving firms' loss shrinks to zero as the price climbs to the bottom of ATC.
What to notice: at P₁ these firms are above AVC, so in the short run they keep producing (Chapter 10). In the long run, a loss that never ends is a reason to leave.
Worked example Trace the long-run adjustment

Firms in a competitive industry are earning economic profit, and entry is open.

  1. Start on the firm's graph: at Q*, P > ATC, so firms earn economic profit.
  2. Profit attracts entry. On the market graph, supply shifts right.
  3. The market price falls. On the firm's graph, the horizontal P = MR line falls with it.
  4. Entry continues until price reaches the bottom of ATC, where economic profit is zero.
End state: P = MR = MC = min ATC. For losses, run every step in reverse: exit, supply shifts left, price rises.
Misconception"A firm taking losses should exit right away."▸

In the short run the firm owes its fixed costs whether it operates or not, so it keeps producing as long as price covers AVC. Exit is a long-run decision: when the lease runs out or the equipment can be sold, a firm that expects the losses to continue leaves.

That is why exit takes time, and why the price climbs back gradually rather than all at once.

Major concept 3 · Where the flare goes out

Long-Run Equilibrium and Efficiency

Entry continues while firms earn economic profit, and exit continues while they take losses. The movement stops only when economic profit is zero. Every firm is then producing where price equals marginal cost (its profit-maximizing quantity) and where price equals average total cost (zero profit). Both can be true only at the bottom of ATC, where MC crosses it. That gives the long-run equilibrium condition for a perfectly competitive firm: P = MR = MC = min ATC.

Zero economic profit sounds like failure, but it is the normal profit from Chapter 10. The owner covers every cost, including what her time and money could earn elsewhere. She is doing exactly as well as her next-best option: well enough to stay, not well enough to draw in anyone new.

Fig 11.4 Long-run equilibrium in perfect competition. The price line touches the bottom of ATC, exactly where MC crosses it. The firm earns zero economic profit and has no reason to enter or leave.
On the exam: zero economic profit is not shutdown. The firm covers every cost, including normal profit. Shutdown is a short-run decision about price and AVC.

The end point of entry is good news for buyers. Economists don't usually respond to profit from a good idea by saying, "Cap it." They ask, "Can competitors enter?" If entry is open, the first firm's profit summons rivals, supply expands, and the price falls until buyers capture most of the gain. A firm wants its profit to last; consumers want the value to spread. Free entry is how a private discovery becomes a public gain.

Long-run perfect competition also hits two kinds of efficiency at once. Allocative efficiency means P = MC: the value buyers place on one more unit equals the cost of producing it, so no unit worth more than it costs goes unmade, and no unit is made that costs more than it is worth. Productive efficiency means producing at min ATC: each unit is made at the lowest cost per unit the technology allows.

Misconception"Zero economic profit means the owner is barely scraping by."▸

Economic profit subtracts the owner's opportunity cost: the salary and return she could earn elsewhere. Zero economic profit means she earns exactly that. Her accounting profit is positive, her time and money earn a fair return, and she has no reason to leave.

What zero economic profit rules out is an extra reward big enough to draw in copycats.

Quick check In long-run equilibrium, P = ATC. Should firms enter, exit, or stay? ▸
Stay. P = ATC means zero economic profit: no extra profit to attract entry, and no loss to force exit.
How did you do?
Quick check Which efficiency condition is P = MC? Which is producing at minimum ATC? ▸

Major concept 4 · Knocked off the resting point

When Demand or Costs Change

Long-run equilibrium is a resting point, not a permanent address. Tastes change, input prices change, and governments add taxes and fees. Every shock plays out in two acts. In the short run, the number of firms is fixed, so each firm can only slide along its MC curve to a new quantity. In the long run, entry and exit change the number of firms until the price is back at the bottom of ATC, which may be a new bottom.

Before you draw anything, ask one question: did the shock change the price, or did it change the firm's costs? The answer tells you which curve moves on the firm's graph.

A demand shock moves the price line. Suppose protein bars catch on and market demand rises. The market price climbs from P₁ to P₂ along the market supply curve. On the firm's graph, the horizontal price line rises with it, and the firm slides up its MC curve to a larger quantity. Nothing about the firm's costs has changed: same kitchen, same ingredients, same ATC and MC. The profit rectangle appears only because the price moved above ATC.

Then the flare goes up. Entry shifts market supply right until the price is back at the bottom of ATC, and each firm is back at its original quantity. The extra output comes from more firms, not bigger ones. A fall in demand runs the story backward: the price line drops, firms take losses, some exit, and the price climbs back to the bottom of ATC with fewer firms.

Fig 11.5 A demand increase in a constant-cost industry. Demand shifts from D₁ to D₂ and the price rises from P₁ to P₂ (A to B). The firm's cost curves stay put; its price line rises, and it earns a profit. Entry shifts supply from S₁ to S₂ until the price is back at P₁ (C).
What to notice: on the firm's graph, only the price line moved. Demand changes what the firm can sell for, not what it costs to produce.

Connect A and C in Fig 11.5 and you have the industry's long-run supply curve. If new firms can enter with the same costs as the old ones, the industry can grow without its costs rising, so in the long run it will supply any quantity at the same price: the bottom of ATC. Its long-run supply curve is horizontal. Economists call that a constant-cost industry. If growth bids up the price of something the industry needs (land, specialized workers), each new firm costs a little more to run, and long-run supply slopes upward instead: an increasing-cost industry. That is the only way a change in demand reaches the firm's cost curves: not because buyers want more, but because the industry's expansion raises the prices of its inputs.

Misconception"Demand went up, so the firm's costs went up."▸

Demand is about buyers; cost curves are about what it takes to produce. When demand rises, the firm's price line moves, and the firm moves along its unchanged MC curve to a larger quantity. Its average cost may be higher at that quantity, but only because it moved along the curve, not because the curve shifted.

The one exception is an increasing-cost industry, where expansion bids up input prices. Even then, the curves shift because input prices changed, not because buyers wanted more.

Worked example How many firms?

A competitive industry has constant costs. Each firm's ATC bottoms out at $12 when it produces 50 units. At a price of $12, buyers want 6,000 units.

  1. Long-run price: P = min ATC = $12.
  2. Each firm produces where its ATC is lowest: 50 units.
  3. Number of firms = market quantity ÷ output per firm = 6,000 ÷ 50 = 120 firms.
120 firms, each making 50 units at $12. If demand rose so that buyers wanted 7,500 at $12, entry would continue until there were 7,500 ÷ 50 = 150 firms, and the price would end up back at $12.

A cost shock moves the cost curves. A cost shock starts on the firm's graph instead. Which curves move depends on which kind of cost changed, and you already have the rule: fixed cost never enters marginal cost.

Variable (per-unit) cost rises
A pricier input, a per-unit tax, a fee on every unit sold. Every unit now costs more, so MC, AVC, and ATC all shift up. Because MC is each firm's supply curve, market supply shifts left right away.
Fixed cost rises
Higher rent, a bigger annual license fee, a lump-sum tax. Total cost rises by the same amount at every quantity, so only ATC shifts up. MC and AVC don't move, and neither does market supply, at first.

Say a tariff raises the cost of steel for a competitive industry of tool makers by $15 per tool. MC shifts up, market supply shifts left, and the price rises, but by less than $15, because buyers cut back as the price climbs. Each firm's ATC is now $15 higher, so firms take losses. In the long run, losses cause exit. Supply keeps shifting left until the price equals the bottom of the new ATC. A per-unit cost lifts ATC straight up without moving its lowest point left or right, so the price ends exactly $15 higher and each surviving firm makes the same quantity as before. The industry shrinks through fewer firms, and in a constant-cost industry buyers end up paying the whole $15.

Now say the city doubles the annual license fee for food trucks. Nothing changes at the margin: the next taco costs what it did, so MC doesn't move and neither does market supply. In the short run, the price and each truck's quantity stay put. Every truck simply earns less: the price is now below its higher ATC, so it takes a loss. It keeps operating, because closing wouldn't refund the fee. In the long run, some trucks exit, supply shifts left, and the price rises to the bottom of the new ATC. That bottom sits higher and farther right, because MC still crosses ATC at its lowest point and MC hasn't moved. Spreading a bigger fixed cost over more tacos is the cheapest way to cover it. The trucks that remain are fewer and busier.

Fig 11.6 Two cost shocks, long-run results. Left: a per-unit cost shifts MC and ATC up by the same amount, so the new long-run price P₂ is higher by exactly that amount, at the same quantity per firm. Right: a fixed-cost increase shifts only ATC. The new bottom of ATC is higher and farther right, so the price rises and each remaining firm produces more. Gray dashed curves show where the firm started.
What to notice: MC always crosses ATC at its lowest point. If MC doesn't move, the new minimum has to slide up along MC, which is why q₂ is larger.
Misconception"A higher license fee makes each firm produce less."▸

A license fee is a fixed cost: the firm pays it whether it sells one taco or a thousand. It doesn't change the cost of the next unit, so MC doesn't move, and in the short run the profit-maximizing quantity doesn't change either.

In the long run the fee pushes some firms out, the price rises, and each survivor produces more, spreading the fee over more units. The industry produces less; each remaining firm produces more.

Worked exampleA cost shock in numbers

Return to the industry above: each firm's ATC bottoms out at $12 at 50 units, and 120 firms supply 6,000 units. A new regulation adds $3 to the cost of every unit. At a price of $15, buyers want 5,400 units.

  1. A per-unit cost shifts ATC straight up. New minimum ATC = $12 + $3 = $15, still at 50 units.
  2. Long-run price = new min ATC = $15.
  3. Number of firms = 5,400 ÷ 50 = 108 firms.
The price rises by the full $3, each firm still makes 50 units, and 12 firms exit. In a constant-cost industry, buyers pay the whole per-unit cost in the long run; the industry shrinks through fewer firms.
Exam move · trace any shock in four steps

1. Name the shock. Did it change the price (demand) or the firm's costs? If costs, per-unit or fixed?

2. Move the right curve. Demand: the market demand curve and the firm's price line. Per-unit cost: MC, AVC, ATC, and market supply. Fixed cost: ATC only.

3. Short run. The number of firms is fixed. Find the new price, set the firm's quantity where P = MC, and shade the profit or loss.

4. Long run. Profit draws entry; loss forces exit. Shift market supply until P = min ATC, then report the price, each firm's quantity, and the number of firms.

Quick check Each firm's ATC is lowest at $8, at 200 units. At $8, buyers want 30,000 units. How many firms are there in long-run equilibrium? ▸
30,000 ÷ 200 = 150 firms, each producing 200 units at $8.

Quick checkA $2 per-unit tax hits a constant-cost industry. In the long run, how much does the price rise?▸

Retrieval practiceCompare a rise in demand with a rise in fixed cost: what moves first on the firm's graph, and where does each industry end up in the long run?▸

A demand increase moves only the price line. The price rises above ATC, the firm slides up its MC curve, and it earns a profit. Entry brings the price back to the same min ATC: same quantity per firm, more firms. A fixed-cost increase moves only ATC. The price and quantity don't change at first, but the firm now takes a loss. Exit raises the price to the new, higher min ATC, where each remaining firm produces more and there are fewer firms. A common slip is shifting the cost curves when demand changes, or shifting MC when a fixed cost changes.

Major concept 5 · Create value or block entry

Two Ways to Keep Profit

If entry erodes profit, a firm that wants its profit to last has two broad options. The first is to keep creating value: lower costs, improve quality, earn trust, solve the next problem before anyone else does. The second is to make entry harder, either by building an advantage rivals can't easily copy or by getting the government to keep them out (Concept 6). To see the difference clearly, start with the businesses most often accused of creating nothing.

The wholesaler, the broker, the ride app, the reseller: they "make nothing," and the popular verdict is that they are parasites skimming a cut between the people who do the real work. "Cut out the middleman" is one of the most common pieces of business advice there is. The economist's reply is that the middleman is often the one creating the most value in the chain. You just can't see it, because what they produce isn't a thing.

The clearest account comes from the Duke economist Michael Munger, who argues that firms like Uber don't really sell rides. They sell reductions in transaction costs. A transaction cost is everything that stands between you and a deal, beyond the price itself, and Munger sorts it into three: triangulation (finding the right partner and agreeing on a price), transfer (actually paying and delivering), and trust (believing the other side will come through). Before Uber, a stranger's car was useless to you: you couldn't find it, couldn't price it, couldn't trust the driver. The app didn't build a car; it manufactured triangulation, transfer, and trust, and that is a real product. To a customer, a dollar of hassle removed is worth as much as a dollar off the price.

Which gives us the honest line. Picture two ways to make money on a river. One person dredges the channel so barges pass faster and cheaper: a real cost falls, value is created, and the fee is earned. Another stretches a chain across the water and charges boats to lower it: nothing moves faster and no cost falls; they have added an obstacle and rented out the key. Same fee collected, opposite economics. The first creates value; the second only extracts it, which economists call rent-seeking . The test for any middleman, any platform's cut, any toll: did a real cost fall because you're here, or did you just hang a chain across the river?

Rules of business success · Rule #5: build the habit, not the hit

One profitable idea will be copied. Become the kind of firm, or person, that keeps finding new value. And look where others won't: when everyone runs away from a hard, unglamorous need, run toward it. The problem others avoid may be the one customers most need solved.

Quick check A wholesaler buys from 200 farms and sells to 50 grocery stores, "making nothing." In transaction-cost terms, what is the wholesaler producing? ▸
Triangulation and transfer at scale. Instead of 200 × 50 separate searches, negotiations, and deliveries, each side deals once with the wholesaler. The cost that falls is all that matching and moving, and that reduction is the product.
How did you do?
Quick check Apply the river test: a ticket platform that instantly matches fans to verified resale seats, versus a scalper who buys up a whole show and resells at a markup. ▸
It depends on whether a real cost fell. Matching fans to verified seats lowers triangulation and trust costs: coordination. Buying up supply only to resell adds no matching or trust; if it is just cornering tickets to charge more, that is closer to the chain across the river. Reasonable people argue the line, which is the point.
How did you do?
Quick check Give one example of renewing value and one of protecting profit. ▸
Renewing value: a firm cuts delivery costs with better logistics. Protecting profit: a firm lobbies for a rule that keeps qualified rivals out.
How did you do?
Retrieval practiceName the two ways a firm can keep profit in the long run, and give the economist's test for a middleman.▸

Renew value (keep lowering costs, improving quality, solving new problems) or make entry harder. For a middleman, ask: did a real cost fall because they're here (triangulation, transfer, or trust), or did they just hang a chain across the river? A common slip is to treat every middleman as a parasite, or every platform's cut as earned.

Major concept 6 · Where the moat came from

Barriers to Entry: Earned, Traded, and Protected

Concept 5 ended with a chain across the river. It's tempting to conclude that anything keeping rivals out is a chain. But some of the most valuable things a firm can build are barriers, and some barriers are deals society makes on purpose. The useful question isn't whether a barrier exists. It's where the barrier came from.

A barrier to entry lets economic profit last because the flare goes up but rivals can't answer it. That sounds like bad news for buyers, and sometimes it is. But barriers come from three very different places, and economists treat them very differently.

Earned barriers. Some barriers are what's left behind by creating value. Walmart spent decades driving down the cost of moving goods (Concept 1), and a newcomer can't match that cost on its first day. A reputation for showing up on time, a brand customers trust, know-how that took years to learn, a network that gets more useful as more people join: each makes entry harder, and each was built by serving customers better than the alternatives. Call it a moat. A moat is the reward for creating value, and the chance to build one is a big part of why firms keep investing in lower costs and better products. Earned moats also stay contestable. A rival who finds a cheaper way or a better product can still cross, as Sears, Blockbuster, and BlackBerry each learned.

Traded barriers. Some barriers are deals that society makes on purpose. A new drug can cost more than a billion dollars to develop and pennies to copy (Concept 1). If rivals could copy it the day it launched, the profit flare would go out at once, and no one would pay to discover the next one. A patent blocks copying for about 20 years: buyers pay higher prices for a while, and in exchange the invention gets made. Copyright does the same for books, songs, and software. A license can be a fair trade too, when buyers can't judge quality until it's too late: surgery, electrical wiring, a bridge. In each case the barrier is the price, and the invention or the safety is what society buys with it.

Protected barriers. Some barriers buy nothing for buyers. A cap on the number of taxi licenses, a three-year permit wait, a tariff, or a subsidy only incumbents get keeps rivals out without making the product cheaper, better, or safer. That is the chain across the river, stretched across a whole market.

Earned
Examples: scale and low cost, a trusted brand, know-how, a network everyone uses.
Buyers get: the value that built it.
Policy: leave it alone and let rivals try to beat it.
Traded
Examples: patents, copyright, licenses where buyers can't judge safety.
Buyers get: inventions that otherwise wouldn't exist, or safety they can't check.
Policy: keep the deal honest: no longer or stricter than it needs to be.
Protected
Examples: license caps, needless permits, tariffs, subsidies only insiders get.
Buyers get: higher prices and fewer choices.
Policy: break the blockage.

So the economist's question is never "barrier or no barrier?" It's "where did this barrier come from?" If customers built it by choosing a firm again and again, let rivals try to beat it. If it's a trade, ask whether society is getting what it pays for: does the patent last longer than the research needs, and does the license demand more than safety requires? If it mainly protects insiders, remove it.

That points to the policy question. A contestable market is one that rivals can enter and challenge, and even the threat of entry keeps incumbents honest. So when profit persists, don't ask only "why are these firms greedy?" Ask "why hasn't entry happened?" Sometimes the industry is genuinely hard. Sometimes entry is blocked by law, paperwork, or privilege, and then the cure is to remove the block. Trade is one of the most powerful forms of entry: imports are competitors. A tariff (a tax on imports) or a quota (a limit on them) is a barrier to entry by another name. It protects domestic producers' profits from competition and makes buyers pay more. Trade gets its own chapter later in the course.

Fig 11.7 When entry is blocked, the adjustment can't finish. Supply stays at S₁, the price stays at P₁, and the profit rectangle persists. Buyers never get the lower price that entry would have brought.
What to notice: the firm's cost curves are the same as in Fig 11.2. The only difference is the barrier.
Policy rule · attack protected profit, not profit

If high profit comes from lower cost, better quality, or a newly discovered need, let it summon competitors. If high profit survives because entry is blocked, break the blockage: needless permits, exclusive privileges, incumbent subsidies, and trade barriers. If it comes from a deliberate trade, like a patent, keep the deal honest: long enough to pay for the invention, and no longer. The goal is not to punish value creation. It is to keep markets open enough that value spreads.

Misconception"Barriers to entry are always bad for buyers."▸

A barrier hurts buyers when it buys them nothing. A low-cost supply chain, a reputation for honesty, and a patent that pays for the next drug are barriers too, and buyers would be worse off without them.

Remove every barrier and you'd remove patents, reputations worth building, and much of the reason to invest in a cheaper way of doing things. Ask where the barrier came from, not just whether it exists.

Quick checkSort these barriers as earned, traded, or protected: a patent on a new insulin pen, a state license required to arrange flowers, Costco's buying power.▸
Traded: the patent buys the invention for a limited time. Protected: arranging flowers poses no real safety risk, so the license mainly keeps rivals out. Earned: Costco's low costs come from serving customers at enormous scale.
How did you do?
Quick checkA town's only hardware store has earned a profit for 30 years. No law protects it. Is that a problem for policy?▸
Probably not. Nothing stops a rival from opening. The store's reputation, know-how, and convenience are an earned moat. If its prices climbed high enough, the profit would draw a competitor. Policy steps in when the law keeps rivals out.
How did you do?
Quick checkWhy can a 20-year patent be a good deal for buyers, even though it raises prices?▸
Without it, rivals could copy a costly invention right away, the profit would vanish, and the invention might never be made. Buyers pay more for a while in exchange for a product that otherwise wouldn't exist.
How did you do?
Quick check Why can imports act like entry? ▸
Foreign producers compete with domestic firms, add to supply, and push the price down, limiting how much excess profit domestic firms can keep. Tariffs and quotas weaken that pressure.
How did you do?
Retrieval practiceName the three sources of barriers to entry, give an example of each, and say what an economist would do about each one.▸

On the exam

The moves you can now make

Five questions at exam level: profit to entry, loss to exit, how many firms, a cost shock, and blocked entry. Try each on paper first, then reveal.

Type A · long-run adjustment from profit

A perfectly competitive firm is earning economic profit. Explain what happens in the long run, using both the market graph and the firm's graph.

Show model answer ▸

Profit attracts entry. On the market graph, supply shifts right and the price falls. On the firm's graph, the horizontal P = MR line falls with the market price, and the firm moves down its MC curve to a smaller quantity. Entry continues until price equals the bottom of ATC. In long-run equilibrium, P = MR = MC = min ATC, and firms earn zero economic profit.

Compare with your paper. How did you do?

Type B · long-run adjustment from loss

A perfectly competitive firm is suffering economic losses but covering its variable costs. What does it do in the short run, and what happens in the long run?

Show model answer ▸

In the short run it keeps producing, because price is above AVC (Chapter 10). In the long run, losses cause exit. On the market graph, supply shifts left and the price rises. On the firm's graph, the P = MR line rises. Exit continues until the remaining firms earn zero economic profit, where P = min ATC.

Compare with your paper. How did you do?

Type C · number of firms

A constant-cost competitive industry is in long-run equilibrium. Each firm's ATC reaches its minimum of $20 at 400 units. At $20, buyers want 48,000 units. Find the price, each firm's output, and the number of firms. Then demand rises so that buyers want 60,000 units at $20. What happens in the long run?

Show worked solution ▸
Worked example Price, output, and number of firms
  1. Long-run price = min ATC = $20.
  2. Each firm produces where ATC is lowest: 400 units.
  3. Firms = 48,000 ÷ 400 = 120.
  4. After the demand increase, the price rises briefly and firms earn profit, which draws in entry. Supply expands until the price is back at $20: 60,000 ÷ 400 = 150 firms.
In a constant-cost industry, demand changes the number of firms in the long run, not the price.
Compare with your paper. How did you do?

Type D · a cost shock

A perfectly competitive, constant-cost industry is in long-run equilibrium. The city raises the annual license fee that every firm must pay. Explain what happens in the short run to the price, each firm's output, and each firm's profit, and in the long run to the price, each firm's output, and the number of firms. How would your answer change if the city instead charged a fee on every unit sold?

Show model answer▸

The license fee is a fixed cost, so only ATC shifts up; MC and the market supply curve don't move. In the short run, the price and each firm's quantity stay the same, but the price is now below ATC, so firms take losses. They keep producing, because the price still covers AVC.

In the long run, losses cause exit. Market supply shifts left and the price rises until it reaches the bottom of the new ATC. That bottom is higher and farther right along the unchanged MC curve, so the price ends higher, each remaining firm produces more, and there are fewer firms.

A per-unit fee is a variable cost: MC, AVC, and ATC all shift up, and market supply shifts left right away. The price rises by less than the fee in the short run, so firms take losses. In the long run, exit continues until the price has risen by the full fee; each firm produces the same quantity as before, and there are fewer firms.

Compare with your paper. How did you do?

Type E · barriers to entry

A domestic industry earns economic profit year after year. Imports are limited by a quota, and new domestic firms need permits that take three years to get. Explain why profit persists, and what policy would make the market more contestable.

Show model answer ▸

Profit persists because entry is blocked. The quota limits foreign entry through imports, and the slow permits discourage domestic entry. Without new supply, the price stays above ATC. To make the market contestable, remove or loosen the quota and streamline needless permitting, while keeping rules that solve a real safety or information problem.

AP-style long free response · ~10 points · graphs required · model answer and scoring below

Firms in a perfectly competitive industry are identical. The market price is $40. A typical firm maximizes profit at 300 units, where its average total cost is $53 and its average variable cost is $37. The firm's average total cost reaches its minimum of $51 at 400 units.

  1. Draw correctly labeled side-by-side graphs for the market and for a typical firm. On the firm's graph, show the price, MC, ATC, AVC, and the profit-maximizing quantity. (3 pts)
  2. Calculate the firm's economic profit or loss. (1 pt)
  3. In the short run, should the firm produce or shut down? Explain. (1 pt)
  4. Explain what happens in the long run to the number of firms and to the market price, and show the change on your market graph. (2 pts)
  5. Identify the long-run equilibrium price and the quantity each remaining firm produces. (2 pts)
  6. In long-run equilibrium, what is the firm's economic profit? Explain what it means. (1 pt)
Write a little more first.
Reveal a full-credit response ▸

(a) The market graph shows supply and demand crossing at $40. On the firm's graph, a horizontal line at $40 is the firm's demand, MR, and AR; MC crosses it at 300 units. At 300, ATC ($53) is above the price and AVC ($37) is below it, and MC passes through the bottoms of AVC and ATC.

(b) Loss = (ATC − P) × Q = (53 − 40) × 300 = $3,900.

(c) Produce. The price ($40) is above AVC ($37), so producing covers variable cost and pays part of fixed cost; shutting down would lose all of fixed cost, (53 − 37) × 300 = $4,800.

(d) Losses cause firms to exit. Market supply shifts left, and the market price rises.

(e) The price rises until it equals minimum ATC: $51. Each remaining firm produces 400 units, where MC = min ATC.

(f) Zero economic profit. This is normal profit: the firm covers every cost, including the owner's opportunity cost, so there is no reason to enter or exit.

Market thesis

In free markets, the main ways to get rich are to provide value or to cheat the system. Competition is the consumer's ally: free entry turns one firm's discovery into everyone's lower price, and drives profit toward zero unless something blocks the door.

If economists ruled the world…

we would make markets easier to enter and let competition solve the profit problem. If entry is open, let profit call competitors in. If entry is blocked, break the blockage. And we'd stop calling middlemen parasites: we'd judge them by whether a real cost fell because they showed up.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Economic profit attracts entry; economic losses cause exit.
  • Long-run equilibrium in perfect competition is P = MR = MC = min ATC, with zero economic profit.
  • Free entry pushes firms toward allocative efficiency (P = MC) and productive efficiency (min ATC).
  • LRATC falls with economies of scale and rises with diseconomies of scale.
  • Barriers to entry let profit last. Economists judge each one by where it came from: earned by serving customers, traded for invention or safety, or merely protected.
  • A demand shift moves the firm's price line; a cost shift moves its cost curves. Per-unit costs move MC, AVC, and ATC; fixed costs move only ATC.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Which barriers to entry protect consumers, and which mainly protect incumbents?
  • When is a middleman a coordinator earning its keep, and when is it a gatekeeper charging a toll: the reseller, the platform's cut, the licensing board?
  • When should policy defend a temporary profit as the reward for a discovery, and when should it attack a profit that is protected?
Takeaways
In your life

When everyone runs away, run toward. Not toward danger for its own sake, but toward real needs others avoid because they are difficult, unglamorous, or uncomfortable.

In your work

Build the habit, not the hit. Don't expect one profitable position to stay untouched. Keep learning, improving, and finding the next need to fill.
