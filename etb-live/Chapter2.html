Consumer Choice

Utility and The Marginal Revolution

Chapter 2~ 52 min read

Every day, about a dozen Americans die waiting for a kidney. Tens of thousands more spend years on dialysis. Nearly every single one of them has a friend, a relative, or a stranger across town who could spare a kidney and live a perfectly healthy life—we are born with two, after all, but need only one.

And yet, the kidneys do not come. Why?

Economists believe, as an article of faith, that people respond to incentives. This is a tragedy of a missing market: no signal, no incentive, and no way for two people to make each other better off.

In 1984, a ban was sponsored by a young Tennessee congressman who argued that markets for organs might lead to the degradation and commoditization of the human body, the exploitation of the poor, and the facilitation of abuse and fraud.

That young Tennessee congressman later became Vice President.

His name was Al Gore.

In the United States, buying or selling a kidney is a federal crime. We have decided as a society that bodily organs are too sacred to have a price tag.

Or, at least, some of them.

You cannot sell a kidney, but you can sell your sperm or your eggs. You cannot sell your blood for a transfusion, but you can walk into a clinic this afternoon and sell your blood plasma for cash. In fact, because we allow cash incentives, the United States supplies roughly two-thirds of the world's plasma. Same body. Same needle. Opposite rules.

Our moral boundaries are deeply inconsistent. Economists are split on various kidney market proposals, but they overwhelmingly believe the price system creates good outcomes. If there are things that should carry no price, that means people have no incentive to provide them. By legally mandating that a kidney must cost zero dollars, we haven't actually eliminated the price. We have just changed its form. The price of a kidney isn't financial; it is measured in time, suffering, and human lives.

The ultimate price of a free-but-underproduced good is the infinite price of "unavailable."

The big picture
People respond to incentives — and the master incentive is a price. A price does not measure how much a thing matters in total; it measures what one more unit is worth, given how much we already have. Because each extra unit satisfies a slightly less urgent want, that marginal value falls as we consume more, which is why abundant water is cheap and scarce diamonds dear — and why a kidney, denied any legal price, goes unsupplied while people die in the queue. By the end you should be able to predict how behavior shifts when incentives change, say what utility is (and what it isn't), resolve the diamond–water paradox, compute marginal utility from a total, spend a budget with the equal-marginal-utility-per-dollar rule — and use all of it to see how a society could build a kidney market that saves thousands of lives without exploiting anyone.

Major concept 1

Marginal Thinking

The Diamond-Water Paradox

Next, to a puzzle! Try to solve it. If you can't, don't worry: Adam Smith couldn't either!

This puzzle will help you understand the world, economics, and the solution to the kidney problem. It concerns itself with what makes something valuable, and thereby, what sets prices. When the first set of pioneering economists wanted to figure out value and price, they had a number of competing ideas: maybe it is how useful something is? Maybe it's how rare something is?

But they ran into a problem. Nothing is more useful than water; almost nothing you own matters more to staying alive. Yet water is nearly free. A diamond is useless — you cannot drink it, eat it, or shelter under it — yet it costs a fortune. How can the most valuable thing on earth be cheap, and a useless bauble dear? Smith called it the difference between value in use and value in exchange, and he could describe the gap but not explain it. For nearly a century it sat there, unsolved — the diamond–water paradox.

We assume the price of water should reflect the worth of having any water at all— all of it, the whole life-giving category. But nobody ever buys "all the water." You buy the next glass, having already had your fill today. And the next glass, when you aren't thirsty, is worth almost nothing — a cent, maybe less. That last, barely-wanted glass is what sets the price, because in a world awash in water, the next unit is all that's ever in question. Diamonds run the other way: you own none, so the next one is rare and craved, and its worth — its value at the margin — is enormous.

If diamonds were as common as water, would they have the same price?

The paradox collapses once we separate total usefulness with worth at the margin. Water wins on total usefulness by a mile and loses at the margin, because we already have so much. Diamonds are completely useless, but they are rare at the margin. You would give your life for access to kidneys in general, but you would happily be compensated to give up just one kidney.

Price tracks the margin, not the total — and value, it turns out, lives in the last unit.

This was the insight of the 1870s, and it was so far-reaching it earned the name the marginal revolution. Moving value from the whole to the margin solved Smith's puzzle: people make decisions on the basis of the next unit of consumption.

Quick checkWater is essential and diamonds are useless, yet diamonds cost more. In one phrase, why?▸
Because value is set at the margin, not by total usefulness — water is so abundant that the next glass is barely wanted, while diamonds are rare, so the next one is craved.
Retrieval practiceExplain why "this good is essential, so it should be expensive" is exactly backwards.▸

Essential goods tend to be ones we've arranged to have in abundance precisely because they're essential — and abundance drives marginal value, and therefore price, down. Water is cheap not despite being essential but, in a sense, because society made so much of it available that the next unit is barely wanted. Price is set by the marginal unit — the last, least-urgent one bought — not by the importance of the good in the abstract. The wrong answer clings to "important = valuable = expensive," which is exactly the confusion the marginal revolution dissolved.

Major concept 2

Total, Marginal, and Diminishing Utility

The second slice never thrills like the first.

Utility is the satisfaction, well-being, or value a person gets from a choice — and economists give it a playful unit, the util. A cold soda on a hot day might be worth 10 utils to you; a warm one, 4.

Here's the thing: Utils are bunkum. They're totally made up.

Here is the awkward truth: satisfaction cannot really be measured by utils. There is no happiness thermometer. We can't open your head and read off "27.3 utils." Worse, we can't compare across people at all — my 10 utils and your 10 utils aren't the same thing in any literal sense. This is the measurement problem, and for a while it looked like it might sink the whole idea of economics.

Fortunately, "utils" are a lot like models: imperfect, but useful. Pretending satisfaction is countable makes the logic visible, even if we can't use them to measure.

How can we measure? How can we make that precise enough to compute? By using revealed preferences. I don't know how many utils you got from your soda, but I know that you got more utility per dollar from your soda than from any of the other options on the menu or you would have bought them instead!

Marginal utility also helps us understand why the next unit always seem to make us less happy. Why? Because we satisfy our most urgent wants first. The first glass of water on a hot day is bliss; the second is good; by the fifth you're merely topping off; the eighth you wouldn't drink for free. This is the law of diminishing marginal utility, one of the most dependable patterns in economics: as you consume more of something, the marginal utility of each additional unit falls.

To do calculations with marginal utility, separate two numbers. Total utility is all the satisfaction from everything you've consumed so far. Marginal utility is just the extra from the next one — the change in total utility:

MU = change in TU ÷ change in quantityHere's a schedule for glasses of water on a hot afternoon, in utils.

Worked exampleComputing marginal utility from total utility

Find the marginal utility of the 3rd glass, using the table.

  1. Marginal utility is the change in total utility from one more unit: MU = TU(3) − TU(2).
  2. Read the table: TU at 3 glasses = 24; TU at 2 glasses = 18.
  3. Subtract: 24 − 18 = 6 utils.
The 3rd glass adds 6 utils — less than the 2nd (8) and far less than the 1st (10). That falling sequence, 10 → 8 → 6 → 4 → 2, is diminishing marginal utility. Notice total utility still rises (you're happier with more water) — it just rises by smaller and smaller steps.

Major concept 3

The utility-maximizing rule

Price is the meta-incentive.

Picture a consumer in a delightfully simple world with only two goods to choose between — hot dogs and soda. They want to maximize total utility, and they decide one unit at a time, on the margin, always asking: "what gives me more right now — the next hot dog, or the next soda?" The answer shifts with every purchase, because of diminishing marginal utility: the more hot dogs you've eaten, the less the next one appeals.

A simple two-good model is easy to understand: a consumer will make choices that maximize their marginal utility.

But what of real life? What of an infinite-good universe?

Before we move on, make sure you are fluent in calculating marginal utility from total utility. Get that subtraction fluent and the next step is a breeze; fumble it and it will trip you every time.

Comparing raw utils across goods is like comparing two trips when one distance is in miles and the other in kilometers. It's tricky to model infinite goods, so instead we make every market into a three good model: good x, good y, and price. By using prices we get a common unit. In real life you don't weigh one good in isolation; you walk in with a single wallet and face dozens of competing price tags. A price is the meta-incentive: the universal scale that translates a $4 burrito, a $12 movie ticket, and a $50 pair of shoes into the very same denominator — dollars.

Note that price is an incentive for buyers who want to minimize what they pay, and sellers who want to maximize what they receive in a sale. As Milton Friedman once pointed out, we do not want the price to go up, nor do we want the price to go down. We want the price of things we buy to go down, and the price of things we sell (like our labor) to go up.

To be rational is just to optimize against that scale, stretching a limited budget for the most satisfaction it can buy. You've run this calculation in your head a thousand times; you call it hunting for the best bang for your buck.

So you don't chase the highest raw marginal utility. A trip to space has enormous marginal utility, but it costs a fortune per unit of happiness. You chase the most satisfaction per dollar: divide each good's marginal utility by its price, and spend your next dollar wherever that ratio is highest. Moreover, you might spend money if it's a good deal, but you also might save your money if that brings you more utility!

The utility-maximizing rule

A buyer optimizes by making the same simple choice over and over again, choosing which bundle will maximize marginal utility per dollar:

MU₁/P₁ = MU₂/P₂

If one good delivers more utility per dollar than another, keep shifting money toward it until the ratios meet.

As you spend money, notice something interesting: decreasing marginal utility sets in, and the amount of marginal utility per dollar drops. You continue consuming until the marginal utility drops below that of another consumption choice, and you purchase that. As you continue to make consumption decisions, your marginal utility per dollar will begin to equalize.

That equalizing is the quiet signature of diminishing marginal utility, and you feel it far beyond snack stands. At a buffet you don't load your plate with only the single best dish; after a few bites its marginal utility drops below the next dish, so you spread across the table until each forkful is about equally worth taking. Studying for five classes, you don't pour every hour into one; as the marginal payoff of the next hour falls, you shift to another, until the last hour in each subject gives roughly the same boost. The optimum always has the same shape — move money or effort from wherever it is worth less to wherever it is worth more, and you arrive, inevitably, with the margins balanced.

Diminishing marginal utility also says something quietly human: we crave variety. Love hot dogs all you like — by the fourth, its marginal utility has fallen so far that a cold soda, or almost anything else, easily beats a fifth. That is why almost no one pours an entire budget into a single good: each good argues itself out of your wallet as you pile it up, handing the next dollar to something else. It is also the first hint of why certain goods travel in pairs — the hot dog that makes you reach for a soda — a thread we will pick up later on when we discuss complementary goods.

Worked exampleFinding the utility-maximizing bundle

Now put prices on the very same hot dogs and soda from the table above: a hot dog costs $3, a soda costs $1, and you have $15. Find the utility-maximizing bundle.

  1. Turn marginal utility into bang-per-buck. Hot dog MUs (from the table) are 30, 24, 18, 12; at $3 each, MU/$ = 10, 8, 6, 4. Soda MUs are 8, 6, 4, 2; at $1 each, MU/$ = 8, 6, 4, 2.
  2. Spend in order of highest MU/$: 1st hot dog (10); then 2nd hot dog (8) and 1st soda (8); then 3rd hot dog (6) and 2nd soda (6); then 4th hot dog (4) and 3rd soda (4).
  3. Track the budget: 4 hot dogs = $12, 3 sodas = $3 → exactly $15. Stop; the budget is spent.
  4. Check the rule: 4th hot dog MU/$ = 12/3 = 4; 3rd soda MU/$ = 4/1 = 4. Equal. ✓
Buy 4 hot dogs and 3 sodas. Notice the lesson the prices just taught: by raw marginal utility the first hot dog (30) towered over the first soda (8), but once the cheap soda is priced in, their bang-per-buck runs nearly neck and neck — and the last dollar on each delivers the same 4 utils. Every dollar is used, and no reshuffle could raise total utility. That is the maximum.

Retrieval practiceWhy does the rule divide marginal utility by price, instead of just buying whatever has the highest marginal utility?▸

Because you're spending limited money, not limited units, so the right question is "what do I get per dollar?" — not "what do I get per item?" A diamond might have enormous marginal utility, but at thousands of dollars each dollar buys very little satisfaction; a cheaper good with modest marginal utility can deliver more utility per dollar. Dividing MU by price puts every good on the same footing — utils per dollar — so a $3 hot dog and a $1 soda compare fairly. And this rule quietly produces the demand curve: when a good's price falls, its MU/$ jumps up, so you buy more until diminishing marginal utility brings MU/$ back into balance — which is just "lower price → larger quantity demanded," the law of demand the next chapter starts from.

When prices and income change

Now that we've solved one bundle, at one set of prices, under a single budget constraint (four hot dogs and three sodas at $15), it's time to get a little more like real life. See, the interesting questions are about change — what happens to that bundle when some variable moves, say price or income? We call these kinds of questions comparative statics: how does your outcome change when a variable changes? This is where the marginal apparatus starts to earn its keep.

Changes in price. Suppose the stand raises the price of a hot dog from $3 to $4; soda and your $15 budget are unchanged. Every hot dog's marginal utility per dollar now falls — you are dividing the same MU by a bigger number — so the old bundle is no longer balanced. Its first four MU/$ figures drop to 7.5, 6, 4.5, 3, and that last 3 is now beaten by a soda. Re-solve and you land on three hot dogs and three sodas (3 × $4 + 3 × $1 = $15). The price went up; the number of hot dogs you buy went down, from four to three.

That is not a coincidence — it is the whole point. Price is the denominator of MU/P, so when a good's price rises, its MU/P falls below the balanced level and you buy less; when a good's price falls, its MU/P rises above the balanced level and you buy more, until diminishing marginal utility pulls MU/P back into balance.

Let's state it simply: when price rises, you'll buy less. When price goes down, you'll buy more. And, importantly, when the price of the goods you purchase goes down, you'll get more utility. Remember this, because we're going to come back to it.

The same logic covers a free good. If a good's price is zero, there is no denominator to hold you back: the rule says keep consuming until its marginal utility itself falls to zero — you stop only when one more unit would start to make you worse off.

Foreshadowing the demand curve. Run the same experiment at a few prices and read off how many hot dogs the rule selects: at $3 you buy 4, at $4 you buy 3, at $5 you buy 2. Plot price against quantity and you have drawn a downward-sloping curve of how much you would be willing to buy at a variety of prices. Cool! This is a very simplified version of a demand curve, which we'll discuss in the next chapter. All we had to do to get it was assume a rational shopper maximizing utility under a budget. Lower price → larger quantity demanded is a pretty important conclusion, and we'll come back to it soon!

Changes in Income. Now hold prices where they started ($3 and $1) but raise the budget from $15 to $19. Nothing about the rule changes — with prices fixed, the balanced MU/P you are aiming for is the same — you simply have more to spend. Re-solve and you buy more of both: five hot dogs and four sodas (5 × $3 + 4 × $1 = $19). More income, spent by the same rule, buys a bigger bundle — no new machinery required.

We now have the vocabulary to name what just happened. A normal good is one you buy more of when you get more money. Most goods function this way, and that is why they are called normal — our hot dogs and sodas both rose with the bigger budget, so for this shopper both are normal goods. Some goods have the opposite relationship: you buy fewer of them as your income increases. These are inferior goods, and the name is about the relationship with income, not the quality of the good. Bus tickets are the classic case: when money is tight you ride the bus, but as your income climbs you buy a car and ride less — so your demand for bus tickets falls even though nothing about the bus itself changed.

Major concept 4

Price as the Meta-Incentive

How to build a market for kidneys

Now that you understand how marginal utility works, you can make a more informed decision about kidney markets.

Hold two people side by side. A healthy donor has two kidneys and needs one; the marginal utility of their second kidney is low — its job is mostly redundant, and they will live a normal life without it. A patient in renal failure has zero working kidneys; the marginal utility of a single kidney to them is almost beyond measure — it is the difference between dialysis-bound and free, between dying and living. Diminishing marginal utility says these two numbers are wildly far apart. Whenever marginal values sit that far apart, a trade can make both people better off. The kidney ban doesn't prevent a bad trade; it forbids an extraordinary one.

That tension is exactly why the "protect the vulnerable" argument is so hard: it cuts both ways. A market might pressure a poor donor— but the ban abandons a poor patient, since lower-income people are disproportionately the ones who languish on dialysis and die waiting. Moreover, poor donors may be the ones who stand to gain the most from giving up a kidney in monetary terms, because marginal utility of money is higher for the poor. Whichever way we choose, some vulnerable person bears the cost. The honest question isn't "markets or dignity?" It's "which of these people do we leave to pay the price?"The bipartisan End Kidney Deaths Act, introduced in Congress in 2025, would give non-directed kidney donors a refundable tax credit of $10,000 a year for five years — $50,000 total — and it's engineered to answer the objections one by one. The kidney goes to the next patient on the waiting list, so the rich can't outbid the poor. The reward is spread over five years, so no one in crisis can sell a kidney for fast cash on a bad day. Donors still pass the same rigorous medical and psychological screening. And the credit is written so it doesn't legally count as "buying" an organ. At the time of the writing of this book, this act has not become law. Reasonable people still disagree about it — but it is a concrete demonstration that the shortage is a solvable problem, not a tragic fact of nature.

Economists are famously utilitarian: we judge a policy by the good and harm it actually produces, not by how it looks. That makes us a little disagreeable. When the socially comfortable answer — "some things simply shouldn't be for sale" — quietly produces a waiting list of suffering people, the economist is the one who says so out loud, siding with the outcome that saves lives over the one that merely feels proper.

Economists split on exactly how to fix kidney shortages, but they are united in the conviction that people would supply more kidneys if the incentives were friendlier to it — and you'll watch that disagreeable streak play out in full by the end of the chapter.

The economist Alex Tabarrok has said a price is "a signal wrapped in an incentive." Forbid the price and you switch off the reward to supply — so the supply, predictably, does not come, and people die in a shortage we are ultimately responsible for.

Worked examplePredicting the response to an incentive

A rideshare app raises fares automatically when a storm spikes demand — "surge pricing." Predict what happens to the number of drivers who log on, and name the principle.

  1. Identify the incentive that changed: the price (and so the reward for driving) just went up.
  2. Ask who faces it and which way it pushes them: for drivers, a higher fare is a larger reward to be on the road right now.
  3. Predict the behavior: more drivers log on — quantity supplied rises — even though the weather is worse.
More drivers appear, because people respond to incentives and a higher price is a stronger reward to supply. The same higher price pushes the other way on riders, who take fewer trips — one price, two opposite nudges. Switch the price off (cap the fare) and the extra drivers stay home: no reward, no response. That is the kidney, in miniature.
Common misconception"Economics assumes people are greedy and selfish."▸

It assumes no such thing. The working assumption is responsiveness, not selfishness: behavior shifts when incentives shift. Those incentives are routinely moral and social — reputation, duty, love, the wish to help — which is why people donate blood for a sticker and a cookie. "People respond to incentives" is a claim about how people change, not about what they care about. An economist who forgets this and models everyone as a coin-counting cynic will mispredict the world, badly.

Quick checkA city adds a 25-cent deposit to every soda can, refundable on return. Predict the effect of the new $0.25 payment on how many cans get returned. Why does this work?▸
Returns rise: the refund is a reward for bringing cans back, and people respond to incentives. The deposit changed the cost-benefit of tossing a can versus returning it.
Quick checkWhy is "people respond to incentives" not the same as "people are selfish"?▸
Because incentives include moral and social rewards, not just money. Responsiveness describes how behavior changes when rewards change; it says nothing about whether the rewards people value are selfish or generous.
Retrieval practiceIn what sense is a price an incentive, and what does that predict when a price is forbidden?▸
A price rewards suppliers for providing a good and makes buyers weigh what they give up — it is the master incentive. Forbid it (force the price to zero) and the reward to supply disappears, so the good is under-supplied. That is precisely the kidney shortage.

Notice the sleight of hand the word "donor" performs. "Giving up on having kidneys" sounds like a catastrophe — and it would be. But that's not the trade. The trade is "willing to give up a single, spare kidney" — a much smaller sacrifice, with the donor's own health carefully protected.

The entire reason why kidney markets could work is because there is a difference in value for the marginal kidney!

Agreement and Disagreement

None of this is to say that the objections to kidney markets are unimportant.

Where economists agree — and where they don't

Almost universal agreement (the positive claim): people respond to incentives; the shortage is real and lethal; and reducing the disincentives to donate would save lives. Virtually no economist disputes this part.

Real disagreement (the normative and design questions):

• Exploitation of the poor. A cash market might pressure the desperate into selling a body part they'd keep if they had better options — consent under financial duress isn't fully free.

• Crowding out altruism. The sociologist Richard Titmuss argued that paying for blood can drive away the people who gave freely — a price can corrode the very generosity it tries to recruit.

• Repugnance. Nobel laureate Alvin Roth named the repugnant market: some trades a society refuses on moral grounds even when they'd clear a shortage, and that refusal is a real value, not mere squeamishness.

• Commodification and equity. Would a market let the rich buy organs while the poor wait — turning a human body into inventory?

These aren't strawmen, and listing them is the point: the disagreement among economists is about how to solve the problem ethically, not whether people respond to incentives.

Here's the part skeptics miss: those objections are not dead ends. They're design constraints — and people have already designed around them.

Roth himself built an elegant answer. Rather than cash, he engineered a kidney exchange: when a willing donor isn't a medical match for their intended patient, a clearinghouse links them into chains — your donor gives to a stranger, that stranger's donor gives to you — so incompatible pairs become compatible. No money changes hands, repugnance is avoided, and thousands of transplants have happened that otherwise couldn't. It's a market in matches, not in organs, and it helped earn Roth the 2012 Nobel Prize. The objection produced the solution.

The United States has also quietly built a clever middle path — not payment, but the removal of disincentives. It is currently illegal to pay for an organ, yet a federal program (the National Living Donor Assistance Center) reimburses a living donor's travel, lost wages, and dependent-care costs, and a private program (the National Kidney Registry's Donor Shield) covers up to roughly $30,000 in lost wages and expenses. The careful framing is that these funds are not a reward for donating — only a guarantee that donating won't leave you worse off financially. It's genuinely cool: it makes a heroic act cost-neutral without ever putting a price on the kidney itself.

But — and this is where care matters — that middle path stops exactly short of the people who could most benefit from going further. It's worth resisting the comfortable thought that the middle path is "close enough." While we sell plasma, blood banks experience persistent shortages. Not-paying has not "solved" blood.

Reimbursing costs makes donation free; it doesn't make it worth it for someone whose six weeks of recovery is a financial catastrophe even after wages are covered. There are good, generous people of modest means who would gladly give a kidney to a stranger if doing so also lifted their own family — and right now the law tells them that the one thing they cannot receive is the very thing that would let them say yes. We protect them from being exploited by a market; we do not protect them from the quieter loss of a trade that would have helped them and saved a stranger's life.

Case studyIran's legal kidney market

Since 1988, Iran has been the only country where a living person can legally be paid for a kidney. The price is partly set by committee: the government pays each donor a fixed amount, the recipient usually adds a negotiated sum on top, and a nonprofit patients' association arranges the match.

The result. Iran reported that by 1999 its waiting list for kidney transplants had essentially disappeared. In the United States, where paying donors is illegal, thousands of people die each year waiting for a kidney.

What did the work. Not a perfect price. The official number is crude, set once for everyone. What mattered was the incentive: paying donors something brought forth a supply that altruism alone never had.

The critiques. Donors are overwhelmingly poor, and some sell out of desperation. Follow-up surveys of donors are mixed: some report high satisfaction, others regret. And skeptics note that the "empty" waiting list leaves out patients who were never diagnosed or referred in the first place.

The takeaway. A price does two jobs: it carries an incentive, and it carries information. Iran's administered price captured much of the first and less of the second. Whether a kidney should be sold at all is a moral question; economics can inform that choice but can't settle it.

EvidenceThe state of play

The shortage. Around 90,000 Americans wait for a kidney, facing multi-year waits, and roughly a dozen die each day on the transplant list; a 1984 federal law (NOTA) forbids paying donors. organdonor.gov.

The current middle path. A federally funded program reimburses living donors' travel, lost wages, and dependent-care costs — framed explicitly as removing disincentives, not paying for the organ. NLDAC.

The live proposal. The bipartisan End Kidney Deaths Act (H.R. 2687, 2025) would grant non-directed donors a $50,000 refundable tax credit over five years, routed so the kidney goes to the longest-waiting patient. congress.gov.

Common misconception"Compensating donors means the rich will buy up the kidneys."▸

That's a risk of one specific design — an open cash auction — not of compensation as such. The proposals actually on the table route every donated kidney to the next patient on the shared waiting list, exactly as today; the compensation rewards thedonorfor supplying an organ to the pool, not a buyer for jumping the queue. You can reward supply without auctioning off priority. Conflating the two makes a solvable design question sound like an inevitable moral catastrophe.

Quick checkUse marginal utility to explain why a kidney trade can make both the donor and the patient better off.▸
The donor's second kidney has low marginal utility (they live normally without it); the patient's first working kidney has enormous marginal utility (life vs. death). Because the marginal values are so far apart, moving the kidney from low-value to high-value use creates a huge gain — both sides come out ahead.
Quick checkName one serious objection to a paid kidney market and one design feature that answers it.▸
Objection: the rich could outbid the poor for organs. Answer: route every donated kidney to the next patient on the shared list (as the End Kidney Deaths Act does), so compensation rewards donors without auctioning priority. (Other valid pairs: rash decisions under duress → spread payment over five years; repugnance of selling organs → Roth's no-cash kidney exchange.)
Retrieval practiceEconomists "agree on the diagnosis but differ on the cure." Explain what that means for kidneys.▸

The diagnosis is the positive claim nearly all economists share: people respond to incentives, so forbidding any reward for donating chokes off supply and produces a lethal shortage. The cure is the normative-and-design question they argue about: cash payment, a tax credit, kidney exchange, or just removing disincentives — and how to prevent exploitation, coercion, and commodification along the way. The disagreement is real and worth taking seriously, but it's a debate about how to end the shortage, not about whether incentives would. That distinction is the whole point: "we can't agree on the perfect fix" is not a reason to accept thousands of preventable deaths as if they were unavoidable.

On the exam

The moves you can now make

Four moves this chapter buys you: compute marginal utility from a total, spend a budget so the last dollar on each good buys equal satisfaction, read the total/marginal-utility relationship off a graph, and predict how behavior responds when an incentive changes. Try each cold, then reveal.

Type A · compute marginal utility

A diner's total utility from slices of pizza runs 0, 15, 27, 36, 42 for 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 slices. Find the marginal utility of the 3rd slice, and say whether marginal utility is diminishing.

Show worked solution▸
  1. MU of the 3rd slice = TU(3) − TU(2) = 36 − 27 = 9 utils.
  2. Compute the others the same way: MU = 15, 12, 9, 6.
MU of the 3rd slice = 9 utils. The sequence 15 → 12 → 9 → 6 falls with each slice, so yes — marginal utility is diminishing, even though total utility is still rising.

Type B · utility maximization

You have $15. Pizza slices cost $3 (MU: 24, 18, 12, 6); sodas cost $2 (MU: 16, 12, 8, 4). Find the utility-maximizing bundle.

Show worked solution▸
  1. Bang-per-buck. Pizza MU/$ = 8, 6, 4, 2. Soda MU/$ = 8, 6, 4, 2.
  2. Buy in descending MU/$: pizza & soda at 8, then both at 6, then both at 4.
  3. Track the budget: 3 pizzas ($9) + 3 sodas ($6) = $15. Stop — budget spent.
  4. Check the rule: 3rd pizza MU/$ = 12/3 = 4; 3rd soda MU/$ = 8/2 = 4. Equal. ✓
Buy 3 pizza slices and 3 sodas. The last dollar on each buys 4 utils, and the whole $15 is used — the utility-maximizing condition MU₁/P₁ = MU₂/P₂ holds.

Type C · read the graph

The total-utility curve for a good is shown. Over what range of quantity is marginal utility positive? At what quantity is marginal utility zero? And what is marginal utility doing past that point?

Try firstMarginal utility is the slope of total utility — how steeply it rises. Watch what the slope does as you move right.
Show worked solution▸

Marginal utility is the slope of the total-utility curve. While TU is rising— from quantity 1 up to the peak at 5 — marginal utility is positive (each unit still adds satisfaction). It's also diminishing the whole way: the curve rises but flattens, so the slope shrinks toward zero.

At the peak (Q = 5), the curve is momentarily flat — slope zero — so marginal utility is zero. Past it, total utility falls, so marginal utility is negative: extra units now subtract satisfaction (the ninth glass of water you'd pay to avoid).

Type D · predict the response to an incentive

A blood bank that has always relied on unpaid volunteers begins paying $50 a donation. (i) Using "people respond to incentives," give the first-order prediction for the quantity of blood donated. (ii) Then give one reason the net effect might be smaller — or even negative.

Show model answer▸

(i) A payment is a new reward to donate, so the first-order prediction is that the quantity of blood donated rises: people respond to incentives, and a $50 reward draws in donors who wouldn't have come for free.

(ii) But incentives can interact. Paying can crowd out altruistic donors — turning a noble gift into a cheap transaction they no longer want part of — so some volunteers stop. If that crowding-out is large, the paid donors may not fully replace the lost volunteers, and total donation could rise little or even fall. The lesson: the simple "more reward → more supply" prediction is usually right, but social and moral incentives can pull the other way, and a careful answer names both.

Capstone · free response

Put the moves together

AP-style short free response · 7 points · model answer, rubric, and pitfalls below

A consumer has $24 to spend on burgers and shakes. Burgers cost $4 each; shakes cost $2 each. The marginal utility (in utils) of each successive unit is: burgers 40, 32, 24, 16, 8; shakes 18, 16, 12, 8, 4.

  1. Calculate the marginal utility per dollar of the first three burgers and the first three shakes. (2 pts)
  2. Determine the utility-maximizing combination of burgers and shakes. Show that it uses the entire budget and satisfies the utility-maximizing condition. (3 pts)
  3. The price of shakes rises to $4. Explain, using marginal utility per dollar, what happens to the quantity of shakes the consumer buys — and name the law this illustrates. (2 pts)
Reveal a full-credit response▸

(a)Divide each marginal utility by the good's price. Burgers ($4): 40/4 = 10, 32/4 = 8, 24/4 = 6. Shakes ($2): 18/2 = 9, 16/2 = 8, 12/2 = 6.

(b)Spend in order of highest marginal utility per dollar: 1st burger (10), 1st shake (9), then 2nd burger and 2nd shake (both 8), then 3rd burger and 3rd shake (both 6), then 4th burger and 4th shake (both 4). That is 4 burgers and 4 shakes: 4 × $4 + 4 × $2 = $16 + $8 = $24, the whole budget. The condition holds — the 4th burger gives 16/4 = 4 utils per dollar and the 4th shake gives 8/2 = 4, so MUburger/Pburger= MUshake/Pshake

(c)At $4, each shake's marginal utility per dollar is cut in half (e.g., the first shake falls from 18/2 = 9 to 18/4 = 4.5). Shakes now deliver less satisfaction per dollar than before, so the consumer shifts spending toward burgers and buysfewershakes. A higher price leading to a smaller quantity bought is the law of demand — here derived straight from marginal utility per dollar.

Reveal the scoring rubric▸
  • 1 pt(a) Burger MU/$ correct (10, 8, 6), with division shown.
  • 1 pt(a) Shake MU/$ correct (9, 8, 6), with division shown.
  • 1 pt(b) Identifies the bundle: 4 burgers and 4 shakes.
  • 1 pt(b) Shows the budget is fully used ($16 + $8 = $24).
  • 1 pt(b) Shows the equal-MU-per-dollar condition holds at the bundle (4 = 4).
  • 1 pt(c) States quantity of shakes demanded falls, because their MU per dollar drops.
  • 1 pt(c) Names the law of demand.

Seven independent points, scored the way a real reader scores them — each earned on its own.

Reveal what loses points▸
  • ConceptualMaximizing by highest marginal utility instead of MU per dollar— that would send all the money to burgers (MU 40) and ignore that shakes are cheaper. The budget makes per-dollar the only fair comparison.
  • ConceptualPicking a bundle where MU/$ is equal but the budget isn't fully spent (or is overspent). Both conditions must hold at once — equal bang-per-buck and all $24 used.
  • PrecisionIn (c), saying "demand falls." The good's own price rose, so it's the quantity demanded that falls, not demand itself — a distinction the next chapter makes precise.
  • Exam craftStating the bundle in (b) with no work — "Calculate" and "show" require the MU/$ comparison and the budget arithmetic on the page.
Market thesis

A price doesn't measure how much a thing matters — it measures what one more unit is worth. Forbid the price, and sellers will not supply. The kidney will not come.

If economists ruled the world…

they wouldn't all agree on kidney markets, but they would insist that compensation for donation would increase the number of kidneys donated. They would also insist on measuring the outcomes of all the different policy options, including the "do nothing" options, whose price is paid in lives.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • People respond to incentives; a price is the master incentive, rewarding supply and rationing demand at once.
  • Value lives at the margin: marginal utility falls as you consume more, and price tracks the marginal unit, not total usefulness.
  • The kidney shortage is real and lethal, and reducing the disincentives to donate would save lives — that part is nearly unanimous.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Cash, a tax credit, or a no-money kidney exchange — which way of rewarding donors fits our values?
  • Does paying corrode altruism (Titmuss), and is that a price worth paying to end a lethal shortage?
  • Who do we protect — the poor donor a market might pressure, or the poor patient the ban leaves to die?
Takeaways
In your life

When a choice isn't worth it — the third dessert, the second ticket, the last minute of scrolling — that's diminishing marginal utility, not regret. Let it tell you when to stop.

In your work

Want a behavior to change? Change the incentive that sits at the margin of the choice. Incentivize what you desire to see more of.
