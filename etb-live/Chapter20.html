Unit Capstone: The Economics of Healthcare

Where every failure comes due

Chapter 20~ 54 min read

It is 2 a.m. and your chest hurts. You call an ambulance. You do not ask three companies for quotes. You do not compare the price of this hospital against the one across town. You do not wait for a sale. You cannot walk away, you cannot shop, and you do not know what you need — the people treating you do. And when the bill comes, it goes to an insurer, so the price you face at the moment of the decision is almost nothing.

Stop and count what just happened. The buyer cannot escape the price. The buyer cannot judge the product. The buyer does not pay the price directly. The seller knows far more than the buyer. And if you have an infection, your illness — and your treatment — affect other people too.

Every single thing this unit warned about is in that one ambulance ride. This is the capstone, so the question is the hard one: why does the market we most need to work behave least like a market — and what, if anything, fixes it?

The big picture
Healthcare breaks nearly every condition a working market needs, and it breaks them all at once, on the same person: demand you cannot escape, a resource that depletes (antibiotics), costs that spill onto third parties (contagion), benefits that go unbought (vaccines), and information so lopsided that the patient often cannot judge what they are buying. No single failure explains it, so no single fix resolves it — which is why every country builds a different patchwork and none has a settled answer. This chapter adds the unit's last tool, asymmetric information, follows the money to the third party who pays, and then watches the whole world place different bets.

Major concept 1

Asymmetric Information

Markets do not need perfect knowledge, but they need enough for prices to mean something.

Asymmetric information exists whenever one side of a trade knows materially more than the other. A used-car seller knows the car's history; the buyer sees polished paint and guesses. An insurance buyer knows their own health and habits better than the insurer. A patient knows their symptoms, but the doctor knows what they mean — and what the treatment is worth.

This is the unit's last failure, and it has a special home. In 1963, the economist Kenneth Arrow argued that medicine is unlike ordinary markets precisely because of uncertainty and unequal knowledge: the patient cannot evaluate the product, often cannot judge whether treatment worked, and must trust an expert whose interests may differ from theirs. Health economics begins there. So we learn the tool where it was born.

Asymmetric information comes in two shapes, and the whole topic turns on telling them apart. One is hidden type — a fact about who you are that exists before the deal (how risky a driver, how sick a patient, how good a car). The other is hidden action — a choice you make after the deal that the other side cannot fully watch. Before and after. Type and action. Get that distinction clean and the rest of the chapter follows.

Quick checkA patient cannot tell whether a recommended test is necessary or just profitable for the clinic. Which side holds the information?▸
The provider. This is asymmetric information, and because the provider also acts on the patient's behalf, it is also a principal-agent problem.
Major concept 2

Adverse Selection and the Death Spiral

Hidden type before the trade can make a market unravel from the healthy end.

Adverse selection is the hidden-type problem. The classic illustration is the used-car market. Sellers know whether their car is a gem or a lemon; buyers do not. So buyers offer an average price. That price is too low for owners of genuinely good cars, who walk away — leaving more lemons, which lowers the average, which lowers offers again. Good cars get driven out of the market by the bad. The buyer's inability to tell them apart, not any lack of desire, is what breaks it.

Health insurance has the same skeleton, and it is the AP-favorite case. An insurer cannot fully see who is healthy and who is sick, so it charges a premium based on the average. To a healthy person, that average looks like a bad deal, so some drop out. With the healthy gone, the remaining pool is sicker, so average cost rises, so the premium rises, so the next-healthiest tier drops out — and around it goes. Economists call the result a death spiral.

Fig 20.1The insurance death spiral. Because the insurer cannot see who is healthy, every turn of the cycle prices out the next-healthiest group, and the pool collapses toward only the sickest.
Worked exampleWhy the healthy walk away

A pool is half low-risk (expected cost $1,000) and half high-risk (expected cost $5,000).

  1. If the insurer cannot tell them apart, the fair average premium is (½ × $1,000) + (½ × $5,000) = $3,000.
  2. To a low-risk person who expects $1,000 of care, paying $3,000 looks like a bad bet, so many low-risk people decline.
  3. With the low-risk gone, the pool is mostly high-risk, so the average — and the premium — climbs toward $5,000.
The premium chases the healthy out the door. That is adverse selection turning into a death spiral.
MisconceptionAdverse selection and moral hazard are basically the same thing.▸

This is the single most-tested confusion in the whole topic, so nail it. Adverse selection is hidden type, before the trade — who signs up (the sick are likelier to buy insurance). Moral hazard is hidden action, after the trade — how people behave once covered (the insured use more care). One is about selection into the deal; the other is about behavior within it. Before/after, type/action. If you can place a scenario on that timeline, you can name it.

Quick checkHow could a government policy stop an insurance death spiral?▸
By keeping the healthy in the pool — for example, a mandate to buy coverage or subsidies that make the average premium worth it to low-risk people. Both prevent the healthy from self-selecting out.
Major concept 3

Moral Hazard

Hidden action after the trade: protection quietly changes behavior.

Moral hazard is the after-the-trade twin of adverse selection. Once someone is insured, the price they face at the point of care drops toward zero, and behavior shifts: more visits, more tests, less attention to prevention. The patient is not lying or cheating — they are responding rationally to a changed incentive, the same way the fisher and the free rider did in earlier chapters. When the cost of the next unit lands on someone else, people consume more of it.

The repair is to keep a sliver of the price in front of the patient. A deductible makes the patient pay the first portion; a copay keeps a small charge on each visit. These tools restore some price signal — but only some, and here is the tension that runs through every health system: push too hard and you discourage care people genuinely need, including the prevention that saves money later. Protect too completely and you invite waste. There is no setting of the dial that is right for everyone. We are about to see why that dial exists at all.

MisconceptionMoral hazard means insured people are being dishonest.▸

The word sounds like an accusation, but it describes a structure, not a character flaw. A perfectly honest person uses more care when care is nearly free at the point of use, because that is a sensible response to the price they face. Moral hazard is the predictable result of separating the person who decides from the person who pays — the same incentive logic as a negative externality or a commons, not evidence of bad morals. The fix is in the incentives (deductibles, copays), not in lectures about honesty.

Major concept 4

The Problem of Insurance

Insurance is built for catastrophe — so why does it pay for your checkup?

Start with what insurance is actually for. To an economist, insurance pools risk that is rare, ruinous, and hard to predict — the house fire, the car crash, the cancer. You pay a small certain premium to be protected from a large uncertain loss, and pooling many people makes that possible. By that logic you would never insure the predictable. You do not buy "grocery insurance," because groceries are a known, routine cost, not a risk; running them through an insurer would just add a middleman and a markup. The same should be true of a routine checkup or a generic prescription. Those are known costs, not catastrophes.

And yet insurance in many countries pays for exactly those routine things. Why did it sprawl so far past catastrophe? The center of the answer is buyer market power. A large insurer or employer brings millions of patients to the table, and uses that bulk to negotiate "in-network" prices well below the list or cash price a lone patient would face. So even for ordinary care, running the bill through insurance often gets you a lower price than paying cash — you are borrowing the big buyer's bargaining power. That perverse discount quietly pulls more and more spending under the insurance umbrella, far past the catastrophic core. (A historical accident reinforced it: during World War II, US wage controls pushed employers to compete with tax-free health benefits, welding insurance to the job.) Notice the shape of that argument — a powerful buyer bending prices — because it is the doorway to Unit 4.

So is insurance good? Yes, and that is the point: it is good and it is a trade-off. Pooling catastrophic risk is what makes Chapter 15's inescapable demand survivable — it is the reason a heart attack does not have to mean bankruptcy. But stretch insurance to cover the routine and you trade away the price signal on everything it touches. This is where the third party who pays comes into focus. Whether the payer is a private insurer or the government, the patient is now spending someone else's money — Friedman's bottom row from Chapter 1, where the link between cost and value is weakest. That is the same fact as moral hazard, seen from the spending side rather than the behavior side. The honest question every system has to answer is where to draw the line between what we insure and what we simply pay for.

MisconceptionMore insurance coverage is always better.▸

Broader coverage feels unambiguously good, but economics says it is a trade-off. Insurance earns its keep on the rare and ruinous, where pooling protects you from disaster. Pushed onto routine, predictable costs, it mostly adds administration and erases the price you would otherwise weigh — which feeds the cost growth everyone complains about. "Cover everything" and "control costs" pull against each other. The interesting design question is not more or less coverage but which costs belong under the umbrella.

Retrieval practiceIn Friedman's terms, why does third-party payment weaken cost control?▸
Because the patient is spending someone else's money on themselves — the cell where people care about value but not cost. The decision-maker no longer feels the price, so the discipline that links cost to value goes slack. Moral hazard is this same fact viewed from the patient's behavior.
Major concept 5

Signaling, Screening, and the Agent Who Knows More

Markets build tools to make hidden information credible — from both sides.

Hidden information does not always win. Two repair strategies sort the parties out, depending on who moves. Signaling is the informed side revealing itself with something costly and hard to fake: a surgeon's board certification, a used-car dealer's warranty, a degree, a reputation built over years. The signal works precisely because a low-quality type would find it too expensive to imitate.

Screening is the mirror image: the uninformed side designs choices that make the hidden types sort themselves. An insurer offering a menu of plans — high deductible and low premium, or low deductible and high premium — lets healthy and sick buyers reveal their type by which they choose. The test does the work the missing information could not.

Underneath all of this in medicine sits the principal-agent problem. Your doctor is your agent, choosing on your behalf, but with their own incentives and far more knowledge. Pay them per procedure and the structure quietly nudges toward more procedures; pay them a flat fee per patient and it nudges toward fewer. The point is not that doctors are venal — it is that, as with every failure in this unit, the structure shapes the choice before character ever enters.

Where this comes fromAkerlof, Spence, and Stiglitz

The three pieces of this concept map onto three economists who shared the 2001 Nobel Prize for the analysis of markets with asymmetric information. George Akerlof built the lemons model that gives adverse selection its shape. Michael Spence formalized signaling — why a costly, hard-to-fake action can credibly carry hidden information. Joseph Stiglitz developed screening, where the uninformed side designs the sorting mechanism.

This is fair AP territory, not just framing: adverse selection, moral hazard, signaling, screening, and the principal-agent problem are all named, testable concepts. What you will not be asked to do is graph them — the work here is precise identification, which is exactly what the death-spiral and before/after distinctions train.

Quick checkA job applicant earns an expensive certification mainly to prove they are capable. Signaling or screening?▸
Signaling. The informed side (the applicant) takes a costly, hard-to-fake action to reveal hidden quality. If the employer instead designed a test to sort applicants, that would be screening.
Major concept 6

Different Countries, Different Bets

Every system is a wager about which failure hurts most. None escapes them all.

If there were a clean answer, the world would have copied it. Instead, every country builds a different machine, and each machine is best understood as a bet — a decision about which of this unit's failures it will fight hardest, and therefore which it is willing to live with. Read the table from the most market-driven systems at the top to the most fully socialized at the bottom, and watch the trade-off rotate.

Look down the "bet" column and the lesson of the unit stares back. The market-driven systems guard the price signal and pay for it in catastrophic exposure and inequity. The socialized systems guarantee access and pay for it in rationing and queues. Singapore is the interesting outlier precisely because it read this chapter: it insures the catastrophic, makes people feel the price of the routine, and leans on the state only where buyers are weakest. It is not a free lunch either — nothing here is — but it is a deliberate answer to the exact failures we have catalogued.

MisconceptionThe lesson is obviously single-payer (or obviously the free market).▸

Whichever side you walked in believing, this table should complicate it. The failures genuinely pull in different directions: the case for broad public pools (adverse selection, catastrophic risk) sits right next to the case for keeping price signals alive (moral hazard, the problem of insurance). Every real system buys a solution to one failure by accepting more of another. An honest reading does not hand you one country to copy; it hands you a menu of trade-offs that reasonable people weigh differently. The settled part is the diagnosis. The system is the contested part.

Major concept 7

Healthcare Breaks Every Condition

Pick any market assumption from this unit. Healthcare violates it — which is why no single fix can work.

Step back from the countries and ask why none of them found a clean answer. The reason is the whole unit at once. A market delivers its famous results only when a list of quiet conditions holds, and across six chapters we pulled them out one at a time and watched a market stumble each time one failed. Healthcare is the chapter where they fail together, in one place, on one person. Read the table as a map of everything you have learned.

Six failures, one patient, all at once. That is why no single lever works: inescapable demand argues for shielding people from catastrophic bills, moral hazard argues for keeping a price in front of them, adverse selection argues for broad mandatory pools, positive externalities argue for subsidizing the spillovers, and the antibiotic commons argues for restraint. Each diagnosis is solid; each points at a different, sometimes opposite, instrument. If economists share a discipline here, it is a sorting rule rather than a single policy: insure the catastrophic, price the shoppable, subsidize the essential and the spillover-rich, and publicly fund the public goods. Match the tool to the failure — and name which failure you are fixing before you reach for a fix.

MisconceptionHealthcare is expensive mainly because of greed.▸

Greed exists in every industry, including the ones with cheap, well-functioning markets, so it cannot be the distinguishing cause. What makes healthcare different is the stack of structural failures in this table firing at once: inescapable demand, a third party who pays, and information so lopsided the buyer cannot judge the product. A market with all of those features would behave strangely even if everyone in it were a saint. Blaming character skips the diagnosis — and points at the wrong fix.

On the exam

Name the failure, then match the fix

The moves you can now make: separate hidden type from hidden action, trace a death spiral, and sort a real health policy onto the failure it actually addresses.

Identification

People who expect to need a lot of dental work are the most likely to buy dental insurance. Adverse selection or moral hazard?

Reveal answer▸

Adverse selection: hidden type, before the trade. The high-need types self-select into the pool. (If instead insured people started scheduling more optional procedures, that would be moral hazard — hidden action, after.)

Mechanism

Explain, in order, the steps of an insurance death spiral.

Reveal answer▸

The insurer cannot see who is healthy, so it charges the average premium; healthy buyers find the average too expensive and drop out; the remaining pool is sicker, so average cost rises; the insurer raises the premium; the next-healthiest tier drops out; repeat until only the sickest remain.

Policy match

A government subsidizes flu vaccines. Which market failure is it addressing, and from which chapter?

Reveal answer▸

A positive externality (Chapter 18), corrected with a Pigouvian subsidy (Chapter 19). Vaccination benefits third parties through reduced spread, so the private market under-provides it.

Reveal common mistakes▸
  • Swapping adverse selection and moral hazard. Selection is who signs up (before); hazard is how they behave (after).
  • Calling the death spiral a supply-and-demand shift. It is a self-reinforcing pool dynamic, not a single curve movement.
  • Subsidizing a negative externality or taxing a positive one. Vaccines spill benefits, so they get a subsidy.
  • Claiming one policy fixes everything. Different failures here call for different, sometimes opposing, tools.
Unit thesis

Healthcare is the market where every failure in this unit fires at once — you cannot escape the price, the rules are contested, the resource depletes, costs and benefits spill over, and one side knows what the other cannot — which is why intervention is so often justified, why no single fix can resolve it, and why every country ends up betting differently on which failure to swallow.

If economists ruled the world…

they would protect people from catastrophic medical cost without making every decision feel free at the margin: insure the catastrophic, price the shoppable, subsidize the essential and the spillovers, and publicly fund the public goods — matching each tool to the failure it answers rather than reaching for one grand solution.

  • How to balance affordability today against the innovation that higher prices help fund?
Takeaways
In your life

When a market feels broken, resist the one-word explanation. Ask which condition it breaks — can you escape the price, can you judge the product, who pays, who else is affected — and the real problem usually names itself.

In your work

Before proposing a fix, name the failure it targets. Insurance, pricing, subsidies, and mandates each solve a different problem; matching the tool to the failure is the whole skill.

Where this unit ends — and what comes next

This is the close of the critique. Across six chapters the verdict held its shape: the failures are real and the diagnoses are settled, but every remedy is harder than the failure it answers, because the fixer is also self-interested, the information is also missing, and the rights are also contested. That asymmetry — settled failure, contested fix — is what kept this unit honest rather than turning it into a brief against markets. Unit 4 turns to a different worry: not markets that fail because a condition is missing, but markets distorted because one player grows powerful enough to bend the rules in its favor. You already met the shape of it in this chapter, when a giant insurer used its bulk to wedge prices down. From the absent fence to the heavy thumb on the scale. Unit 4 · Market Power
