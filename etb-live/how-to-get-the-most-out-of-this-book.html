How to Get the Most Out of This Book

A short guide to learning economics instead of merely reading about it

Read this before you begin the chapters.

Economics is easier to understand when you stop treating it as a collection of definitions and start using it to explain what people do.

This book will introduce graphs, models, formulas, vocabulary, and rules. Those tools matter, but the goal is not to memorize them in isolation. The goal is to understand how choices, incentives, tradeoffs, institutions, and unintended consequences shape the world around you.

First, practice using a tool until you are consistent and competent.

Then practice choosing the right tool from the toolkit for the job at hand.

The best way to learn economics is to make predictions, discover where your thinking breaks down, and try again.

Five study techniques that actually help

Jamie Clark’s one-page guide, Effective Study Strategies, describes five research-backed ways to learn more effectively. You can also find Jamie’s larger collection of teaching guides. These techniques may feel less smooth than rereading or highlighting. That is often part of their value.

1. Retrieval practice: recall information

Retrieval practice means trying to remember something before looking at the answer. Close the book and ask: What was the main idea? What do I remember? Can I draw the graph or explain the rule from memory?

Use the book’s pause-and-retrieve questions, quick checks, worked examples, and synthesis questions for this purpose. Try first. Then check your answer and repair what you missed.

2. Spaced practice: distribute your studying

Spaced practice means returning to material over several days or weeks rather than studying everything in one long session.

Read a section today, recall it tomorrow, and use it in a new problem several days later. When you return after some forgetting, your brain has to work to retrieve the idea. That work helps make learning durable.

3. Interleaving: mix up what you practice

Interleaving means mixing different topics or problem types instead of practicing one kind repeatedly until it feels easy.

Early on, it is useful to practice the same kind of problem several times. You are learning how to use the tool. Once you can use it consistently, start mixing. Practice deciding whether a situation calls for elasticity, supply and demand, tax incidence, cost analysis, or another model.

Real economic questions do not announce which chapter contains the answer. Spiral review helps you practice recognizing the problem before solving it.

4. Dual coding: combine words and meaningful visuals

Dual coding means representing an idea with both words and a useful visual. Draw a graph, create a flowchart, sketch a model, or pair a short explanation with a figure.

The visual should clarify the idea. A simple graph that shows what changes is more useful than a page decorated with colors and pictures.

5. Elaboration: explain how and why

Elaboration means connecting a new idea to what you already know and asking deeper questions:

  • Why does this happen?
  • How does this idea relate to the previous one?
  • What is an example and a non-example?
  • Who benefits, and who pays?
  • What would make this explanation incomplete?
First learn the move. Then learn when to make it.

Blocked practice and spiral practice do different jobs. Blocked practice helps you learn how to perform a skill. Spiral or interleaved practice helps you learn when and why to use it.

Practice typeMain question
Blocked practiceCan I perform this skill?
Spiral or interleaved practiceCan I recognize when this skill is needed?
Transfer practiceCan I adapt the skill in a new situation?

When you see a graph, practice asking:

  1. What is on each axis?
  2. What changed?
  3. Which curve or point should move?
  4. What happens to the equilibrium price or quantity?
  5. Who benefits, and who pays?
Use the book’s features

Pause and retrieve questions ask you to recall an idea before moving on. Quick checks test a specific skill. Worked examples show a method that you should later try to recreate. What to notice sections point out something important in a graph or argument. Misconception sections show how an appealing idea can lead you astray. See the unseen questions ask you to look beyond the most obvious effect.

Use the interactive graphs, optional videos, and audio when they help you see or hear an idea differently. But make a prediction before touching an interactive. Otherwise, you may only be watching the answer happen.

Use AI to practice reasoning

AI can give you additional practice, but do not ask it to do the thinking for you. Try a prompt like this:

Quiz me on this chapter one question at a time. Include graph interpretation, calculations, explanations, and real-world applications. Do not show me the answer until I respond. If I make a mistake, give me a hint and ask a smaller question. Make me explain why my answer is correct. Give me a new example with different numbers when I seem to understand the first one.

You can also ask AI to give you a plausible wrong answer and let you diagnose it, describe a situation without naming the economic concept, or mix old and new problem types together.

AI can make mistakes, especially in calculations, graphs, and claims about current events. Check its work against the book and redo important problems yourself.

A simple study routine
  1. Read until you reach a natural stopping point.
  2. Make a prediction before looking at the answer.
  3. Complete a quick check or retrieval question.
  4. Draw or explain the graph from memory.
  5. Correct your reasoning.
  6. Return to the idea later with a new example.

The point is not to move through the book as quickly as possible. The point is to leave each section able to explain something you could not explain before.
