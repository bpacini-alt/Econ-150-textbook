Institutions

There is no such thing as a free market

Chapter 16~ 25 min read

In his seminal work studying the informal economy of Lima, Peru, economist Hernando de Soto documented a striking paradox of human enterprise. When his research team tried to obtain legal authorization for a small seamstress shop, they found themselves trapped in 289 days of bureaucratic wrangling. It was enormously difficult to start even a small private business. Because formal state institutions were costly, inaccessible, and often extractive, ordinary people were locked out of legal property rights and contracts.

Yet commerce did not disappear. Citizens built a massive, vibrant parallel economy complete with their own informal property claims, business associations, housing, and transportation systems. The catch was that these informal institutions could not provide everything that formal law could. Without legally recognized property titles and enforceable contracts, many entrepreneurs struggled to use their assets as collateral, resolve disputes in court, or expand their businesses beyond a survival level. De Soto called this trapped wealth “dead capital.”

It would be easy to take the wrong lesson from Lima: that the state is the enemy, and markets thrive once it gets out of the way. A second story makes that lesson harder to hold.

Now consider the New York Diamond Dealers Club, famously studied by legal scholar Lisa Bernstein in her classic 1992 paper, “Opting Out of the Legal System: Extralegal Contractual Relations in the Diamond Industry.” Bernstein examined a global market in which merchants routinely exchanged high-value gemstones, sometimes with little more than a handshake and the traditional words “mazel and broche” (Yiddish for "luck and a blessing!")

The handshake worked because it activated a dense institutional system. Rather than relying primarily on state courts, diamond dealers used private arbitration, membership rules, reputational networks, and the threat of expulsion from the trading community. A dealer who violated the club’s rules could lose access not only to one business partner but to an entire network of suppliers, buyers, and future opportunities. The market had not escaped governance. It had created its own form of governance.

Popular economic rhetoric often frames the world as a binary choice: the state either steps aside and lets the free market work its magic, or heavy-handed government intervention suffocates private enterprise. But Lima’s informal economy and New York’s Diamond Dealers Club undermine that simplistic narrative. Markets do not float freely in a vacuum. They depend on rules about property, contracts, information, liability, and enforcement. Those rules may be supplied by governments, private associations, reputational networks, or some combination of all three. The important questions are: Who creates the rules? How are they enforced? Whom do they serve? And do they make mutually beneficial exchange easier or harder?

Paul Romer calls these "the rules of the game." Lima and the diamond district show that markets cannot run without them, whether they come from a legislature, a trade association, or a reputation. This chapter is about what those rules are, who writes them, and what happens when they break.

The big picture
A market is not the absence of rules; it is a product of them. Prices, profit, and competition all presuppose that someone owns the goods, that non-payers can be kept out, that contracts bind, and that a court stands behind the deal. Government can strengthen a market by setting and enforcing those rules, and it can weaken one by neglecting them or selling them to insiders. By the end of this chapter you should be able to name the institutions a working market quietly relies on, tell a market failure apart from an institutional failure, explain how corruption bends the rules toward the people who control them, and classify a good by excludability and rivalry.

Major concept 1: Markets Are Built

The Institutional Foundations of Markets

A market is not what is left when the rules are gone. It is one of the things the rules build.

The first two units of this book argue that markets are a miracle, and they do not stammer. Prices coordinate. Profit and loss discipline. Competition reallocates. Entry and exit keep everyone honest. None of that was wrong. The case for markets is strong, and should be taken seriously.

In the previous chapter, we began our critique of markets by raising questions about morality versus efficiency. All things considered, markets remain among the most effective engines for human flourishing we have, but that does not mean they do not deserve serious critique. We have now offered two such critiques: elasticity shows us that sometimes people simply do not have many options; and just because something is efficient, that does not make it moral.

This chapter pushes the critique further.

The case for markets rests on a foundation of assumptions that we have yet to examine. The greatest and first of these is simply this: A market cannot scale until people know what can be owned, what can be sold, what counts as fraud, who can be kept out, which promises are enforceable, and who answers for harm. Without these, markets may exist, but they cannot easily become secure, accessible, scalable, and productive.

This chapter does not argue that markets are bad; it argues that markets are built.

Two Kinds of Failure

Think back to Lima. People there had skills, willing customers, and every reason to start businesses. What stopped them was the 289 days of paperwork it took to get permission. That is a problem with the rules underneath the market, and no change to the market itself would have fixed it.

That points to a habit worth building early. When an outcome goes badly, it can have two possible authors. Sometimes the market failed: even with good rules, buyers and sellers left alone produce too much of something harmful, too little of something valuable, or too much power in one firm's hands. The rest of this unit and the next are about those cases.

Other times the rules failed. Call this an institutional failure: the property right is unclear, the court will not enforce the contract, the registry is years behind, or the official wants a bribe. The repair for an institutional failure is usually a better rule, such as a clearer title, a working court, or an honest registry. That doesn't always mean more government. Sometimes it means removing a rule that blocks people, like Lima's permits.

Before choosing a fix, figure out which kind of failure you're looking at.

Big questionWhat is prosperity for?

If markets are built, it is fair to ask what they are built for. Economics has a lot to say about how a society becomes richer. It has much less to say about what the riches are for.

The Liberal Synthesis from this book's introduction leaves that question open on purpose. A liberal society protects your right to own, trade, speak, and worship, and then leaves it to you, your family, and your church to decide what a good life looks like. There is wisdom in that restraint: a government that answered the question for everyone would have to enforce its answer. But it leaves markets depending on something they cannot make. The German legal scholar Ernst-Wolfgang Böckenförde said the same of the liberal state: it lives on moral conditions it cannot guarantee by itself. Markets need honest people and people who know what their prosperity is for, and no price can produce either one.

Christianity has stood on both sides of this story. The belief that every person has equal worth before God helped supply the idea of human dignity that rights and markets came to rest on. The same tradition has produced some of the market's sharpest critics: John Chrysostom preaching against hoarding while the poor went hungry, John Wesley telling Methodists to "gain all you can, save all you can, and give all you can," Pope Leo XIII defending workers against an industry that treated them as tools, and Martin Luther King Jr. judging an economy by how it treated the poor.

Chapter 26 takes this question up directly. For now, keep it in mind as you read the rest of the unit. A market measures what people will pay for things. It has no way to measure whether those things are worth wanting, and that judgment falls to each of us.

MisconceptionA "free market" is a market with no government or rules.▸

Free means free within rules, not free of them. Every market presupposes property, contracts, courts, and enforcement. Strip those away and you do not get a freer market; you get no market, just a contest over who can grab what and get away with it. The argument is never "rules versus freedom"; it is always "which rules."

Retrieval practiceIn one sentence, why can't a market exist "before" its rules?▸
Because a price presupposes that something can be owned, that non-payers can sometimes be excluded, that promises are enforceable, and that a court stands behind the deal — so the rules are not a limit added on top of the market; they are what make exchange possible in the first place.

Major concept 2

Property Rights

Ownership Needs Institutions Too

A property right is best understood as a bundle— the right to use a resource, sell it, improve it, exclude others from it, transfer it, and seek compensation when it is damaged. Without a clear bundle, exchange turns fragile: investment becomes a gamble, bargaining becomes confused, and responsibility for harm becomes impossible to pin down. Property rights answer questions that markets cannot answer by themselves:

Who may use this land? Who may sell it? Who may inherit it? Who may enter it? Who is responsible if it causes harm?

Property rights are therefore not merely about ownership. They are about legal recognition, authority, exclusion, transfer, and responsibility.

Where the line gets drawn — Friedman's airplane

The economist Milton Friedman liked to make an important point using an airplane: no one thinks a jet cruising at ten thousand feet trespasses on the house below, and everyone thinks a plane clipping the roof at ten feet does — yet nothing in nature marks where, in between, the right to the airspace ends. Somebody has to draw that line and enforce it. Property rights are not discovered lying in the world; they are defined by social rules. Setting property rights is one of the most important roles for government.

HistorySlavery and property rights

To give an example of just how critical property rights are, let us consider one of the most consequential property rights of all: the right to one's own person. A society must decide not only who can own property—men or women, Black people or white people, citizens or foreigners—but also whether human beings themselves can be treated as property.

Slavery was not simply a society without property rights. It was a society that gave some people property rights over other people. Law defined human beings as transferable assets, authorized their sale and inheritance, enforced claims over their labor, and denied them the ordinary rights of personhood.

Seen this way, abolition was among other things a fight over the property-rights bundle. It took a whole category of claims off the books, and it moved the most basic right of all, ownership of oneself, to the people who had been denied it.

But a right you cannot enforce is only a wish with your name on it. If a court will not stand behind your contract, the contract is decoration. If the rules change unpredictably, people cannot reliably invest; they are only betting on political outcomes. If fraud is tolerated, buyers stop trusting sellers, and trade thins out. This is why rule of law is so important: the background promise that the rules are known in advance and applied to everyone the same way. When we say "rule of law" we also mean courts, liability rules, and enforcement like police, all of which help decide who must answer when one person's actions damage another.

Here are some of the institutions the earlier market model has quietly been assuming the whole time.

Institutions as technology

It helps to think of institutions less like fences and more like tools. A reliable court, a working land registry, a trusted payment system — each one lowers the cost and risk of exchange, the way a better machine lowers the cost of production. If a society can build or borrow rules that make investing and trading safer, those rules can generate wealth almost the way a new technology does. Closer to home, think of the fifty states as laboratories of democracy — each testing rules, watching what happens, and copying what works. Institutions can be learned. That does not guarantee the lessons are wise or just.

Big ideaCharter cities: a market for institutions?

The economist Paul Romer starts somewhere surprising: immigration. When a person moves from a poor country to a rich one, much of what changes for them is not the scenery — it is the rules. The same person, with the same skills, often becomes far more productive simply by stepping into a better set of institutions. So a large share of the gain from migration is really a gain from switching rule-sets.

That reframes the problem. If the value lives in the rules, perhaps we do not have to move every person to the institutions — perhaps we can bring the institutions to the people. Romer's charter city is exactly that: a new jurisdiction that adopts a trusted package of rules — property, courts, contract enforcement — and invites people to live and invest under them.

Push the idea one step further and you get something striking: a market for institutions. Just as firms compete to offer better goods, jurisdictions could compete to offer better rules, with people and capital voting with their feet toward the places that govern well. Keep it honest — charter cities are contested and rarely tried, and who writes the charter is a hard question. But as a thought experiment it crystallizes the chapter's thesis: institutions are not the background to the market. They are one of its products.

Quick checkA startup signs a supplier deal, but local courts routinely ignore contracts they dislike. Which quiet assumption has broken, and what happens to trade?▸
Courts and enforcement (and with them, rule of law). When agreements can't be enforced, contracts lose their teeth — credit dries up, long deals get riskier, and trade shrinks toward people you already trust.

Major concept 3

Corruption

When the Rules Work… for Certain People

Good rules make markets flourish. What do bad rules do?

The economists Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson draw a line between two kinds of rules. Inclusive institutions spread property rights, impartial enforcement, and open entry across a whole society. Extractive institutions concentrate those same things in the hands of insiders — protected monopolies, captured courts, ownership reserved for the connected.

The unsettling part is that a market on extractive institutions still runs. Prices clear. Trades happen. On the surface it looks like any other market. But it is quietly transmitting the rigging beneath it — funneling value toward whoever wrote the rules — in prices that read as perfectly neutral. As a simple explanation, think of it this way: institutions always work. The question is who they work for. And typically, the answer is "institutions work for those who built them."

In other words, corruption.

Across countries, higher corruption travels with lower income, lower investment, weaker firm growth, and slower long-run development. An older theory held that corruption might "grease the wheels," a bribe helping a business slip past a slow bureaucracy. Most development economists now think the opposite is closer to the truth: corruption sands the wheels. It makes rules unpredictable, raises transaction costs, and rewards connections over value. Worse, it is self-feeding: an official who can demand a bribe to remove an obstacle has every reason to invent the obstacle in the first place.

Read that pattern carefully. It is a correlation, not a clean proof that corruption alone causes poverty. Poor countries may have weak institutions partly because they are poor, and both corruption and poverty may trace back to deeper causes — conflict, instability, a broken rule of law. But the association is large, persistent, and consistent with a clear mechanism, and the direction of the modern consensus is not in doubt: corruption is a drag, not a lubricant.

So the useful question is not the hopeless "how do we make people honest?" It is institutional: how do we make corruption harder to hide, easier to detect, and costlier to attempt? Open records and transparent budgets, competitive public contracting, independent audits, a free press, impartial courts. But transparency alone is not enough — and this is the part people skip. Information only bites when someone can act on it and a court can impose a real cost. A leaked ledger changes nothing if no one is empowered to prosecute. That is the same lesson as Concept 2 wearing new clothes: a rule is only as strong as the enforcement behind it.

The deepest point is what corruption does to competition itself. When rules are for sale, people stop competing to create value and start competing for influence — for licenses, contracts, and privileges. Economists call that rent-seeking, and it is the mirror image of the market's best promise. Strong institutions tilt the field back, so that reward flows toward serving people rather than capturing rules.

The Hidden Price of a Deal

Corruption adds a cost that never shows up on a price tag. Economists call it a transaction cost: everything you pay to make a deal happen beyond the price itself. Buying a car costs the sticker price plus the hours spent searching listings, test-driving, haggling, and arranging a loan. Buying a house carries so much searching, inspecting, and paperwork that most people hire a realtor to shoulder part of it. Chapter 11 showed that some of the most valuable firms, Uber among them, are really in the business of shrinking these costs.

Good institutions do the same thing for a whole society. A trusted court makes a promise cheap to enforce. A clean land registry makes it cheap to check who owns a lot. Corruption runs the other way. Every bribe, invented delay, and favor owed is a new cost bolted onto the deal, and it is collected by the people who captured the rules.

The idea returns in Chapter 18. The economist Ronald Coase showed that when rights are clear and transaction costs are low, neighbors can sometimes settle a dispute with a private bargain instead of a government rule. When transaction costs are high, they cannot. Either way, transaction costs decide which deals are possible, and institutions do much of the work of setting them.

Retrieval practiceWhat is the modern "sands the wheels" view of corruption, and why is the cross-country pattern only a correlation?▸
Corruption generally slows an economy: it makes rules unpredictable, raises transaction costs, rewards connections over value, and gives officials a reason to manufacture the delays they charge to remove. The link to low income is a strong, persistent correlation, not proof of one-way cause — poverty and corruption can share deeper roots, and causation likely runs in both directions.

Major concept 4

Public Goods and Common Resources

Not Every Good Thing Is a Public Good

Property rights work best when an owner can keep other people out. Some goods make that easy, and some make it nearly impossible, which is why markets handle some goods well and others badly. Economists sort goods with two questions. First, excludability: can non-payers be kept out? A theater checks tickets; a login gates a stream; a grocer keeps the sandwich behind the counter. But some goods are not easy to exclude: groundwater, national defense, or fireworks.

Second, rivalry: does one person's use subtract from another's? A sandwich is rival — if you eat it, I can't. A national-defense system is non-rival — protecting you doesn't use up protection for me.

Cross those two questions and you get four kinds of good. This is one of the highest-yield tools in the unit, so it is worth your time to learn to place a good on it quickly.

The next chapter takes up how to respond to each kind of good, starting with the common resource. For now, your job is to understand what the four kinds are and how to tell them apart.

Education is usually not a public good

Education may be good for the public, but it is usually not a public good in the technical sense. Schools can exclude non-enrolled students, and seats, teacher attention, and resources are partly rival. Education can still deserve public support — for its positive spillovers, for equity, for productivity — without being a public good. The classification asks only two questions, and "it's socially valuable" is not one of them.

Quick checkA streaming service can lock out non-subscribers, and one more viewer rarely degrades the stream for others. Which of the four goods is it?▸
A club good — excludable (a login keeps non-payers out) but non-rival for many users (your watching doesn't use up the show for me), at least until congestion.
Retrieval practiceWhy is the provider (government or private firm) irrelevant to whether something is a public good?▸
Because the category is defined by two features of the good itself — excludability and rivalry — not by who supplies it. Governments sell plenty of private goods (toll roads, postage, university seats), and private actors provide things with public-good features. Ask only: can non-payers be excluded, and does one person's use subtract from another's?
On the exam

Diagnose the good, then diagnose the failure

The moves you can now make: classify a good by excludability and rivalry, separate a true public good from a good that is merely valuable, and tell a market failure apart from an institutional failure.

Classification

A crowded, unpriced city road is difficult to keep non-payers off, and each extra driver slows everyone else down. Place it on the four-goods grid, and name the coordination problem its cell is prone to.

Reveal answer▸

Non-excludable and rival → a common resource. Its characteristic problem is overuse (here, congestion). How to actually fix that — pricing, quotas, access rules — is Chapter 17's job; classification is yours.

Technical definition

A city subsidizes seats at a public university and calls them a "public good." Using the two classification questions, explain why that label is technically wrong — and why the seats might still deserve subsidy.

Reveal answer▸

University seats are excludable (admissions and tuition keep non-payers out) and rival (a filled seat and a professor's attention are finite). Both classification questions fail, so it is not a public good — the government provider is irrelevant to the category. It can still merit subsidy for positive spillovers, equity, or productivity; "worth funding" and "public good" are different claims.

Identify and explain

In one country, a promising firm can't raise money because courts won't reliably enforce loan contracts. In another, a factory's smoke damages a laundry next door and no one is liable. For each, say whether the better diagnosis is "the market failed" or "the rules failed," and name the institution at stake.

Reveal answer▸

Both are closer to institutional failures than raw market failures. The first is broken contract enforcement / rule of law — fix the courts, not the credit market. The second is a missing liability rule (an unpriced externality) — assign the right and a remedy may follow, sometimes by private bargaining. In each case the repair is a clearer or better-enforced rule.

FRQ practice

The harbor town

Suggested time: 8 minutes · 5 points · no graph required

A fish-processing company wants to build a plant on a lot beside a small town's harbor. It has a buyer lined up for its product and a bank willing to lend, but the town's land registry is six years behind, so the company cannot prove the seller actually owns the lot, and the bank will not lend without proof. A town official quietly offers to "move the paperwork along" for a cash fee. Meanwhile, anyone with a boat may fish in the bay, and the catch has been shrinking for a decade. Every ship that enters the harbor at night relies on the town's lighthouse.

  1. Identify the institution whose failure is stopping the bank from lending, and explain why this is better diagnosed as an institutional failure than a market failure. 1 pt
  2. Explain how the official's fee affects the company's transaction costs, and name the behavior the official is engaged in. 1 pt
  3. Paying the fee might get this one plant built faster. Explain why a town where such fees are normal is likely to grow more slowly over time. 1 pt
  4. Classify the fish in the bay using excludability and rivalry, and name the problem typical of that kind of good. 1 pt
  5. Classify the lighthouse's signal using excludability and rivalry, and explain why a private firm would struggle to pay for it by selling it. 1 pt
Reveal scoring guide▸
  • 1 ptNames the land registry, clear title, or property rights (rule of law also earns credit). Explains that a willing buyer, seller, and lender already exist, so the market is ready; what is missing is a working rule that proves ownership.
  • 1 ptThe fee raises transaction costs: it is an extra cost of completing the deal beyond the price of the land. The official is engaged in corruption or rent-seeking, gaining wealth by selling access to a rule rather than by producing anything.
  • 1 ptAny one of: corruption "sands the wheels" by making rules unpredictable and raising costs for every deal; it rewards connections over value; it gives officials a reason to create delays they can charge to remove; investors and lenders stay away when outcomes depend on bribes.
  • 1 ptNon-excludable (anyone with a boat can fish) and rival (a fish caught is gone), so a common resource. Its typical problem is overuse or depletion.
  • 1 ptNon-excludable (a ship cannot be kept from seeing the light) and non-rival (one ship seeing it does not dim it for others), so a public good. Ships can benefit without paying, so free riding makes it hard to fund by sale.
Reveal common mistakes▸
  • ConceptCalling the registry backlog a market failure. The buyer, seller, and lender are all willing; the rule underneath them is broken.
  • ConceptTreating the fee as a harmless price for speed. That is the old "grease the wheels" view; the fee raises costs, rewards connections, and invites more delays.
  • ConceptCalling the fish a public good because no one owns the bay. Fish are rival, which makes them a common resource.
  • CraftPrescribing a fix for the bay, such as quotas or permits, when the question only asks you to classify. Remedies are Chapter 17's job.

Market thesis

A market is a product of its rules, not the absence of them — so getting the institutions right is not a limit on the market. It is what lets the market work at all.

If economists ruled the world…

they would pour effort into the plumbing — clear property rights, honest courts, low transaction costs, uncaptured rules — because get that right and self-interest quietly serves strangers. The wealth of nations turns out to be, in large part, the rules of nations.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Markets require property rights, contract enforcement, and rule of law before exchange works at all.
  • Whether a good is a public good depends on excludability and rivalry, not on who provides it.
  • Corruption is a drag on development, not a lubricant; the pattern is strong and consistent.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • When something goes wrong, is it the market that failed, or the rules? The diagnosis decides the cure.
  • When is the fix a clearer property right, and when is it a public program?
  • What is prosperity for, and who should answer that question?
Takeaways
In your life

When something feels like "just how the market works," ask who wrote the rules that made it work that way — and whether the fix is a better rule.

In your work

Your business runs on borrowed institutions — courts, contracts, clear title. They feel free until they fail; know what you are standing on.
