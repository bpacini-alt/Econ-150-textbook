Institutions

There is no such thing as a free market

Chapter 16~ 38 min read

In his seminal work studying the informal economy of Lima, Peru, economist Hernando de Soto documented a striking paradox of human enterprise. When his research team tried to obtain legal authorization for a small seamstress shop, they found themselves trapped in 289 days of bureaucratic wrangling. It was enormously difficult to start even a small private business. Because formal state institutions were costly, inaccessible, and often extractive, ordinary people were locked out of legal property rights and contracts.

Yet commerce did not disappear. Citizens built a massive, vibrant parallel economy complete with their own informal property claims, business associations, housing, and transportation systems. The catch was that these informal institutions could not provide everything that formal law could. Without legally recognized property titles and enforceable contracts, many entrepreneurs struggled to use their assets as collateral, resolve disputes in court, or expand their businesses beyond a survival level. De Soto called this trapped wealth “dead capital.”

In short, government can be an enemy to prosperity.

Now consider the New York Diamond Dealers Club, famously studied by legal scholar Lisa Bernstein in her classic 1992 paper, “Opting Out of the Legal System: Extralegal Contractual Relations in the Diamond Industry.” Bernstein examined a global market in which merchants routinely exchanged high-value gemstones, sometimes with little more than a handshake and the traditional words “mazel and broche” (yiddish for "luck and a blessing!")

The handshake worked because it activated a dense institutional system. Rather than relying primarily on state courts, diamond dealers used private arbitration, membership rules, reputational networks, and the threat of expulsion from the trading community. A dealer who violated the club’s rules could lose access not only to one business partner but to an entire network of suppliers, buyers, and future opportunities. The market had not escaped governance. It had created its own form of governance.

In short, markets are not a product of zero intervention. They are a product of the right kinds of institutions.

Popular economic rhetoric often frames the world as a binary choice: the state either steps aside and lets the free market work its magic, or heavy-handed government intervention suffocates private enterprise. But Lima’s informal economy and New York’s Diamond Dealers Club undermine that simplistic narrative. Markets do not float freely in a vacuum. They depend on rules about property, contracts, information, liability, and enforcement. Those rules may be supplied by governments, private associations, reputational networks, or some combination of all three. The important questions are: Who creates the rules? How are they enforced? Whom do they serve? And do they make mutually beneficial exchange easier or harder?

The work of economist Daron Acemoglu and his colleagues helps place these examples in a much larger historical pattern. In research recognized with the 2024 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences, Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson showed how political and economic institutions help determine whether societies broadly share in prosperity or concentrate power and wealth among a narrow elite. Inclusive institutions give ordinary people more secure property rights, greater access to economic opportunity, and a meaningful ability to participate in markets. Extractive institutions, by contrast, may preserve the appearance of economic activity while directing its benefits toward those who control the rules.

The lesson is not that every rule is good, or that government always improves markets. It is that economic prosperity is an output deriving from effective institutional design. Weak or inaccessible institutions can leave people trapped in informal arrangements, exposed to uncertainty and unable to scale. Heavy-handed or extractive institutions can crush enterprise under bureaucracy or reserve opportunity for insiders.

Markets function on a foundation of institutions, what economist Paul Romer calls "the rules of the game." Those rules can help people cooperate, invest, compete, and create value—or they can enable exclusion, coercion, and rent-seeking.

As a budding young economist, your job is to explore the kinds of institutions that markets require that produce broadly-shared prosperity.

The big picture
A market is not the absence of rules; it is a product of them. Prices, profit, and competition all presuppose that someone owns the goods, that non-payers can be kept out, that contracts bind, and that a court stands behind the deal. When government steps back, it can enhance a market, and it can just as easily remove the social scaffolds on which markets rely, making them unreliable, unequal, or fragile. By the end of this chapter you should be able to name the various institutions a working market quietly relies on, tell a market failure apart from an institutional failure, judge whether a private bargain could even repair a given problem, and separate a genuine harm from a bare dislike dressed up as a right.

Major concept 1: Markets as Packaged Deal

The Moral, Cultural, and Legal Roots of Markets

A market is not what is left when the rules are gone. It is one of the things the rules build.

The first fifteen chapters argu e that markets are a miracle, and they do not stammer. Prices coordinate. Profit and loss discipline. Competition reallocates. Entry and exit keep everyone honest. None of that was wrong. The case for markets is strong, and should be taken seriously.

In the previous chapter, we began our critique of markets by raising questions about morality versus efficiency. All things considered, markets remain among the most effective engines for human flourishing we have, but that does not mean they do not deserve serious critique. We have now offered two such critiques: elasticity shows us that sometimes people simply do not have many options; and just because something is efficient, that does not make it moral.

We push further in our critique in this chapter.

The case for markets rests on a foundation of assumptions that we have yet to examine. The greatest and first of these is simply this: A market cannot scale until people know what can be owned, what can be sold, what counts as fraud, who can be kept out, which promises are enforceable, and who answers for harm. Without these, markets may exist, but they cannot easily become secure, accessible, scalable, and productive.

This chapter does not argue that markets are bad; it argues that markets are built.

The Christian Roots—and Moral Limits—of Markets

Markets, including relatively free markets, are much older than modern liberalism. They flourished at various times and places long before the nineteenth century. To claim that markets are a product of the 1800s is a misunderstanding. It is equally a misunderstanding to suggest that markets have always existed in the same way they do now.

What changed in the 1800s was not the invention of markets, but their unprecedented expansion.

Markets were able to flourish in part because of liberal political ideals, constitutional government, secure property rights, expanding commerce, industrial technology, and improved legal institutions. As an easy mental anchor, recall that this expansion happened around the same time as the political and industrial revolutions that reshaped Europe and North America, amid a growing emphasis on universal human dignity, legal equality, and personal freedom, and around the same time as the twin revolutions of France and The United States.

Modern markets, then, emerged alongside a broader liberal order that increasingly emphasized universal human dignity, legal equality, personal freedom, and limits on arbitrary power. The market was not the entire package. It was one expression of a larger conviction that ordinary people, not just rulers, could be trusted to run their own lives.

As these forces came together, markets scaled across national and global networks, producing an extraordinary surge in productivity, prosperity, and human population.

What was the role of Christian thought in this expansion?

Perhaps no one has argued more forcefully or memorably about the connections between Christian thought and markets than Max Weber. Weber argued that Protestant ideas about vocation, discipline, and worldly conduct helped create a culture compatible with capitalism.

Other scholars have emphasized different connections. Harold Berman and Brian Tierney have argued that canon law, church–state pluralism, and Christian natural-law reasoning helped develop legal traditions of contract, property, corporate association, and limits on rulers. Joseph Schumpeter and Raymond de Roover highlighted the economic reasoning of medieval scholastics, while Rodney Stark emphasized Christian ideas about reason, human dignity, and vocation as cultural supports for commercial society.

These scholars are not making the same argument, but together they identify several possible connections between Christianity and the development of modern markets. There is a serious scholarly tradition connecting Christian ideals and institutions with markets, though it is by no means the scholarly consensus.

The Christian belief that every person possesses inherent dignity—that all people are, in principle, equal in ultimate worth before God—supplied moral resources that later liberal societies drew on heavily. That belief could support markets by treating ordinary people as responsible agents capable of owning property, making contracts, and directing their own lives.

Lest we adopt an overly simplistic account of the history, let us avoid two possible errors. First, we should not conclude that Christianity is wholly unrelated to the prosperity that followed in the 1800s. There is a serious scholarly tradition linking Christian ideals to markets, and I believe that account.

Second, we should not conclude that Christianity was the primary or sole undergirding requirement for prosperous markets. That would center Christianity to the exclusion of the many other contributions from other cultures, religions, and thinkers that deserve gratitude.

If Christianity is not the whole explanation, what is? A more defensible account, and a more sophisticated one, is aconfluence: Christian moral ideas, natural-rights theory, constitutional government, religious pluralism, the rise of empirical science, expanding commerce and cities, industrial technology, and hard-won resistance to arbitrary authority all ran together at the right time and in the right places.

Christianity’s relationship with markets is also complicated because it is both foundational to, and often critical of, markets. The same teachings about personal dignity and agency that can support voluntary exchange also challenge the notion that markets can provide meaning, protect dignity, or determine the value of human beings on their own.

From the preaching of Basil the Great and John Chrysostom, who condemned the hoarding of wealth while others lacked necessities, to John Wesley’s instruction to “gain all you can, save all you can, and give all you can,” Christian teachers have often treated wealth as morally dangerous when detached from responsibility. Pope Leo XIII later defended private property and economic initiative while condemning an industrial order that reduced workers to instruments of profit. Martin Luther King Jr. likewise argued that an economy should be judged not only by how much wealth it produces, but by whether it serves human dignity and meets the needs of the poor.

Leo XIII’sRerum Novarumand King’s “Notes on American Capitalism” illustrate this tradition: both accepted the importance of economic activity while insisting that markets must remain subordinate to human dignity, social responsibility, and the needs of the vulnerable.

Restorationist Economic Thought

Latter-day Saint thought offers a similar combination of economic freedom and moral restraint. Joseph Smith’s revelations affirmed work, agency, stewardship, and productive enterprise, but they also commanded the Saints to consecrate their resources and ensure that the poor were cared for.

The early Latter-day Saints experimented with what they called theUnited Orderor thelaw of consecration. This was not modern socialism. It did not abolish choice or place all economic decisions in the hands of the state. Instead, individuals consecrated their property to God, received stewardship over what they could productively manage, and accepted responsibility for the welfare of the community.

ProfileAn odd socialist?

Joseph Smith was once described as "an odd socialist: he seemed to love the poor without hating the rich." Joseph Smith’s economic vision was unusually egalitarian without being hostile to prosperity. He defended work, agency, property, and productive enterprise, but insisted that wealth carried obligations and that no Christian community could regard the suffering of the poor as someone else’s problem.

The Bourgeois Virtues

Deirdre McCloskey offers another way to understand the moral foundations of markets. In her account, markets do not function through incentives and institutions alone. They also require habits of honesty, trustworthiness, courage, prudence, and fairness—what she calls the bourgeois virtues.

A merchant who keeps promises, a lender who tells the truth, an employer who pays what was agreed, and a customer who does not cheat are not merely being nice. They are making exchange possible.

Markets can also reinforce these virtues. Repeated exchange gives people reasons to develop a reputation for honesty and reliability. Competition punishes some forms of deception, while long-term relationships reward trustworthiness. Markets therefore need integrity before they can function well, but they can also give people practical reasons to practice integrity.

This does not mean markets automatically make people virtuous. They can reward greed, manipulation, and predation when rules are weak or moral obligations are absent. McCloskey’s point is more modest and more useful: commercial society depends on virtues that neither prices nor laws can fully manufacture.

PerspectiveFreedom requires responsibility

Libertarian political philosophy concerns itself with freedom, especially freedom from government. At its heights, libertarian thought pushes us to count the costs of government enforcenment of various laws, even if the underlying behavior is truly damaging. For example, while cheating on a spouse may be immoral, asking government to adjudicate such disputes may not be worth the cost in resources and erosion of the rule of law. Similarly, Libertarians rightly emphasize that there is a moral shift in doing something that is required by law rather than out of one's own personal morality: helping the poor, volunteering for a good cause, or giving money to a charity. Libertarian thinkers argue that government should do as little as possible to maximize freedom, and often, to maximize personal morality.

The various opposing camps argue that markets devoid of personal responsibility are a departure from the historical provenance of markets in the first place. Markets depend on people being free to act, but also accountable for fraud, broken promises, and harm to others. On this view, economic freedom is not the absence of all obligations; it is freedom protected and structured by obligations.

At one extreme, libertarians argue that government interferes in the personal freedom, and sometimes personal morality, of its citizens. At the other extreme, what may in name be libertarian may in actuality be merely libertine: a desire to be free to act in ways that affect others without feeling the consequences of their actions.

A Larger Confluence

Christianity is not a sufficient explanation for markets, and prosperous markets are not sufficient for a vibrant Christianity. Christian ideas helped provide some of the moral and institutional foundations within which modern markets developed, but Christianity also supplied some of their most persistent critics.

The broader historical evidence suggests that capitalism arose from the interaction of religious beliefs, legal institutions, political structures, technology, and commercial practice. Christian thought played a role, as did Islamic, Jewish, and Enlightenment thought. So did French and American ideals about equality and political liberty, the rise of empirical science, and the legal and political stability that made long-term investment and cooperation possible.

To restate, economic liberalism—the case for open markets, secure property, and voluntary exchange—did not spring from a single cause. It emerged alongside a broader liberal order concerned with human dignity, legal equality, political liberty, and limits on arbitrary power.

The market was one expression of a larger conviction that ordinary people, not just rulers, could be trusted to run their own lives.

MisconceptionA "free market" is a market with no government or rules.▸

Free means freewithinrules, not freeofthem. Every market presupposes property, contracts, courts, and enforcement. Strip those away and you do not get a freer market — you get no market, just a contest over who can grab what and get away with it. The argument is never "rules versus freedom"; it is always "which rules."

Because a price presupposes that something can be owned, that non-payers can sometimes be excluded, that promises are enforceable, and that a court stands behind the deal — so the rules are not a limit added on top of the market; they are what make exchange possible in the first place.
Major concept 2

Property Rights

Behind every ordinary exchange sits a quiet stack of institutions the buyer never sees.

Aproperty rightis best understood as abundle— the right to use a resource, sell it, improve it, exclude others from it, transfer it, and seek compensation when it is damaged. Without a clear bundle, exchange turns fragile: investment becomes a gamble, bargaining becomes confused, and responsibility for harm becomes impossible to pin down. They answer questions that markets cannot answer by themselves:

Who may use this land? Who may sell it? Who may inherit it? Who may enter it? Who is responsible if it causes harm?

Property rights are therefore not merely about ownership. They are about legal recognition, authority, exclusion, transfer, and responsibility.

Where the line gets drawn — Friedman's airplane

The economist Milton Friedman liked to make an important point using an airplane: no one thinks a jet cruising at ten thousand feet trespasses on the house below, and everyone thinks a plane clipping the roof at ten feet does — yet nothing in nature marks where, in between, the right to the airspace ends. Somebody has to draw that line and enforce it. Property rights are not discovered lying in the world; they are defined by social rules. Setting property rights is one of the most important roles for government.

HistorySlavery and property rights

To give an example of just how critical property rights are, let us consider one of the most consequential property rights of all: the right to one's own person. A society must decide not only who can own property—men or women, Black people or white people, citizens or foreigners—but also whether human beings themselves can be treated as property.

Slavery was not simply a society without property rights. It was a society that gave some people property rights over other people. Law defined human beings as transferable assets, authorized their sale and inheritance, enforced claims over their labor, and denied them the ordinary rights of personhood.

The history of Christianity and slavery reveals both the power and the danger of moral traditions. Many abolitionists drew deeply on Christian convictions about human dignity, sin, brotherhood, and liberation. At the same time, many Christians defended slavery by appealing to scripture, tradition, racial hierarchy, or claims about social order. Christian faith did not automatically produce abolition, but it supplied arguments that could be—and eventually were—turned decisively against slavery.

The history is therefore neither a simple story of Christian triumph nor a reason to dismiss Christianity’s moral contribution. It is a story of a tradition whose principles were contested, compromised, and sometimes courageously applied.

But a right you cannot enforce is only a wish with your name on it. If a court will not stand behind yourcontract, the contract is decoration. If the rules change unpredictably, people cannot reliably invest; they are only betting on political outcomes. If fraud is tolerated, buyers stop trusting sellers, and trade thins out. This is whyrule of lawis so important: the background promise that the rules are known in advance and applied to everyone the same way. When we say "rule of law" we also meancourts,liabilityrules, and enforcement like police, all of which help decide who must answer when one person's actions damage another.

As we continue through this chapter, there is another idea that is worth clarifying at this point.A bad outcome can have two possible authors. Maybe themarketfailed. Or maybe therulesfailed — and the repair is a clearer property right, a working court, an honest registry, not necessarily more government. If you want markets to function, sometimes the best thing to do is look beyond the market to the rules underneath.

Can markets fail? Yes. There are occasions, as we will see in the subsequent chapters, when markets, left to themselves, do not create optimal outcomes. They tend to be cases in which the assumptions we make about markets do not hold: for example, competitive markets might fail when a single market player has significant market power.

But we are speaking here of another class of failure that also deserves attention: institutional failure. This is when the institutional rules upon which markets rely are not enforced or protected sufficiently for markets to flourish.

Institutions as technology

It helps to think of institutions less like fences and more like tools. A reliable court, a working land registry, a trusted payment system — each one lowers the cost and risk of exchange, the way a better machine lowers the cost of production. If a society can build or borrow rules that make investing and trading safer, those rules can generate wealth almost the way a new technology does. Closer to home, think of the fifty states as laboratories of democracy — each testing rules, watching what happens, and copying what works. Institutions can be learned. That does not guarantee the lessons are wise or just.

Big ideaCharter cities: a market for institutions?

The economist Paul Romer starts somewhere surprising: immigration. When a person moves from a poor country to a rich one, much of what changes for them is not the scenery — it is the rules. The same person, with the same skills, often becomes far more productive simply by stepping into a better set of institutions. So a large share of the gain from migration is really a gain from switching rule-sets.

That reframes the problem. If the value lives in the rules, perhaps we do not have to move every person to the institutions — perhaps we can bring the institutions to the people. Romer's charter city is exactly that: a new jurisdiction that adopts a trusted package of rules — property, courts, contract enforcement — and invites people to live and invest under them.

Push the idea one step further and you get something striking: a market for institutions. Just as firms compete to offer better goods, jurisdictions could compete to offer better rules, with people and capital voting with their feet toward the places that govern well. Keep it honest — charter cities are contested and rarely tried, and who writes the charter is a hard question. But as a thought experiment it crystallizes the chapter's thesis: institutions are not the background to the market. They are one of its products.

Major concept 3

Corruption

When the rules are captured

Good rules make markets flourish. What do bad rules do?

The economists Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson draw a line between two kinds of rules.Inclusive institutionsspread property rights, impartial enforcement, and open entry across a whole society.Extractive institutionsconcentrate those same things in the hands of insiders — protected monopolies, captured courts, ownership reserved for the connected.

The unsettling part is that a market on extractive institutions stillruns. Prices clear. Trades happen. On the surface it looks like any other market. But it is quietly transmitting the rigging beneath it — funneling value toward whoever wrote the rules — in prices that read as perfectly neutral. As a simple explanation, think of it this way: institutions always work. The question is who they workfor.And typically, the answer is "institutions work for those who built them."

In other words, corruption.

Across countries, higher corruption travels with lower income, lower investment, weaker firm growth, and slower long-run development. An older theory held that corruption might "grease the wheels," a bribe helping a business slip past a slow bureaucracy. Most development economists now think the opposite is closer to the truth: corruptionsandsthe wheels. It makes rules unpredictable, raisestransaction costs, and rewards connections over value. Worse, it is self-feeding: an official who can demand a bribe to remove an obstacle has every reason to invent the obstacle in the first place.

Read that pattern carefully. It is acorrelation, not a clean proof that corruption alone causes poverty. Poor countries may have weak institutions partlybecausethey are poor, and both corruption and poverty may trace back to deeper causes — conflict, instability, a broken rule of law. But the association is large, persistent, and consistent with a clear mechanism, and the direction of the modern consensus is not in doubt: corruption is a drag, not a lubricant.

So the useful question is not the hopeless "how do we make people honest?" It is institutional: how do we make corruptionharder to hide, easier to detect, and costlier to attempt? Open records and transparent budgets, competitive public contracting, independent audits, a free press, impartial courts. But transparency alone is not enough — and this is the part people skip. Information only bites when someone can act on it and a court can impose a real cost. A leaked ledger changes nothing if no one is empowered to prosecute. That is the same lesson as Concept 2 wearing new clothes: a rule is only as strong as the enforcement behind it.

The deepest point is what corruption does to competition itself. When rules are for sale, people stop competing to create value and start competing for influence — for licenses, contracts, and privileges. Economists call thatrent-seeking, and it is the mirror image of the market's best promise. Strong institutions tilt the field back, so that reward flows toward serving people rather than capturing rules.

Major concept 4

Institutions Lower Transaction Costs

Clear property rights don't just protect a thing. They open a place to bargain.

Corruption is just one example of something calledtransaction costs.

When you buy a new car, you pay the purchase price; but you also spend time searching listings, trying out the car, negotiating, and arranging financing. When you hire a babysitter, you pay the hourly rate; but you also have to find a reliable and trustworthy sitter, negotiate pay, and communicate expectations. When you buy a house, you literally hire someone to handle many transactions costs for you: a realtor who finds a seller for you, helps you with inspections, appraisals, title searches, attorneys, paperwork, and months of negotiation. These are all examples of transaction costs: the tangential costs that are not the direct price, but the affiliated activities required for purchase.

Mike Munger has noted that some prominent companies are not selling what you think; they are simply selling lower transaction costs. Consider Uber. While taxi services already exist, Uber makes it dramatically easier to find a driver, compare prices, request a ride, track its arrival, pay electronically, and evaluate the driver afterward. The ride is the obvious product, but much of Uber’s value comes from reducing the hassle and uncertainty of arranging it.

Corruption is when institutions extract wealth for the benefit of those who have captured the institution. Effective institutions do the reverse: they pass along wealth via lowered transaction costs.

That is the intuition behind theCoase theorem: when rights are clear andtransaction costsare low, private bargaining can sometimes move a resource to its best use — even when one person's activity spills onto another. In the next couple of chapters, we'll talk about spillover effects, or externalities: when your actions can affect others, or their actions can affect you. Remember this: sometimes, if you can eliminate transactions costs, you can allow people to solve their own problems.

Consider two neighbors. One likes to play loud music. The other wants peace and quiet. One solution is for the government to institute a noise ordinance. Another one, however, is for the two neighbors to talk out the solution to the problem: "what if I pay you $20 to limit the music to before 8 pm?" Both people are made better off, and there is no need for intervention. Coase says that this is sometimes possible, so long as transaction costs are low.

The word doing all the work issometimes. Two neighbors arguing over one tree branch can strike a deal by dinner: the parties are few, the harm is easy to see, the right can be clarified, and a handshake can be enforced. But if we scale it up, things can become far more difficult very quickly. A thousand drivers pollute one city's air. Who negotiates with whom? Who measures which car caused whose asthma? Who stops the one holdout who refuses to sign? The bargain that was trivial for the tree branch becomes impossible for the city — not because the harm is smaller, but because thetransaction costsexploded.

Private solutionsWhen can a Coase-style bargain actually work?
  • Clear rights:the parties know who is allowed to do what, and who can ask for compensation.
  • Few parties:two neighbors can deal; a million strangers breathing the same air cannot.
  • Measurable harm:you can show what was damaged and by how much.
  • Low transaction costs:the parties can find each other, negotiate cheaply, and enforce the result.

This is why Coase belongs in a chapter about institutions, not as a stray trick later. It names the cases where the repair is aclearer right, not a bigger program — and, just as usefully, the cases where private bargaining has no chance. Chapters 18 and 19 come back through this same door from the other side: there, a Coase bargain sits on a menu next to taxes and permits as one possible remedy for a spillover. Here, it is simply the answer to "what does a property right actually give you?"

MisconceptionThe Coase theorem proves externalities solve themselves, so government is unnecessary.▸

Coase says bargainingcanfix a spillover when rights are clear and transaction costs are low — not that it will. Those conditions fail exactly where harm is spread across thousands of strangers who cannot find each other, measure the damage, or stop holdouts. Coase names the cases where private deals work; it never promises they always do.

Quick checkA factory's runoff harms one fishery just downstream, and the damage is easy to measure. Compared with citywide smog, why is this a better candidate for a private bargain?▸
Few parties (one factory, one fishery), a clear and measurable harm, a right that can be assigned, and low enough transaction costs to negotiate and enforce a deal. Citywide smog has many sources, many victims, hard-to-trace harm, and enormous transaction costs.
Major concept 5

Public Goods and Common Resources

"Public good" is a technical category, not a fancy label for "thing I like."

Another limitation of markets is that they are effective with certain classes of goods, and less effective with others. Economists sort goods with two questions. First,excludability: can non-payers be kept out? A theater checks tickets; a login gates a stream; a grocer keeps the sandwich behind the counter. But some goods are not easy to exclude: groundwater, national defense, or fireworks.

Second,rivalry: does one person's use subtract from another's? A sandwich is rival — if you eat it, I can't. A national-defense system is non-rival — protecting you doesn't use up protection for me.

Cross those two questions and you get four kinds of good. This is one of the highest-yield tools in the unit, so it is worth your time to learn to place a good on it quickly.

Case fileCalifornia’s paper rivers

Over a century, California handed out legal rights to water without a price and without a shared ledger — and the claims stopped adding up. Researchers found the state has granted rights to about five times more surface water than its rivers actually carry: roughly 370 million acre-feet of paper rights against the ~70 million that flows in an average year. On the San Joaquin River, the rights on the books total nearly nine times the water that exists.

The result isn’t just untidy. In a drought, regulators genuinely cannot tell whose supply to cut, because far more water is “owned” than nature delivers — while “use it or lose it” rules quietly reward flooding a field over conserving. This is what allocating a scarce thing by rules alone, with no working market, looks like at the limit: a ledger of claims to a river that was never there.

Source: Grantham & Viers, “100 years of California’s water rights system,” Environmental Research Letters (2014); UC Davis Center for Watershed Sciences.

In the next chapter, we will detail potential responses to various goods. For now, your job is to understand what they are and how they are defined. I introduce this topic in this chapter for a reason, however:for a common resource, the tool may be tocreatea market where none existed. Give water a clear, tradable right and a price and the person who values it most can buy it from the person who values it least — turning invisible scarcity into a signal. That only works when the right is clear, use can be measured, and third parties are protected; but it shows that "another tool" doesn't always mean "less market."

Education is usually not a public good

Education may be goodforthe public, but it is usually not apublic goodin the technical sense. Schools can exclude non-enrolled students, and seats, teacher attention, and resources are partly rival. Education can still deserve public support — for its positive spillovers, for equity, for productivity — without being a public good. The classification asksonlytwo questions, and "it's socially valuable" is not one of them.

Major concept 6

The Rights You Do Not Have

A market order depends not only on the rights people hold, but on the ones we do not have.

Contemporary American culture really loves to talk about our rights. Just as important, are the rights we do not have. Markets need peopleto nothave certain rights. A functioning order requires limits on the claims strangers can press against your legitimate use of your own things.

Traditionally, wedding ceremonies have been portrayed as asking the guests to "speak now, or forever hold your peace" if anyone objects to the wedding. This invitaion to object is an excellent example of a right that you need to not have. Even in wedding ceremonies, objection does not hand any objector the power to stop the marriage merely because they disapprove. You may dislike the match. You do not thereby acquire a veto.

To see why this is so important, consider a world in which everyone gets to vote on every decision you make. You want to paint your nails? A community council gets to vote on it. You want to let your lawn grow another day? You have to get it approved. This isn't just an issue of efficiency. It isn't that the town council will take up too much time to decide. It's thatit's simply none of their business.

Ownership works the same way. An owner may generally use property without collecting permission from everyone who dislikes the choice. The neighbor who hates your paint color, the resident who preferred the empty lot, the group that wishes the old building had stayed — each may object, and none of that objection, by itself, becomes a right to prevent.

In other words, you have every right to say"I dislike this project"but you have no right to interpret that as"I am entitled to prevent this project."This is a jurisdictional rule: you get to decide what you do in your own sphere: yourself, your family, and your property.

Are there cases when what you do affects others? Yes. We will devote the next two chapters to considering cases of externalities for that exact reason. But the key insight here is somewhat different: a market cannot exist unless we all agree to define "harm" pretty narrowly. That means that I let my neighbor paint her house, and she lets me add a ball room to my basement.

If this seems silly, it's not. In fact, it may be among the most important contemporary economic fights that you can find on the news today: housing crises. The short version is this: the laws of supply and demand apply to housing stock. So, if there are very high housing prices, the simple way to fix it is to simply build more housing—and, importantly, at higher density.

But those who own homes tend not to want more people in their neighborhood: increased traffic, new neighbors, and projections of potential new riff-raff all rear their heads in zoning meetings throughout the country, and many new housing development projects die. This is known by the term NIMBYism: "Not In My BackYard!" The same is true of historic preservation: a 1930s building that is an eyesore needs to be demolished, but a single community member can hold up the project if they make a strong appeal to the city council on the basis of historical value.

Case fileThe “historic” laundromat

In San Francisco’s Mission District, an owner proposed replacing a single-story laundromat and its parking lot with an eight-story building holding 75 homes. Opponents petitioned to have the laundromat declared a historic landmark. The city’s own review found it wasn’t — it “lacks sufficient integrity to convey its identified historic significance.”

So the objection changed weapons. Even after the historic claim collapsed, the Board of Supervisors delayed the project again — to study the shadows the new building might cast. The owner sued for abuse of discretion, seeking $17 million. The saga ran about eight years; the exhausted owner finally sold, and the lot was cleared in 2022.

The deeper worry — displacement in a gentrifying neighborhood — was real. But “this might change my neighborhood” was converted into a chain of vetoes over someone else’s lot, and the housing shortage those vetoes protect is a big part of why the neighborhood became unaffordable in the first place.

Source: San Francisco Chronicle; The Real Deal; SF Planning Dept. Historic Resource Evaluation (2018–2022).

Again, some objections are attempts to convert a preference into authority over someone else's land. Others are something entirely different: genuine external harms. Pollution, unsafe construction, fraud, unreasonable noise that spills past the property line — these impose real, uncompensated costs on others, and society has every reason to step in. Ownership was never a license to do anything.

So the institutional question is not "may owners do as they please?" It is sharper:what counts as an overriding reason to interfere with an owner's use?Good institutions work hard to tell a genuine harm apart from a dressed-up veto — because the first deserves a remedy and the second, if honored, quietly dismantles everyone's ability to use anything. And notice where that question points. "What is a real harm, and who should bear its cost?" is the doorway into externalities, the subject waiting in Chapters 18 and 19. Preferences, objections, and interests are the ordinary stuff of a free society. The discipline is in refusing to let them harden, unearned, into rights.

A healthy market order depends not only on protecting the rights people have, but also on refusing to invent rights they do not have.

On the exam

Diagnose the good, then diagnose the failure

The moves you can now make: classify a good by excludability and rivalry, separate a true public good from a good that is merely valuable, and tell a market failure apart from an institutional failure.

Classification

A crowded, unpriced city road is difficult to keep non-payers off, and each extra driver slows everyone else down. Place it on the four-goods grid, and name the coordination problem its cell is prone to.

Reveal answer▸

Non-excludable and rival → acommon resource. Its characteristic problem is overuse (here, congestion). How to actually fix that — pricing, quotas, access rules — is Chapter 17's job; classification is yours.

Technical definition

A city subsidizes seats at a public university and calls them a "public good." Using the two classification questions, explain why that label is technically wrong — and why the seats might still deserve subsidy.

Reveal answer▸

University seats areexcludable(admissions and tuition keep non-payers out) andrival(a filled seat and a professor's attention are finite). Both classification questions fail, so it is not a public good — the government provider is irrelevant to the category. It can still merit subsidy for positive spillovers, equity, or productivity; "worth funding" and "public good" are different claims.

Identify and explain

In one country, a promising firm can't raise money because courts won't reliably enforce loan contracts. In another, a factory's smoke damages a laundry next door and no one is liable. For each, say whether the better diagnosis is "the market failed" or "the rules failed," and name the institution at stake.

Reveal answer▸

Both are closer toinstitutionalfailures than raw market failures. The first is broken contract enforcement / rule of law — fix the courts, not the credit market. The second is a missing liability rule (an unpriced externality) — assign the right and a remedy may follow, sometimes by private bargaining. In each case the repair is a clearer or better-enforced rule.

The vacant lot

Suggested time: 8 minutes · 5 points · no graph required

A developer owns a vacant lot in a residential neighborhood and obtains permits to build a small apartment building. Neighbors object. Several say the building will change the neighborhood's character and block their view of the open lot. One neighbor, who runs a licensed daycare next door, says months of construction dust and truck noise will make her home unusable for the children. A neighborhood group petitions the city to halt the project.

  1. 1 ptIdentify the property right the developer is exercising, and one institution from this chapter the project relies on to proceed at all.
  2. 1 ptClassify "an unobstructed view of the empty lot" using excludability and rivalry, and state whether a private market would reliably provide it.
  3. 1 ptUsing two specific objections from the scenario, distinguish a genuine external harm from an attempt to acquire veto power.
  4. 1 ptThe daycare owner and the developer could, in principle, bargain over the construction-noise harm. State two conditions under which a Coase-style private bargain is likely to succeed here.
  5. 1 ptExplain why "I dislike this project" does not by itself establish a right to stop it, connecting your answer to the idea that a market order depends on limiting rights people do not have.
Reveal scoring guide▸
  • 1 ptNames the right to use / build on owned property (part of the property-rights bundle), plus one supporting institution — e.g. rule of law, courts, enforceable permits, or clear title.
  • 1 ptClassifies the view as effectively non-excludable and non-rival (public-good-like), so a private market will not reliably supply or price it — there is no ordinary way to sell "the view" to the neighbors.
  • 1 ptGenuine harm: the daycare's dust/noise imposes a real, measurable cost across the property line. Veto-seeking: "blocks my view" or "changes the character" converts a preference into a claim to control someone else's land.
  • 1 ptAny two of: few parties (two neighbors), clearly defined rights, measurable harm, low transaction costs / enforceable agreement.
  • 1 ptExplains that objections and preferences do not automatically become rights; if every dislike were a veto, no one could reliably use property, so a functioning order must refuse to invent rights people do not have (while still remedying genuine harms).
Reveal common mistakes▸
  • ConceptTreating a blocked view or "neighborhood character" as a compensable external harm. A lost preference is not the same as an imposed cost.
  • ConceptCalling the apartment building itself a public good. It is an ordinary private good; theviewis the public-good-like part.
  • ConceptAssuming any objection equals a right. The chapter's whole point is that it does not.
  • CraftPrescribing a specific remedy (a tax, a ban) when the question only asks you to diagnose. Answer the question asked; the remedies are Chapters 18–20.
Market thesis

A market is a product of its rules, not the absence of them — so getting the institutions right is not a limit on the market. It is what lets the market work at all.

If economists ruled the world…

they would pour effort into the plumbing — clear property rights, honest courts, low transaction costs, uncaptured rules — because get that right and self-interest quietly serves strangers. The wealth of nations turns out to be, in large part, therulesof nations.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Markets require property rights, contract enforcement, and rule of law before exchange works at all.
  • When rights are clear and transaction costs low, people can sometimes fix a spillover themselves — no program required.
  • Corruption is a drag on development, not a lubricant; the pattern is strong and consistent.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • When something goes wrong, is it the market that failed, or the rules? The diagnosis decides the cure.
  • When is the fix a clearer property right, and when is it a public program?
  • How does a society help people do what is right — not only what they are paid to do?
Takeaways
In your life

When something feels like "just how the market works," ask who wrote the rules that made it work that way — and whether the fix is a better rule.

In your work

Your business runs on borrowed institutions — courts, contracts, clear title. They feel free until they fail; know what you are standing on.
