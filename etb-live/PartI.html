Before We Begin

Ah, the quotidian miracle of the pencil!

In 1958, Leonard Read let a pencil tell its own story. Take one in your hand, and feel the awe that any student of economics shares. See why Read bothered. 

The cedar was logged in the Pacific Northwest; the graphite came from a mine an ocean away, ground with clay and fired in a kiln; the brass band, the yellow lacquer, the pumice and oil in the little eraser — each arrived from a different corner of the world, shaped by thousands of hands that never met and never will. 

No one drew up the plan. No one is in charge. And yet the pencil sits on your desk, costing almost nothing, and you never gave it a thought.

That was Read’s point, and it is a strange one to sit with: not a single person on earth knows how to make a pencil. Not the logger, not the chemist who mixes the graphite, not the head of the pencil company. The knowledge is scattered across millions of minds, and no one mind holds even a sliver of the whole. There is no pencil czar. There is no committee. And still the shelves are full.

So how does a thing get made that no one knows how to make? 

One trade at a time. 

Every link in that chain is a voluntary exchange, and a voluntary exchange only happens when both sides expect to come out ahead — the logger and the mill, the mill and the factory, the factory and the store, the store and you. 

Economists have a precise name for a deal that leaves someone better off and no one worse: a Pareto improvement. Most people just call it a win-win. The pencil is a monument built entirely out of them — millions of tiny win-wins struck between strangers, each guided by nothing louder than a price, a signal wrapped in an incentive that quietly tells every one of them where they are needed. 

The first half of this book explores this simple insight and watches it grow into a rich set of beliefs about how markets work, what underpins them, and how they help us flourish. This is the astonishing thing the first half of this book is about: the world is made a little richer, one trade at a time. 

And when we do that at scale, nations thrive, people flourish, and individuals are more capable than in any other environment to reach their full potential. 

The promise

For the first half of this book, we take markets seriously — we look at markets as one of the most remarkable blessings people have ever stumbled into — and we hand you the evidence to evaluate for yourself. In the second half, we will give you the same kind of evidence to evaluate on whether markets deserve their criqitues. 

The harder questions keep for later. First, the wonder.

Leonard E. Read, “I, Pencil: My Family Tree as Told to Leonard E. Read,” The Freeman, December 1958.
