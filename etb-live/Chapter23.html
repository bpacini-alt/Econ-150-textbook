Unit 4 · When Competition Breaks Down

Imperfect Competition I:
Monopolistic Competition

A little power, competed away

Chapter 23~ 46 min read

In 2017, Sawyer Hemsley was a student at Utah State University when he and his cousin Jason McGowan opened a cookie shop in Logan, Utah. They sold one thing: a warm, oversized milk chocolate chip cookie. People liked it. They added a pink sugar cookie, then a menu that changed every week, with each new lineup revealed online like a product drop.

Crumbl wasn't selling something nobody else sold. Every grocery store in the country sells cookies. What Crumbl sold was its cookies: the pink box, the open kitchen where you could watch them bake, the weekly flavors fans argued about on TikTok. No one else could sell you that. In a small way, Crumbl had a monopoly.

The profits drew a crowd. Crumbl grew from one shop to about 1,100 in less than eight years. Other cookie shops opened with big cookies and rotating menus of their own. In 2022 Crumbl sued two of them, Crave and Dirty Dough, saying their packaging and branding were “confusingly similar.” Both denied it, and Dirty Dough's founder said the publicity doubled his sales.

Then the numbers turned. From 2022 to 2023, the average Crumbl store's sales fell 37 percent, and its profit fell by more than half. Crumbl's CEO blamed part of the drop on crowding: towns that once had one Crumbl now had two or three. Fading novelty and tighter budgets played a part too. Many of the customers each new store won were customers another store lost, and often that store was another Crumbl. Stores began to close, and at least 57 had closed by October 2026.

So Crumbl was different, and people loved it, and the profit still didn't last. This chapter explains how both can be true. It's about markets with many sellers, each a little different from the rest, where anyone is free to open next door. Economists call it monopolistic competition.

The big picture
Product differentiation gives each firm a little pricing power: a downward-sloping demand curve of its own. Free entry then competes the profit away, until firms break even while still charging above marginal cost and running below full capacity. Measured against perfect competition, that looks inefficient. The markup also pays for something perfect competition can't offer, which is variety. Whether that trade is worth it is a question the model can't answer for us.

Major concept 1

Monopolistic Competition

Many Firms, but None Quite the Same

Monopolistic competition is the structure that borrows from both sides of the book. From perfect competition it takes many firms and free entry and exit: nobody needs permission to open a cafe, a salon, a food truck, or an app. Most of the businesses you walk past fit this pattern: the coffee shops, burger places, and dirty-soda stands near campus, hair salons, clothing brands, dentists, and cookie shops like Crumbl. From monopoly it takes product differentiation: the products are close substitutes but not identical, so each firm has a few customers who prefer it specifically.

Differentiation is what separates this structure from perfect competition. In perfect competition every firm sells the same thing, so no one can charge a cent above the going price without losing every customer. Here, because your cafe is not quite the same as the one next door, you can raise your price a little and keep most of your regulars. You face your own downward-sloping demand curve. You are, in a small way, a price searcher rather than a price taker.

Key aphorism

… each seller has an absolute monopoly of his own product, but is subject to the competition of more or less imperfect substitutes.

Edward H. Chamberlin, The Theory of Monopolistic Competition, p. 9 (excerpt). A café controls its own distinctive offering; nearby substitutes limit what it can charge.

Learn more

Chamberlin introduces this passage by describing product differentiation. Control over one distinctive product can coexist with competition from other sellers. Your café may be the only source of its particular roast, room, and atmosphere while other cafés compete for the same customers. The strength of those substitutes limits the pricing power that differentiation provides. Read the passage in the original book.

Major concept 2

Differentiated Firms in the Short Run

A Sliver of Pricing Power

Because each firm has its own downward-sloping demand curve, it has its own marginal-revenue curve lying below that demand — the same logic as a monopolist, for the same reason: to sell one more cup you shave the price a little. So the firm follows the familiar rule, producing where MR = MC and reading its price up on the demand curve. If demand is strong relative to cost, the firm can earn short-run economic profit, the shaded rectangle below.

This is the “monopolistic” half of the name. For a while, a well-loved cafe can charge more than a cup costs to make and keep the difference. What separates it from a true monopoly is that nothing stops anyone else from opening a cafe too. The next concept follows what happens when they do.

Fig 23.1Short run. The firm produces where MR = MC, prices up on its demand curve at P*, and because P* exceeds ATC, earns the green profit rectangle. Its demand is more elastic (flatter) than a monopolist's, because close substitutes sit right next door.
When demand is weak: a short-run loss

Not every new cafe gets lucky. If demand is weak, the firm still produces where MR = MC and reads its price off demand, but now ATC sits above the price. The same rectangle between P* and ATC, out to Q*, is a loss instead of a profit (Fig 23.2).

Should it close? In the short run, use the rule from Chapter 10. Rent and other fixed costs are owed whether the doors are open or not, so the firm compares its price with average variable cost (AVC). If price covers AVC, revenue pays the wages and supplies and chips away at the rent, so staying open loses less than closing. If price falls below AVC, the firm should shut down for now.

In the long run, losses work like profit in reverse. Some firms leave, their customers spread to the firms that remain, and each survivor's demand shifts right until P = ATC again.

Fig 23.2Short run with weak demand. The firm still produces where MR = MC and prices on its demand curve at P*, but ATC sits above P*, so the red rectangle is a loss. Price still covers AVC, so staying open loses less than shutting down for now.

Competing on more than price

A firm with its own downward-sloping demand curve can do more than pick a price. It can try to move the demand curve itself. Economists call this nonprice competition: better quality, a handier location, faster service, a menu that changes every week, and above all advertising and branding.

Advertising tries to do two things to the firm's demand curve. It tries to shift the curve right, so more people want the product at every price. And it tries to make demand less elastic, so customers stay when the price goes up because, to them, nothing else is quite the same. Crumbl's weekly flavor reveals on TikTok did both. They brought people in to try the new flavors, and they built fans who would pay more for a pink box they couldn't get anywhere else.

Advertising isn't free. It adds to the firm's costs, which pushes its ATC curve up. Economists also disagree about how much it helps buyers. Some ads carry real information about what's for sale, where, and at what price. Others mostly try to tilt buyers toward one brand without telling them much. Either way, advertising can't stop entry. If a campaign earns a profit, that profit draws rivals, and the rivals advertise too.

Quick checkA cafe runs an ad campaign that convinces its regulars nothing else tastes the same. What happens to its demand curve, and to its costs?▸
Demand shifts right (more buyers want it at each price) and becomes less elastic (regulars are less willing to switch when the price rises). The campaign also raises costs, so ATC shifts up. If it still earns a profit, new rivals will chip that profit away in the long run.
Retrieval practiceExplain in your own words the two things advertising tries to do to a firm's demand curve.▸

It tries to shift demand to the right, so more people want the product at every price. It also tries to make demand less elastic, so customers keep buying when the price goes up because they don't see a close substitute.

Quick checkWhy does a monopolistically competitive firm's marginal revenue lie below its price, just like a monopolist's?▸
Because it faces a downward-sloping demand curve. To sell one more unit it must lower the price, and the lower price applies to all units, so the revenue from one more unit is less than its price. Differentiation, not size, is what gives it a downward-sloping demand curve.

Major concept 3

Differentiated Firms in the Long Run

Entry Competes the Profit Away

Short-run profit is a signal, and in a market with free entry the signal is answered. New cafes open. Every entrant pulls a few customers away from each existing firm, so each firm's demand curve drifts leftward and grows more elastic — with more substitutes nearby, your regulars have more places to defect to. Entry continues as long as profit is positive, and it stops only when profit reaches zero.

You can watch this happen in town after town. Frozen yogurt took off after the first Pinkberry opened in 2005, and shops rushed in until there were too many of them. They took customers from one another, and many closed. Gourmet cupcakes followed a similar path: Crumbs Bake Shop went public in 2011 at the height of the cupcake boom and closed every store in 2014. Crumbl's crowded towns are the latest version of the same story.

That resting point has a precise geometry. Economic profit is zero exactly when price equals average total cost — when the firm's demand curve has slid down until it is just tangent to its ATC curve. The firm still produces where MR = MC, and at that quantity the demand curve touches ATC at a single point: P = ATC, no profit, no loss. This is the long-run equilibrium, and it is the chapter's central graph.

Fig 23.3Long run. Entry slides demand left until it is tangent to ATC at Q*, so P = ATC and profit is zero. But notice two things at that point: P still sits above MC, and Q* lies to the left of minimum ATC — the gap to Qmin is excess capacity.
AP watch: long-run tangency gives zero profit (P = ATC), and P > MC, and output below minimum-ATC (excess capacity), all at once.
Retrieval practiceWalk the chain: short-run profit → what happens → long-run resting point.▸

Major concept 4

Efficiency and Product Variety

The tradeoff: Inefficiency may buy variety

Compared with perfect competition, the long-run equilibrium falls short in two ways. First, P > MC: there are customers who value a cup above what it costs to make, but who won't pay the marked-up price, so some mutually beneficial trades don't happen — the allocative inefficiency we first met under monopoly, in miniature. Second, the firm produces at excess capacity: output sits left of minimum ATC, so each firm is smaller and pricier per unit than it could be. A single giant coffee factory could brew each cup more cheaply.

But that single giant factory would serve one kind of coffee. The markup and the excess capacity are the price of having a dozen different cafes instead of one efficient monolith — the price of variety. The competitive benchmark counts the lost trades and the wasted capacity, but it puts no value on the fact that you can choose the roast, the room, and the vibe you actually want. Much of what firms do to stand out is something buyers want and are willing to pay for.

Key aphorism

There is no such thing as a commodity. All goods and services are differentiable.

Theodore Levitt, “Marketing Success Through Differentiation—of Anything,” Harvard Business Review (January 1980). A café can sell similar coffee as a different experience through its location, service, and atmosphere.

Learn more

The chapter treats the product as the whole offering: coffee, location, service, atmosphere, and reputation. Those differences matter when buyers value them. Perfect competition remains a useful benchmark where buyers treat many sellers’ offerings as interchangeable. Where buyers care about the differences, firms can face downward-sloping demand curves. That pricing power can survive even as free entry competes economic profit toward zero. Read Levitt’s article.

So is monopolistic competition inefficient, or is it an efficient way to deliver variety? Economists can measure the markup and the excess capacity. They can't tell you how much variety is worth to you. That part is a value judgment, so this chapter leaves the question open.

Misconception“Excess capacity and P > MC prove monopolistic competition is wasteful and should be fixed.”▸

Both are real, and both are measured against a benchmark — perfect competition — that assumes the product is identical. The moment you value variety, the “waste” is partly the cost of choice. There's no free lunch where you keep the variety and erase the markup; the two come together. Calling it simply wasteful imports a judgment the economics doesn't make.

Retrieval practiceWhat is the word for producing less than the output that would minimize average total cost?▸

Excess capacity. In long-run equilibrium, a monopolistically competitive firm produces to the left of minimum ATC.

AP Micro practice

The moves you can now make

The long-run tangency graph and the three results that hold at it — zero profit, P > MC, excess capacity — are core AP. Try each before revealing.

Identify

A market has 200 firms, free entry, and products that are similar but not identical. Name the structure, and the one feature that distinguishes it from perfect competition.

Reveal answer▸

Monopolistic competition. The distinguishing feature is product differentiation, which gives each firm a downward-sloping demand curve instead of the horizontal one a perfect competitor faces.

Graph reasoning

In long-run equilibrium, what is the relationship between the demand curve and the ATC curve, and what does it imply about economic profit?

Reveal answer▸

Demand is tangent to ATC at the profit-maximizing quantity, so P = ATC. Economic profit is therefore zero — the firm earns only a normal profit. Entry competed away anything more.

Evaluation

Give one sense in which long-run monopolistic competition is inefficient, and one reason that inefficiency might be worth accepting.

Reveal answer▸

Inefficient: P > MC (some valued trades don't happen) and output is below minimum ATC (excess capacity). Worth accepting: the markup and excess capacity are the cost of product variety, which consumers value and which a single efficient producer could not provide.

Market thesis

Differentiation gives each firm a little pricing power, and free entry competes the profit away. What remains is variety, paid for with a markup and excess capacity. Whether that is a good trade is a judgment the model leaves to you.

If economists ruled the world…

they would measure the markup and the excess capacity to the penny — and then admit that the number they can't compute, how much the variety is worth to you, is the one that decides the case.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Differentiation gives each firm a downward-sloping demand curve and a short-run chance at profit.
  • Free entry competes that profit to zero: long-run demand is tangent to ATC, so P = ATC.
  • At that point P > MC and output is below minimum ATC — a markup and excess capacity remain.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Is the lost efficiency worth the variety it buys?
  • Is perfect competition even the right yardstick for a market built on difference?
  • Can there be too much variety — choice that costs more than it's worth?
Takeaways
In your life

When you pay a premium for the brand, the room, or the vibe over a near-identical cheaper option, you're buying variety, not being fooled. Just know that's the trade you're making, and decide when it's worth it.

In your work

You can earn a margin by being different, but free entry erodes it. The real question for any differentiated business is whether your difference is one rivals can copy by next year — or one they can't.

Monopolistic competition had so many firms that none had to watch any other. The last structure does the reverse: strip the market down to a few large rivals, each big enough that its every move lands on the others. Then the question stops being “what does the graph predict?” and becomes “what will they do if I move first?” That is the world of strategy: oligopoly.
