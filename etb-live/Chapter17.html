Market Failures I: Externalities

The hidden third party

Chapter 17~ 55 min read

The case for markets rests on a quiet assumption: the people who make a deal are the people who live with its results. The buyer pays, the seller delivers, and the price carries the information both of them need. This half of the course looks at what happens when that assumption breaks. The most common way it breaks is the subject of this chapter.

A student buys gasoline, a cheap burger, a flight, or a fast-fashion hoodie. The buyer says yes. The seller says yes. Money changes hands. In the clean market story, that voluntary exchange looks like a win.

But sometimes a third person is quietly pulled into the deal. Someone breathes the pollution, sits in the congestion, hears the airport noise, absorbs the carbon risk, or lives downstream from the water damage. The buyer and seller both said yes. But did everyone affected by the trade get a say?

Economists call that spillover an externality. Nothing is wrong with the buyer and seller agreeing. What goes wrong is that someone affected by the deal never got a vote.

The big picture
Externalities are a major market failure: sometimes buyers and sellers make a voluntary exchange that creates costs or benefits for people outside the transaction. If third-party harm is not reflected in the price, markets may produce too much of the harmful activity. If third-party benefit is not reflected in the reward, markets may produce too little of the beneficial activity.

Major concept 1

Externalities

The Third Party

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on someone outside the transaction. In the simplest market story, the buyer receives the benefit, the seller receives the payment, and the price coordinates the exchange. Externalities complicate that story because the price may leave out someone affected by the decision.

Voluntary exchange often looks like a Pareto improvement: the buyer buys because the good is worth more than the price, and the seller sells because the price is worth more than the cost. Both parties say yes. But if a third party is harmed, the trade is no longer a clean Pareto improvement. Someone outside the deal may be worse off.

Externalities can be negative or positive, and they can come from production or consumption. A factory polluting while producing is a negative production externality. Secondhand smoke from consumption is a negative consumption externality. A flu shot can create a positive consumption externality by reducing spread. Research can create a positive production externality when knowledge spills over to other firms.

TypeWho creates it?ExampleLikely result
Negative productionProducerFactory pollutionToo much output
Negative consumptionConsumerSecondhand smokeToo much consumption

MisconceptionExternalities are always negative — they're just a word for pollution.▸

Positive externalities are just as real and just as common: vaccines, education, research, a well-kept garden on a shared street. With these, the market produces too little, not too much, because the decision-maker does not capture the full benefit. And the fix flips too — a positive externality calls for encouragement (a subsidy), not punishment (a tax). Half of this topic is about doing more of a good thing, not less of a bad one.

MisconceptionAn externality means someone broke a rule or cheated.▸

No one cheated. The buyer and seller made a legal, voluntary trade and both came out ahead. The problem is structural, not moral: a third party who bore part of the cost was never at the table to say no. That is exactly why you usually cannot fix an externality by punishing wrongdoing — there often isn't any. The repair is to put the missing cost or benefit into the price, not to find a villain.

Quick checkA commuter drives downtown and adds congestion for everyone behind them. What kind of externality is this?▸
A negative consumption externality: the consumer's action imposes a cost on others.

Major concept 2

Private Cost and Social Cost

The Price Leaves Out the Third Party

Think of supply and demand as information. The demand curve reports how much buyers value each unit. The supply curve reports what each unit costs to make. Markets do well when those reports are true. An externality means one of them leaves something out: the curves show what the decision-makers feel, not everything society gains or loses. So this chapter asks one question: what if supply and demand don’t reflect reality?

The key distinction is private versus social. Marginal private cost is the cost faced by the person or firm making the decision. Marginal social cost is the full cost of one more unit once third-party harm is included.

The formula is the spine of the graph:

MSC = MPC + external cost

If a factory pays for labor, materials, and machines but does not pay for the smoke damage it imposes on neighbors, its private cost is lower than the true social cost. The market price then tells only part of the truth.

The difference between the two is the marginal external cost (MEC): the harm one more unit does to people outside the deal. Benefits work the same way. Marginal social benefit is marginal private benefit plus the marginal external benefit (MEB) that spills onto others.

TermMeaningGraph role
MPCCost to the private decision-maker.Supply curve when external costs are ignored.
MSCCost to society, including external cost.Above MPC when production harms others.
MPBBenefit to the private decision-maker.Demand curve when external benefits are ignored.
MSBBenefit to society, including external benefit.Above MPB when using the good helps others.
Two rules for drawing the graph

A production externality always has two cost curves: private cost (MPC) and social cost (MSC). Demand stays a single curve. If production harms others, MSC sits above MPC; if it helps them, MSC sits below.

A consumption externality always has two benefit curves: private benefit (MPB) and social benefit (MSB). Supply stays a single curve. If using the good helps others, MSB sits above MPB; if it harms them, MSB sits below.

So before you draw anything, ask: does the spillover come from making the good or from using it? That tells you which curve gets a twin.

MisconceptionThe efficient amount of pollution is zero.▸

Almost never. The efficient quantity is where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost — and for most harmful activities that point is well above zero. Driving pollution to zero would mean shutting off much of the electricity, transport, and manufacturing that produce it. The last few tons are usually the most expensive to eliminate, and removing them would cost far more than the harm they do. The goal of externality policy is not no harm; it is the right amount of harm — the amount where one more unit's social cost finally outweighs its social benefit.

Not zero: pollution, crime, even homicide

The same logic reaches well past pollution. The efficient amount of crime isn’t zero, either. Preventing every theft would take more police, more cameras, and less freedom than the thefts themselves cost. Even for the worst harm there is, homicide, no city spends without limit to prevent every one; there is always one more patrol or one more streetlight it could pay for and doesn’t.

We make this tradeoff with lives in plain sight. Highway speed limits could be set at 20 miles per hour, and fewer people would die in crashes. We accept higher limits because crawling everywhere would cost every driver time and money, every day. Saying the best level isn’t zero doesn’t make the harm acceptable, and it doesn’t mean we stop working to reduce it. It means the last bit of prevention has real costs, and those costs count too.

Retrieval practiceIf MPC is $8 and the external cost is $3, what is MSC?▸
MSC = MPC + external cost = $8 + $3 = $11.

Major concept 3

The Negative Externality Graph

When Others Pay, Markets Overproduce

The required AP graph for this chapter is the negative production externality. The market uses MPC because producers face private costs. Society cares about MSC because smoke, congestion, emissions, or other third-party costs are real costs too. Since external cost is positive, MSC lies above MPC.

The market quantity, Qm, occurs where MPB = MPC. The socially efficient quantity, Qs, occurs where MPB = MSC. Because the market ignores the external cost, it produces too much: Qm > Qs.

The shaded triangle is the deadweight loss. Every unit between Qs and Qm is worth less to buyers (the height of MPB) than it costs society to make (the height of MSC), so producing it destroys value. Add up those losses from Qs to Qm and you get the triangle between the MSC and demand curves. The vertical gap between MSC and MPC is the marginal external cost of each unit.

Fig 17.1Negative production externality. The market produces Qm where MPB = MPC, but society wants Qs where MPB = MSC. The gap creates overproduction and deadweight loss.
AP watch: label both quantities. The key conclusion is Qm > Qs.
Quiz watch: this graph often appears with private supply labeled S1 and social cost labeled S2. The vertical gap S2 − S1 is the marginal external cost.
MisconceptionA negative externality means the market produces too little of the good.▸

The opposite. Because the price leaves out the external cost, the good looks cheaper to make than it really is, so the market makes too much of it: Qm > Qs. The deadweight loss is the wedge of over-production — the units that were only worth producing while someone else was silently paying part of the cost. Flip the sign and you flip the policy: the cure is to shrink output toward Qs, not expand it.

Quick checkOn a negative externality graph, why is MSC above MPC?▸
Because MSC includes the private cost plus the external cost imposed on third parties.

Major concept 4

Positive Externalities

When Others Benefit, Markets Produce Too Little

Externalities can also be beneficial. A vaccination protects the person who receives it, but it also reduces the chance that others get sick. Education can raise a student's earnings, but it may also create civic and productivity benefits for others. Research can help the firm that performs it, while also creating knowledge that other firms build on.

With a positive externality, the private decision-maker does not receive the full social benefit. The market follows MPB, but society cares about MSB. Since the external benefit is positive, MSB lies above MPB. The result is underproduction or underconsumption.

On the graph, the market quantity, Qm, is where MPB meets supply. The efficient quantity, Qs, is where MSB meets supply. Because buyers ignore the benefit to others, the market stops short: Qm < Qs. The deadweight loss is now a triangle of missing value: units between Qm and Qs that society values more than they cost to make, but that never get made.

Fig 17.2Positive consumption externality. Buyers follow MPB, so the market stops at Qm. Society would gain from going on to Qs, where MSB = MPC. The shaded triangle is the value lost by stopping short.
Quiz watch: this graph often appears with private demand labeled D1 and social benefit labeled D2. The vertical gap D2 − D1 is the marginal external benefit, and the market underproduces: Qm < Qs.
Quick checkOn a positive externality graph, which is larger, Qm or Qs? And what does the vertical gap between MSB and MPB measure?▸
Qs is larger: the market underproduces. The gap between MSB and MPB is the marginal external benefit, the benefit each unit gives people outside the deal.
Retrieval practiceWhy might vaccines be underconsumed in a private market?▸
The person getting vaccinated receives private protection, but other people also benefit from reduced disease spread. If the private decision-maker does not receive the full social benefit, market quantity can be below the socially efficient quantity.

Major concept 5

Measuring Deadweight Loss

How Big Is the Market’s Mistake?

Deadweight loss is value that could have existed and doesn’t. No one collects it: not the buyers, not the sellers, not the government, and not the people downstream. It is the total cost of the market’s mistake.

What that lost value looks like depends on which way the market misses. In Chapters 6 and 7, deadweight loss meant trades that never happened: a buyer and a seller who both would have gained were kept apart. That is still what it means with a positive externality. The flu shots that never get given are value left on the table.

With a negative externality, the loss runs the other way. The trades did happen, and they shouldn’t have. Each ton of paper past the efficient amount costs society more than it is worth to anyone, so making it destroys value: dead fish in exchange for paper nobody valued that highly. Too little output means missing value. Too much output means value used up on harm.

With an externality, you can measure it with one shortcut.

The triangle’s height is the external cost or benefit of one unit, and its base is the gap between the market quantity and the efficient quantity. So for a negative externality,

DWL = ½ × MEC × (Qm − Qs)

and for a positive externality,

DWL = ½ × MEB × (Qs − Qm)

The shortcut works whenever the external cost or benefit is the same for every unit, which is how most quiz graphs are drawn: the social curve runs parallel to the private one.

Worked example 1Paper mills on a river (a negative externality)

Paper mills sell paper in a competitive market. Their wastewater kills fish downstream, a cost of $20 for every ton of paper they make. The mills’ private cost is the supply curve S1. Social cost, S2, sits $20 above it.

Fig 17.3The paper market. Demand meets private cost at 80 tons; demand meets social cost at 60 tons. The deadweight loss triangle is 20 tons wide and $20 tall.
  1. Market quantity: buyers and mills settle where demand meets private cost (S1): Qm = 80 tons a day, at $60 a ton.
  2. Efficient quantity: where demand meets social cost (S2): Qs = 60 tons a day, at $70 a ton.
  3. Overproduction: Qm − Qs = 80 − 60 = 20 tons a day.
  4. Deadweight loss: ½ × $20 × 20 = $200 a day.
The story behind the triangle: on the 80th ton, a buyer pays $60 and gets about $60 of value from it. But that ton costs society $80: $60 at the mill and $20 in dead fish. Making it destroys $20 of value. The 61st ton destroys almost nothing. Add up the losses on all 20 extra tons and you get the $200 triangle: river damage that bought paper nobody valued enough to pay its full cost.
Worked example 2Flu shots (a positive externality)

A flu shot protects the person who gets it, and it also lowers the chance that the people around them get sick. Suppose that protection for others is worth $10 per shot. Private demand, D1, counts only what people will pay to protect themselves. Social benefit, D2, sits $10 above it.

Fig 17.4Flu shots. Private demand meets supply at 400 shots; social benefit meets supply at 500 shots. The deadweight loss triangle is 100 shots wide and $10 tall.
  1. Market quantity: private demand (D1) meets supply: Qm = 400 shots a week, at $30 a shot.
  2. Efficient quantity: social benefit (D2) meets supply: Qs = 500 shots a week, at $35.
  3. Underproduction: Qs − Qm = 500 − 400 = 100 shots a week.
  4. Deadweight loss: ½ × $10 × 100 = $500 a week.
The story behind the triangle: the 401st shot costs about $30 to give. The person deciding values it at about $30 too, so they shrug and skip it. But counting the protection it gives everyone else, that shot is worth about $40 to society. Skipping it throws away about $10 of value. The losses shrink as you get closer to 500 shots, and together they make the $500 triangle: illness that could have been prevented for less than it ended up costing.
A trick for finding the triangle

The deadweight loss triangle always points toward the efficient quantity. Its flat side sits at the market quantity, where the market actually stops, and its tip sits at Qs, where the market should stop. Look at Figures 17.1 through 17.4: in every one, the tip aims at the optimum. (The tax triangle in Chapter 7 did the same thing, pointing back at the equilibrium the tax moved the market away from.)

So if you aren’t sure which area is the deadweight loss, find Qs first, then look for the triangle that points at it.

MisconceptionThe deadweight loss is the damage done to the third party.▸

Not quite. In the paper market, the river damage on all 80 tons is $20 × 80 = $1,600 a day. Only $200 of that is deadweight loss. Most of the damage comes from the first 60 tons, which are worth making even after you count the dead fish; that is why the efficient amount of pollution is not zero. The deadweight loss is only the part that comes from the 20 tons that were not worth their full cost.

Quick checkIn the paper market, suppose the damage were $40 a ton instead of $20, and the efficient quantity fell to 40 tons. The market still makes 80. What is the deadweight loss?▸
DWL = ½ × $40 × (80 − 40) = ½ × $40 × 40 = $800 a day. Doubling the damage made the triangle twice as tall and twice as wide, so it is four times as big.

Major concept 6

When a Tax Removes Deadweight Loss

Taxes and Subsidies, Seen Again

The rule of thumb

Want more of it? Subsidize it. A positive externality is something society wants more of. Left alone, the market makes too little. A subsidy pushes output up, toward the efficient quantity.

Want less of it? Tax it. A negative externality is something society wants less of. Left alone, the market makes too much. A tax pushes output down, toward the efficient quantity.

In Chapter 7, a tax created deadweight loss. It pushed a healthy market below the quantity where benefits and costs balanced, so trades that would have helped both sides never happened. A subsidy did the opposite: it pushed output past that point and paid for trades that weren’t worth their cost.

With an externality, the market starts in the wrong place, so the same tools can work in reverse. Put a $20 tax on each ton of paper. Now the mills feel the river damage as part of their own cost, their supply curve rises to match social cost, and output falls from 80 tons to 60. Economists call this internalizing the externality: the cost that used to land on the river now shows up in the mills’ own decisions. Prices move too. Buyers now pay $70 a ton, the mills keep $50 after the tax, and the $20 gap between those two prices is the tax. The tax didn’t create a deadweight loss. It removed one. In the flu-shot market, a $10 subsidy per shot does the same thing from the other side, moving output from 400 shots to 500. People pay $25 a shot out of pocket, the clinic receives $35, and the subsidy covers the difference.

This is the idea behind a Pigouvian tax or subsidy, named for the economist Arthur Pigou: set it equal to the external cost or benefit, and the market lands on the efficient quantity. The size matters. A tax bigger than the external cost pushes output below Qs and creates a new deadweight loss of its own. Chapter 19 works out how to set these and where they go wrong in practice.

Quick checkA city taxes plastic bags at 10 cents each. Suppose each bag does about 10 cents of harm to others. Does the tax create deadweight loss or remove it? What if each bag did only 2 cents of harm?▸
At 10 cents of harm, the tax matches the external cost and removes the deadweight loss. At 2 cents of harm, the tax is 8 cents too big: bag use falls below the efficient amount, and the tax creates a deadweight loss of its own.
Retrieval practiceWhy did a tax create deadweight loss in Chapter 7 but remove it in the paper market?▸
In Chapter 7 the market started at the efficient quantity, so pushing output down cost value. In the paper market, the market starts above the efficient quantity because the price leaves out the river damage. Pushing output down to Qs gets back the value the market was wasting.

Major concept 7

Coase, Bargaining, and Transaction Costs

When a Private Deal Can Fix a Public Problem

Chapter 16 taught that markets need property rights, courts, contracts, and rule of law. That matters here. The Coase theorem says that if property rights are clear and transaction costs are low, private bargaining may solve some externalities.

Coase is not "externalities solve themselves." It says bargaining can work under demanding conditions. The parties must know who has the right, who is harmed, how large the harm is, how to negotiate, and how to enforce the deal. Real-world transaction costs often block bargaining, especially when many people are affected.

Bargaining calculationIs there a possible deal?

A factory earns $400 from the activity that makes smoke. The smoke does $700 of harm to the residents nearby.

  1. Stopping the smoke costs the factory $400 and saves residents $700, so stopping it creates $700 − $400 = $300 of net value.
  2. If the factory has the right to pollute: the residents can pay the factory anywhere between $400 and $700 to stop. Both sides come out ahead, and the smoke stops.
  3. If the residents have the right to clean air: the factory would have to pay them at least $700 to keep polluting, but polluting is only worth $400 to it. No deal is possible, so the smoke stops.
  4. Either way, the smoke stops. Who holds the right decides who pays, not what happens.
That is Coase’s insight: when bargaining is cheap, the efficient outcome happens no matter who holds the right. But if bargaining costs more than the $300 it would gain (say, thousands of residents who would have to organize), the deal never happens, and a factory that holds the right keeps polluting.
Clarify rights before moralizing
Ask: who is harmed, how large is the harm, who has the right, can the parties bargain, and what are the transaction costs? This is where Chapter 16’s institutions come back in: unclear property rights, slow courts, and corruption all raise transaction costs, which is one more reason private bargaining so often fails.

Where this goes next

This chapter diagnosed the problem: the price leaves out a cost or a benefit, so the market makes too much or too little, and private bargaining fixes it only when the parties are few and the rights are clear. The next chapter looks at one of the most important special cases: a resource that anyone can use and no one owns, where every user imposes a cost on every other user. After that, Chapter 19 opens the repair kit: taxes that put the missing cost back into the price, subsidies that pay for the missing benefit, and the hard catch that someone has to know the right number and set it honestly.

Classification

A factory produces steel and releases pollution that damages nearby crops. Identify the externality type.

Reveal answer▸

Negative production externality. The producer's activity imposes a cost on third parties.

Graph move

On a negative production externality graph, where are Qm and Qs?

Reveal answer▸

Qm is where MPB/D intersects MPC/S. Qs is where MPB/D intersects MSC. Since MSC is above MPC, Qm is greater than Qs.

Bargaining

A noisy activity creates $250 of benefit for the producer and $600 of harm for neighbors. What is the net social gain from stopping it?

Reveal answer▸

Net social gain = $600 - $250 = $350. If bargaining costs are below $350, there may be a mutually beneficial deal to stop or reduce the activity.

Market thesis

Externalities show that voluntary exchange is not enough when some affected people are outside the exchange: a deal can benefit buyer and seller while pushing costs or benefits onto hidden third parties.

If economists ruled the world…

they would make polluters pay. Tax bads, not goods. The aim is not to punish production; it is to stop pretending a cost disappears just because it is pushed onto someone who never agreed to bear it.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Negative externalities cause overproduction when external costs are ignored.
  • Positive externalities cause underproduction when external benefits are ignored.
  • Coase bargaining can fix some externalities, but only under demanding conditions.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • How should society measure third-party costs and benefits?
  • Which tool fits best: liability, bargaining, taxes, fees, cap-and-trade, or regulation?
  • How much of the harmful activity should remain, given that the efficient amount is rarely zero?
Takeaways
In your life

Before you judge a cheap product, ask who isn't in the price. The bill for the smoke, the noise, or the carbon lands on someone — often neither the buyer nor the seller.

In your work

If your operation creates a cost outsiders pay — noise, emissions, congestion — expect it to be priced eventually. The cheapest time to handle an externality is before a regulator handles it for you.
