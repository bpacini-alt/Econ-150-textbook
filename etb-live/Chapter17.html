Market Failures I:
The Tragedy of the Commons

Everyone's to use, no one's to save

Chapter 17~ 40 min read

A lake full of fish, and a dozen boats that can all reach it. No fence rings the water; no one can be told to stay off. Every skipper knows the stock is shrinking. Every skipper also knows that a fish left in the water today is simply a fish someone else lands tomorrow. So the rational move, for each of them, is to catch as much as they can, now.

Notice there is no villain here. No one is cheating — no monopolist, no fraud, no broken contract. Each skipper is doing exactly what the last fifteen chapters praised: responding to incentives, pursuing their own gain. And precisely because every one of them does, the lake empties. The same self-interest that filled the market with bread and shoes is, on this water, draining it of fish.

Last chapter put a name in one corner of the four-goods grid and left it there: the common resource — something no one can be kept out of, yet every use leaves less for the next person. We classified it. We did not ask what becomes of it. This is what becomes of it. The question is why a resource everyone needs, and no one wants to destroy, gets destroyed anyway — and whether anything short of a fence or a police officer can save it.

The big picture
A common resource is rivalrous but non-excludable: every use subtracts from what is left, yet no one can be shut out. That single combination is a trap. Each user reaps the full benefit of taking one more unit, while the cost — a thinner stock — is split among everyone. So everyone takes more than the resource can bear, no one can be stopped, and the predictable end is ruin. This is not a failure of character. It is a failure of structure, and the same engine of self-interest that coordinates a market drives this one into the ground.

Major concept 1

The Commons Trap

Two properties, one trap.

A common resource is defined by two properties at once. It is rival: the fish you catch, the water you pump, the lane you fill is genuinely subtracted from what others can have. And it is non-excludable: there is no practical fence, login, or ticket that keeps non-payers out. Either property alone is manageable. Together they are a trap.

Recall the four-goods grid from the last chapter. Three of its corners behave. Private goods are rival and excludable, so markets price and ration them well. Club goods are excludable, so a provider can charge. Public goods are non-excludable but non-rival — your use does not subtract from mine — so the danger there is that no one builds them. The common resource sits in the last corner, with the worst pair of traits for a market: you cannot charge for use, and use genuinely uses the thing up.

So the two non-excludable goods fail in exactly opposite directions. A public good is underprovided — non-rivalry plus no way to charge means no one supplies it. A common resource is overused — rivalry plus no way to charge means everyone takes from it. Same missing fence, mirror-image disasters.

The list is longer and more serious than fish. Groundwater aquifers drained faster than they refill. Grazing land cropped to dust. Clean air and a stable climate, with the atmosphere used as a free dump for carbon. Even antibiotics behave like a commons: every unnecessary prescription spends a little of a shared stock of effectiveness, and resistance is the depletion — a thread we will pick up in the healthcare capstone.

MisconceptionA common resource is just a public good.▸

They share one trait — non-excludability — and nothing else. A public good is non-rival: my use does not reduce yours, so the failure is that no one builds it (underprovision). A common resource is rival: my use does reduce yours, so the failure is that everyone drains it (overuse). Same broken fence, opposite catastrophes. Mixing them up reverses both the diagnosis and the cure.

Quick checkAn aquifer can be pumped by any landowner above it, and every gallon pumped is a gallon gone. What kind of good is it, and which failure follows?▸
A common resource: non-excludable (any landowner can pump) and rival (each gallon subtracts). The failure is overuse — the aquifer is drained faster than it recharges.
Major concept 2

Why Self-Interest Turns to Ruin

The cost you create, you don't pay.

Here is the exact gear that turns. When a skipper takes one more haul, they keep the entire benefit of that catch. But the cost they create — a thinner stock, a harder search for everyone next season — is spread across every boat on the lake, including the others. So each skipper personally faces only a sliver of the damage they personally cause. Private benefit lands on one person; the cost is socialized across all.

That asymmetry makes overuse the individually correct choice. A skipper can see the stock falling and still be right, for themselves, to keep fishing — because the fish they leave behind is not saved, it is simply caught by the next boat. Restraint by one user does not rescue the resource; it just transfers the catch to someone less restrained. This is why the commons is a trap and not a morality play: the trap punishes the conservationist and rewards the taker, no matter how decent either one is.

Watch what that does to a renewable stock over time. Left open, use races past the level the resource can replace, and the stock falls toward collapse. Held to a limit — by any means — it can settle at a level that renews itself year after year.

Fig 17.1Two fates for a renewable stock. Under open access, use outruns renewal and the stock collapses. Held to a limit — by a price, an owner, a law, or a community — it can settle where it renews itself.
Worked exampleShould one more boat go out?

A lake is already fished. One more boat would land a catch worth $1,000 and cost $300 to operate. But by crowding the stock, it would reduce the catch of all the other boats by $900 in total.

  1. The new skipper's private calculation: $1,000 catch − $300 cost = $700 profit. Worth it. They go.
  2. The social calculation: $1,000 gained − $300 cost − $900 lost by others = −$200. The boat destroys $200 of net value.
  3. The $900 it imposes on others never appears in the skipper's private decision, so the boat goes out even though society is worse off.
Boats keep entering until private profit hits zero — far past the point where the resource's value is highest. The gap is the tragedy.
MisconceptionThe tragedy happens because people are greedy or short-sighted.▸

It happens with perfectly rational, far-sighted, decent people. The skipper who restrains themselves out of conscience does not save a single fish — the next boat simply catches it. Under open access, overuse is the correct individual choice even for someone who loves the lake. That is what makes it a structural trap rather than a failure of virtue, and it is why moralizing fixes nothing: you have to change the structure, not the people.

Retrieval practiceWhy does a skipper keep fishing even while watching the stock shrink?▸
Because they keep the full value of their own catch but bear only a fraction of the cost they create, and because any fish they leave is caught by someone else rather than saved. Private benefit, socialized cost, and futile restraint all push toward taking more.
Major concept 3

The Two Famous Fixes — and Their Limits

A fence or a rule. Each works somewhere; neither works everywhere.

If the trap is non-excludability plus rivalry, the fixes attack one or the other. The first is to build the fence — assign property rights, an enclosure in the old sense. Give the resource an owner, and that owner now bears the full cost of depleting it, so they have every reason to conserve. This is why owned cattle thrive while wild fish collapse: someone loses money if the herd dies, but no one in particular loses when the last tuna is caught. For some commons, rights can even be made tradable — an ITQ hands each boat a share of a total catch limit it can sell, harnessing the market to ration use.

The second fix is to impose a rule — a government cap on use: catch limits, fishing seasons, licenses, pumping permits, congestion charges. A central authority sets the total the resource can bear and polices it. Where the state can monitor and enforce, this works.

But each fix has a domain, and outside it, fails. A fence needs boundaries you can actually draw and defend. You can title farmland; you cannot cheaply fence the open ocean, the atmosphere, or an aquifer that crosses three states and a border. And enclosure raises a hard question the efficiency story hides: who gets the rights? Handing the fishery to whoever fishes it today can dispossess everyone who comes after, or who came before. A government rule needs an enforcer with reach and knowledge — a regulator who can measure the right limit and make it stick. No world government polices the high seas or the climate, and a distant authority often lacks the local knowledge to know what limit even fits.

MisconceptionThe tragedy proves we must privatize everything (or hand it all to government).▸

Neither fix is universal. Privatization needs boundaries you can draw and defend, and it forces a distributive choice about who receives the rights. Regulation needs an enforcer with both reach and local knowledge. Some of the most important commons — the atmosphere, high-seas fisheries, a shared aquifer — defeat both. The honest claim is narrow: the diagnosis is settled, but the right fix depends on the resource.

Quick checkWhy is privatization a poor fix for carbon emissions into the atmosphere?▸
You cannot draw and defend boundaries on the atmosphere or assign someone ownership of the global climate. With no way to fence it or exclude users, there is nothing to enclose — which is why carbon is usually approached with rules or prices instead.
Major concept 4

Ostrom's Third Way

Between the fence and the state stand the people who actually use it.

For decades the debate had only two answers: privatize the commons or let the government run it. Market or state. Then the political scientist Elinor Ostrom went and looked — at Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese village forests, Spanish irrigation networks, Maine lobster grounds — and found a third path that real communities had used for centuries. The users themselves make and enforce the rules that govern their own commons, without privatizing it and without surrendering it to a distant state.

These are not free-for-alls. Ostrom found that the communities which succeed share a recognizable design: clear boundaries on who may use the resource, rules fitted to local conditions, a real say for the users in writing those rules, monitoring (often by the users themselves), graduated sanctions that start small for first offenses, cheap and fast ways to settle disputes, and an outside authority that recognizes the community's right to organize. The commons survives not because the rules vanish, but because the people closest to the resource make rules that fit it and watch each other keep them.

The deep point reframes the whole chapter. The tragedy is real, but it is not destiny, and the cure is not always a market or a state. Sometimes the institution that saves a commons is one the community builds for itself — neither the fence nor the police officer, but the fishers' own agreement, enforced by fishers. That is the sharpest version of a claim this unit keeps making: the repair for a market failure is often a better institution, and a better institution need not mean more government.

Where this comes fromOstrom — and what Hardin actually meant

The phrase "tragedy of the commons" comes from biologist Garrett Hardin in 1968. It is worth knowing that Hardin himself later clarified that he was describing the unmanaged commons — open access with no rules at all. That qualification is the whole game, because it is exactly where Elinor Ostrom planted her flag. In Governing the Commons (1990) she assembled decades of fieldwork to show that communities around the world have, again and again, governed shared resources sustainably through their own institutions — neither pure markets nor central states. In 2009 she became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in economics for it.

Mark the weight honestly: Ostrom's eight design principles are political economy and institutional analysis, not AP supply-and-demand mechanics, and the principles themselves will not be graphed on the exam. What is fair game — and what matters — is the concept: a common resource is not doomed, and "privatize or regulate" is not the full menu of cures.

MisconceptionOstrom showed communities self-organize on their own, so commons need no rules.▸

Backwards. Ostrom's successful communities run on strict, often elaborate rules — defined boundaries, active monitoring, graduated sanctions, dispute resolution. Her finding is not that rules are unnecessary; it is that the rules can be made and enforced locally, by the users, instead of being imposed by an owner or a distant state. Self-governance is not the absence of governance.

Settled diagnosis, contested cure

Put the chapter in one line. Diagnosis: a rival, non-excludable resource under open access will be overused — this is not in dispute. Cure: a fence, a rule, or a community's own institution — and which one fits depends on the resource, with most real commons needing some blend. The failure is a fact; the remedy is a choice.

On the exam

Diagnose the commons, then repair it

The moves you can now make: classify a resource by excludability and rivalry, explain the overuse mechanism, weigh a private benefit against a social cost, and match a fix to the resource.

Classification

A public grazing pasture is open to all local herders, and every animal that grazes leaves less grass for the rest. Classify the good and name the likely failure.

Reveal answer▸

A common resource: non-excludable (open to all herders) and rival (each animal's grazing subtracts). The likely failure is overuse — the pasture is grazed past what it can regrow.

Mechanism

Explain why an individual herder adds one more animal even though all herders know the pasture is degrading.

Reveal answer▸

The herder captures the full benefit of the extra animal but bears only a small share of the damage, which is spread across all herders. Restraint would not save the grass — another herder's animal would eat it — so adding the animal is individually rational even as it harms the group.

Remedy

Give one strength and one limitation of privatizing the pasture.

Reveal answer▸

Strength: an owner bears the full cost of overgrazing, so has a reason to conserve. Limitation: boundaries and enforcement may be costly, and assigning ownership forces a distributive choice about which herders gain rights and which lose access.

FRQ practice

An open-access fishery

Suggested time: 12 minutes · 9 points

A coastal fishery is open to any boat, and the fish stock is falling year after year. There are currently no limits on how many boats fish or how much they catch.

  1. Classify the fishery using excludability and rivalry. 1 pt 
  2. Explain why it is non-excludable and rival. 1 pt
  3. Explain why an individual boat keeps fishing as the stock falls, referring to private benefit and shared cost. 2 pts
  4. A new boat would land a $1,000 catch, cost $300 to run, and reduce other boats' catch by $900. Show whether adding it raises or lowers total social value, and state whether the boat goes out anyway. 1 pt
  5. Describe two policy fixes and one limitation of each. 2 pts
  6. Explain how a community of fishers could sustain the stock without privatizing it or relying on a central government. 1 pt 
  7. Identify the externality each boat imposes on the others. 1 pt
Reveal scoring guide▸
  • 1 ptCommon resource: non-excludable and rival.
  • 1 ptNon-excludable because any boat can fish; rival because each fish caught is one others cannot catch.
  • 1 ptThe boat keeps the full value of its own catch but bears only a fraction of the cost it creates.
  • 1 ptRestraint does not save the fish — another boat catches them — so overuse is individually rational.
  • 1 ptSocial value = $1,000 − $300 − $900 = −$200, a loss.

  • 1 ptNames two fixes (e.g., assign property rights/ITQ; government catch limit) with a valid limitation of each.
  • 1 ptCommunity self-governance: locally made rules, boundaries, monitoring, and graduated sanctions (Ostrom).

Reveal common mistakes▸
  • Calling a common resource a public good. Both are non-excludable, but a commons is rival, so it is overused rather than underprovided.
  • Blaming the tragedy on greed. It occurs among rational, decent users because the structure rewards taking more.
  • Treating privatization as a universal fix. It needs feasible boundaries and forces a choice about who receives the rights.
  • Describing Ostrom's communities as rule-free. They depend on strict, locally made rules, monitoring, and sanctions.
Market thesis

A resource everyone may use and no one can protect will be used until it is gone — not from malice but from arithmetic, as each user pockets the whole gain and shares only a sliver of the cost.

If economists ruled the world…

they would stop mistaking open access for freedom. A commons survives only when someone — a price, an owner, a law, or the community itself — sets a limit on use. The argument is over which, not whether.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • A rival, non-excludable resource under open access is overused and depleted.
  • Each user gets the full private benefit but shares the cost, so individual restraint fails.
  • Sustaining the resource requires some limit on access — a price, a right, a rule, or a norm.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Which limit fits this resource — a fence, a law, or the community's own rules?
  • When rights are assigned, who receives them, and is that distribution fair?
  • Which commons can a community govern itself, and which need outside enforcement?
Takeaways
In your life

When something is "free for everyone," ask who keeps it from being used up. If the answer is no one, it will be — the shared fridge, the public trail, the office printer.

In your work

A shared resource with no rules gets overused: a team's time, a common codebase, a pooled budget. Name who sets the limit, or watch it dissipate.

Where this goes next

Look again at why the lake emptied. Every boat that motored out imposed a cost on every other boat — a thinner stock, a longer search — and paid none of it. That uncounted cost, created by one decision but landing on people outside it, is not special to fisheries. It is the engine under nearly every failure left in this unit. The next chapter gives it a name and a graph: the externality. The commons, it turns out, was just its most tragic special case.
