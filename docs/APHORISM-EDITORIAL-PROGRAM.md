**Owner/approval:** Benjamin approves the exact final words; an assistant proposes, investigates, tracks, and never silently substitutes wording.

## Why we are doing this

An aphorism is **an insight packaged in a nugget**, not a slogan selected for sound. We want compact, durable reasoning tools: a sentence students can unpack, apply outside the chapter, and use to correct a tempting misconception. The saying is valuable *because* of the argument it carries. It should often be useful in life, work, professional teaching, civic life, or personal decision-making.

The two most useful models raised by Benjamin are Thomas Sowell's trade-off dictum and Daniel Willingham's "Memory is the residue of thought." Each compresses a substantial account of how the world works; neither is a substitute for the explanation. The chapter's central claim comes **before** the catchy sentence.

This is not a mandate to shoehorn a box into every section. Collect 3–6 proposals for a chapter during ideation (some chapters may have more), then retain only the few exceptional, nonduplicative lines that deserve students' attention. It is better to have no new aphorism than a shallow one. Keep lines already written by Benjamin, especially proven language in the published chapters and memorization quizzes, in the candidate pool.

## The five-layer entry for a strong aphorism

1. **Thick concept:** What claim, causal mechanism, evidence, assumptions, and tension is the chapter actually teaching?
2. **Misconception:** What plausible false intuition would this sentence overturn? Do not manufacture a strawman.
3. **Intellectual ancestry:** Look for the original author's wording or a substantial idea from economists, educational psychologists, philosophers, and the present textbook. Distinguish quotation, attributed paraphrase, author-original wording, and assistant draft.
4. **Nugget:** The shortest accurate, memorable, genuinely useful formulation. A misconception-corrector can be playful or provocatively counterintuitive, but its explanation must be responsible.
5. **Unpacking / boundary / transfer:** A one-to-three-sentence explanation of the mechanism; one clear condition when the claim fails or would mislead; one new case where students could reason with it.

Ask: **Could a student explain this in their own words, tell when it applies, and use it to reason about a fresh situation?** If the answer is no, we have a slogan, not an anchor.

Categories often overlap: *foundational*, *counterintuitive*, *misconception-correcting*, *transferable*, and *classic dictum*. Do not select on cleverness, word count, or quote celebrity alone.

### Accuracy, evidence, ethics, and voice

- Verify external quotations against primary sources or authoritative reproductions before publishing words in quotation marks with a name; do not launder an assistant-written adaptation into an attribution. A familiar quote may have disputed provenance.
- Do not treat a chapter's moral recommendation as a scientific fact. Distinguish positive economic analysis from normative conclusions. Represent contestable policy arguments fairly.
- Check the full chapter context and current main-branch text before approving placement; prior summaries, chats, generated prose, and older exports are clues, not canonical text.
- Acknowledge limits: prices can coordinate without representing moral worth; growing a pie does not ensure equitable shares; fixed learning styles are unsupported, but verbal-plus-visual explanation can still be helpful; stronger Tier 1 instruction does not replace accommodations; costs do not always reward confrontation.
- Sound like Benjamin: warm, intelligent, occasionally funny, intellectually serious. Avoid generic AI cadence, forced paradox, precious metaphors, or false oppositions.
- Explain *why* an inherited phrase works; do not replace it merely to make it seem new. If final text uses a direct quotation, provide attribution and an appropriate citation in the chapter.
- Preserve distinct conceptual roles for nearby sayings. E.g., **prices as signals/incentives** (Econ 3) versus **scarcity always requires rationing** (Econ 4); **growth of the pie** (Econ 6/14) versus **trade-offs cannot be wished away** (Econ 1).

## Misconceptions as the engine, not decoration

Examples where the aphorism overturns a tempting intuition:

- **Economics:** economic life is fixed-sum; the legal taxpayer pays the tax; suppressing prices suppresses scarcity; absolute ability determines trade; profits are always socially good; all large firms have harmful market power; voluntary trades cannot harm bystanders; private and social incentives always align; an IQ-like metric for wages determines moral worth.
- **Learning:** constructive learning requires unguided discovery; fixed learning styles should be matched; an active, entertaining lesson must cause learning; short-run fluent performance proves durable understanding; statistical significance proves importance; heritable means immutable; every difficult child needs discipline; kindness means making everything easy; an affirming personality label is scientifically valid; teaching is value-neutral.
- **Visual reveal (EDCOR 24):** contrast (1) empty/text-only explanation, (2) overloaded decorative slide, (3) words plus *one* task-relevant explanatory visual. “We are all visual learners” = a provocative rejection of permanent learner *types*, **not** a literal claim that vision is always the best modality or that students with visual impairments should be excluded. Movement/brain breaks have a different proposed mechanism than dual coding or multimedia instruction.

## Selection and classroom implementation (later PRs, not this PR)

When Benjamin has approved a chapter's few strongest lines:

- Add the approved **exact wording** in a small **“Worth remembering”** box at the point where the idea is taught, followed by one or two explanatory sentences; recap after chapter synthesis. EDCOR 11 is the current reference implementation; shared CSS needs separate technical work across both repositories.
- Repeat the same words in memorization quizzes, slides, and practice rather than silently rephrasing them.
- Practice more than verbatim recall: retrieve; interpret; discriminate correct/incorrect applications; explain the mechanism; identify limits; apply to a novel case; return after spacing. See [Precise Practice](PRECISE-PRACTICE-DESIGN.md). These are anchors, **not** a fifth foundational practice engine.
- AI feedback for explanations remains **provisional** and gated on instructor-scored rubric calibration; never present a numerical score as certified understanding without evidence.

### Review workflow — eight *candidates*, not eight pages of prose

1. In each round, select **5–10 strong individual aphorisms** (default 8), drawn from both books. Start with promising existing wording before replacing it. For each: chapter/link, exact proposed line, underlying concept, misconception, source/ownership, qualification, and one transfer/example.
2. Benjamin labels **YES / MAYBE / REWRITE / NO** (and may supply his own better phrase). **YES** means *editorially chosen for further placement review*, not automatically permission to publish. Record the exact preferred wording, not merely a status.
3. Keep rejected and alternate lines in the candidate bank as historic context unless Benjamin instructs removal. Avoid accumulating synonyms in the student-facing book.
4. Once a batch is settled, check each chapter's full text for duplication and placement; make a **separate book-content PR** with additions/removals visible and preview links. Benjamin merges when satisfied.
5. At the end of each batch, record decisions, chapter placements, sourcing, remaining questions, and the next 5–10. Do not force all chapters to receive a certain number of boxes.
