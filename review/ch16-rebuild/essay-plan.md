## Thesis

Liberal societies decline, on principle, to say what prosperity is for. That restraint is wise for a government, but no person can live by it. Markets run on virtues and purposes they cannot produce, so someone has to supply them. The Christian and Restorationist traditions have been answering the question for a long time, and their answer is neither "prosperity is bad" nor "prosperity is the point."

## Main points

1. **Opening hook.** Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930): he expected abundance and asked what people would do with their freedom from want. (Alternative: Aristotle's point that wealth is only useful for something else.)
2. **The liberal refusal.** The Liberal Synthesis leaves the question to persons, families, and churches, because a state that answered it would have to enforce its answer. But Böckenförde: the liberal state lives on moral conditions it can't guarantee. McCloskey: markets need honesty, trust, and prudence that neither prices nor laws can manufacture. The refusal pushes the question down to each person; it doesn't make it go away.
3. **Where modern markets came from, briefly.** A confluence, not a single cause: Weber, Berman and Tierney, the scholastics, Stark. Christian ideas about dignity were one important stream among many. Avoid both errors: Christianity was unrelated, or Christianity was the whole story. (Keep short; the introduction already covers the confluence.)
4. **Wesley's three rules as the frame.** "Gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can" (sermon "The Use of Money"). Economics has a lot to say about the first two: work, enterprise, thrift, capital. The tradition insists the third is the point. Note Wesley's limits on the first rule (no gain at the cost of a neighbor's health or soul).
5. **Foundation and critic.** The same tradition that supplied dignity also produced the market's sharpest critics: Basil and Chrysostom on hoarding, Leo XIII's *Rerum Novarum* (property defended, workers not instruments), King's "Notes on American Capitalism." Slavery shows both sides: Christians defended it, and Christian arguments helped end it. A principle can condemn the people who hold it imperfectly.
6. **The Restorationist answer: stewardship.** Tie it to Chapter 16's property-rights bundle: stewardship is a property right with duties attached. The law of consecration and the United Order were not socialism. "An odd socialist: he seemed to love the poor without hating the rich." The range of voices (Joseph Smith, Brigham Young, the 1930s First Presidency, Benson, Nibley) shows real disagreement within one tradition, and the Church has no formal economic philosophy.
7. **Freedom and responsibility.** Libertarian vs. libertine. Counting the costs of enforcement is fair; wanting freedom from the consequences of your choices is something else.
8. **Close in Benjamin's own voice.** His answer to the title question.
