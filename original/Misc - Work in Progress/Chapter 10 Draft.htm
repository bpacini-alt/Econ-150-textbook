Firm Model II: Production Decisions

The firm as a problem-solving machine

Chapter 10 ~ 42 min read

What does a firm actually make? A restaurant does not merely make food. It solves hunger, timing, taste, convenience, atmosphere, trust, and the awkward problem of where people can gather without anyone having to host. A ride-share company does not merely make rides. It solves finding, pricing, paying, coordinating, and trusting a stranger's car. An online marketplace does not merely sell stuff. It solves search, logistics, speed, reliability, returns, and trust.

That changes the way production should sound. This chapter is not "factory math." Production is the art of turning scarce inputs into something another person values. Sometimes the output is a pizza, a chair, a haircut, or a phone. Sometimes the output is coordination, a reduced hassle, a guarantee, a safer transaction, a faster answer, or the quiet relief of knowing the thing will arrive on time.

Economists call the firm's method of doing this a production function : inputs go in; a consumer-valued solution comes out. The whole chapter grows from that simple machine. If firms are trying to solve problems for consumers, then the business question is: which firm can turn scarce inputs into a better solution, at a cost low enough that the solution is worth producing?

The big picture
A firm is an organized attempt to turn scarce inputs into a solved problem for consumers. The production function is the map of that transformation; innovation improves the map; profit is the scorecard that compares consumer value with opportunity cost; and the output rule, MR = MC, tells the firm how far to carry the solving. In competitive markets, profit often rewards firms for creating real value. But profit is not automatic proof of virtue: later chapters will separate value creation from market power, cheating, and rent-seeking.

Major concept 1

The Production Function

Inputs go in. A solved problem comes out. That is the firm in its simplest form.

Chapter 9 treated cost as scarcity from the producer's side. This chapter asks what the firm is trying to do with those scarce resources. The answer is not merely "make output," if output means a pile of physical stuff. The deeper answer is: the firm combines inputs to solve a consumer problem.

The model is compact:

Q = f(inputs)

Read that formula in English. The quantity or quality of output depends on the inputs the firm combines and the way it combines them. Labor, capital, land, materials, knowledge, software, layout, management, contracts, routines, and technology all enter the machine. Out comes a meal, a delivery, a diagnosis, a search result, a repaired car, a streamed movie, or a classroom experience.

The word function matters because two firms can have similar inputs and very different outputs. One restaurant has ingredients, cooks, tables, and a payment system. Another has the same categories of inputs. Yet one leaves customers impatient and confused while the other turns dinner into a small miracle of timing, taste, and welcome. The difference is not just the inputs. It is the method: the recipe, workflow, training, layout, judgment, and coordination that turn resources into value.

Fig 10.1 A production function maps inputs into output. Here "output" means more than units; it means a solution consumers value.
What to notice: this chapter is not making marginal product the star. The key AP move here is to interpret the relationship: inputs are transformed into valued output.
Rules of business success · Rule #2: start with the problem

Do not begin with "What do I want to sell?" Begin with "What problem is the consumer trying to solve?" Your product is not merely the label on the menu or the thing in the box. It is convenience, reliability, safety, status, taste, speed, comfort, coordination, trust, or reduced hassle. The best firms are often not the ones with the fanciest inputs. They are the ones that understand the problem most clearly.

Quick check A college tutoring center uses tutors, rooms, scheduling software, practice materials, and training. What is its output if we define output as a consumer-valued solution? ▸
Not merely "hours of tutoring." The output is improved understanding, confidence, better preparation, accountability, and less confusion for students. The production function turns inputs into those valued outcomes.
Retrieval practice State the production function in words and explain why "output" can be more than a physical good. ▸

A production function describes how a firm turns inputs into output. Output can be a physical good, but it can also be a service or a solved problem: speed, reliability, trust, coordination, safety, or reduced hassle. The common mistake is to treat only physical objects as "real" production.

Major concept 2

Innovation Improves the Machine

Innovation is not magic. It is a better way to turn inputs into value.

Once you see the firm as a production function, innovation becomes less mysterious. It is not glitter thrown on a business plan. It is a change in the firm's method of production that lets the same scarce resources do more valuable work.

There are two broad forms. Cost-reducing innovation lets the firm produce the same solution with fewer or cheaper inputs. A restaurant redesigns the kitchen so servers walk fewer wasted steps. A manufacturer reorganizes the line so defects fall. A software firm automates a routine task. The consumer may receive the same output, but fewer resources are burned getting it there.

Then there is quality-increasing innovation . The same inputs create a better solution: faster service, more reliable delivery, a safer payment system, a clearer interface, fresher food, or a stronger guarantee. Sometimes quality rises and cost falls at the same time, which is why innovation can feel almost unfair when a rival finds it first.

Fig 10.2 Innovation shifts the production function upward. At the same input level, the firm now produces more output, better output, or a solution consumers value more.
Cost-side reading: if the same output now requires fewer inputs, average total cost or marginal cost can fall.
Synthesis Two ways innovation creates value
  • Cost-reducing: the same solution uses fewer costly inputs, so the firm can produce at lower cost.
  • Quality-increasing: the same inputs produce a better, faster, safer, more reliable, or less annoying solution.
  • Sometimes both: the most dangerous rival is often the one whose method is cheaper and better.
Quick check A grocery store installs self-checkout and better inventory software. Give one cost-reducing effect and one quality-increasing effect. ▸
Cost-reducing: fewer labor hours are needed for the same number of transactions or less inventory is wasted. Quality-increasing: checkout may be faster and shelves may be more reliably stocked. The same change can improve both cost and consumer experience.
Retrieval practice Given a before-and-after production function, how can you tell whether innovation occurred? ▸

If the firm can produce more or better output from the same inputs, or the same output with fewer inputs, the production function has improved. In a graph, that appears as an upward shift. In a cost graph, the same improvement may show up as lower ATC or MC.

Major concept 3

Profit Is the Scorecard

Revenue asks what consumers valued. Cost asks what the resources gave up. Profit compares the two.

If the firm is a problem-solving machine, profit is not just "money left over." Profit is the scorecard that asks whether the firm used scarce resources in a way consumers valued more than the alternatives those resources could have served.

Start with revenue:

TR = P x Q

Revenue reflects consumers' willingness to pay for the solution. Cost reflects the opportunity cost of the resources used to create it. Put them together:

Profit = TR - TC

But Chapter 9 warned that cost has layers. An accountant counts explicit costs: money paid for wages, rent, materials, utilities, insurance. An economist also counts implicit costs: the salary the owner gave up, the interest the owner's savings could have earned elsewhere, the rent the owner could have charged someone else. That distinction creates two profit measures.

Accounting profit = total revenue - explicit costs

Economic profit = total revenue - explicit costs - implicit costs

Economic profit is the tougher test. A firm can look successful on paper and still be wasting resources if those resources would have created more value somewhere else.

Worked example The cafe that looks profitable

Maya quits a $70,000 job and uses $40,000 of her savings to open a cafe. In its first year the cafe earns $220,000 in revenue. It pays $150,000 in wages, rent, ingredients, utilities, and insurance. Maya's savings would have earned $2,000 in interest elsewhere.

  1. Accounting profit = TR - explicit costs = 220,000 - 150,000 = $70,000 .
  2. Implicit costs = foregone salary + foregone interest = 70,000 + 2,000 = $72,000 .
  3. Economic profit = TR - explicit costs - implicit costs = 220,000 - 150,000 - 72,000 = -$2,000 .
The cafe earns positive accounting profit but negative economic profit. Maya has not yet created enough value to beat her next-best alternative.
Fig 10.3 Accounting profit counts only explicit costs. Economic profit subtracts explicit and implicit costs, so it can be negative even when the business looks profitable.
Market thesis · what profit can signal

In competitive markets, economic profit often signals that a firm found a higher-valued use for resources. Consumers valued the solution more than the next-best use of the inputs. But this is a model, not a moral halo. Firms can also profit through market power, fraud, barriers to entry, political favoritism, or rent-seeking. Later chapters sort those cases out. For now, learn the competitive-market logic: profit is the scoreboard for value created relative to opportunity cost.

Quick check A firm earns $500,000 in revenue, pays $420,000 in explicit costs, and has $60,000 in implicit costs. Find accounting and economic profit. ▸
Accounting profit = 500,000 - 420,000 = $80,000. Economic profit = 500,000 - 420,000 - 60,000 = $20,000.
Retrieval practice Why can accounting profit be positive while economic profit is negative? ▸

Accounting profit subtracts only explicit costs. Economic profit also subtracts implicit opportunity costs, such as the owner's foregone salary or interest. If accounting profit is smaller than those implicit costs, economic profit is negative. The business is busy, but the resources would have done better elsewhere.

Major concept 4

The Output Rule

Do the next bit of solving only if consumers value it at least as much as it costs to provide.

The production function tells the firm how inputs become output. Profit tells the firm how to keep score. The next question is quantity: how much should the firm produce?

The answer is not "as much as possible." A firm can be huge and unprofitable, busy and wasteful, admired and broke. The firm wants the quantity where the gap between revenue and cost is largest. So it compares the next unit's benefit with the next unit's cost.

MR = ΔTR / ΔQ

MC = ΔTC / ΔQ

If marginal revenue is greater than marginal cost, the next unit adds more revenue than cost, so profit rises. Make it. If marginal cost is greater than marginal revenue, the next unit costs more than it brings in, so profit falls. Do not make it. The stopping rule is the famous one:

MR = MC

For a price-taking firm, each unit sells at the market price, so marginal revenue equals price. The rule becomes:

P = MC

Choosing output when price is $24
Q TR TC MR MC Produce?
1 24 18 24 18 yes
2 48 34 24 16 yes
3 72 54 24 20 yes
4 96 78 24 24 stop here
5 120 108 24 30 no
6 144 144 24 36 no
Worked: find the profit-maximizing quantity ▸
  1. Because price is $24 for each unit, MR = $24.
  2. Produce units while MR is at least MC: units 1, 2, 3, and 4 all pass.
  3. The 5th unit has MC = $30, which is greater than MR = $24, so it would reduce profit.
  4. At Q = 4, profit = TR - TC = 96 - 78 = $18 .
The profit-maximizing quantity is Q = 4. The firm is not trying to maximize output; it is maximizing profit.
Fig 10.4 A price-taking firm chooses Q* where P = MR intersects MC. If price is above ATC at Q*, the firm earns positive economic profit, shown by the rectangle.
Quick check If price is $10 and MC values are $4, $7, $10, $13, and $16 for units 1-5, how many units should the firm produce? ▸
Three units. The first three units have MC less than or equal to price. The fourth unit costs $13 to produce but brings in only $10, so it would lower profit.
Retrieval practice Explain MR = MC in ordinary language, using the problem-solving theme of the chapter. ▸

Do the next bit of solving only if consumers will pay at least as much as it costs to provide. If the extra revenue is greater than the extra cost, output should rise. If the extra cost is greater than the extra revenue, output should stop. In a price-taking market, the extra revenue is the price, so the rule becomes P = MC.

Major concept 5

Non-Product Products

Sometimes the product is not the object. Sometimes the product is making the trade possible.

"Cut out the middleman" is one of those phrases that sounds obvious until you ask what the middleman was doing. If the middleman did nothing, fine: cut away. But often the middleman produced the most valuable part of the transaction. Not the physical object, but the matching, timing, delivery, verification, payment, and trust that let the exchange happen at all.

This is where the production-function idea becomes more powerful. If the product is coordination, then producing coordination is production. A wholesaler may not manufacture the tomatoes, but it solves the problem of getting the right tomatoes to the right restaurants at the right time in reliable quantity. A platform may not own the cars, rooms, or goods, but it solves search, matching, reputation, payment, dispute handling, and trust. A payment company may not make the product being bought, but it makes the trade safer and easier.

Economists call these hidden frictions transaction costs . Ronald Coase's deeper insight was that using markets is itself costly. Finding the person, agreeing on terms, monitoring quality, arranging payment, and trusting the other side all take resources. Firms and intermediaries exist partly because they lower those costs.

Michael Munger usefully groups many of these reductions into three T's: triangulation , finding and matching the parties; transfer , moving the good, service, money, or right to use something; and trust , making people confident enough to trade. If a middleman lowers any of those costs, it has produced value.

Fig 10.5 Intermediaries can create value by reducing the cost of finding, moving, paying, verifying, and trusting. The product is coordination.
The test Producer or tollbooth?
  • A valuable middleman lowers a real cost: search, matching, delivery, payment, verification, timing, or trust.
  • A tollbooth middleman merely inserts itself into a trade and makes nothing easier, safer, faster, or more reliable.
  • The economist's question: did a real cost fall because this firm showed up?
Quick check Why can a payment platform create value even though it does not make the item being purchased? ▸
It can lower transaction costs: safer payment, faster settlement, fraud protection, easier recordkeeping, and greater trust between buyer and seller. Those are real outputs even though no physical product was manufactured.
Retrieval practice Explain the difference between a middleman who creates value and a middleman who is merely a tollbooth. ▸

A value-creating middleman lowers a real cost of exchange: search, matching, delivery, payment, verification, timing, or trust. A tollbooth middleman captures money without lowering any real cost or improving the transaction. The difference is not whether the middleman makes a physical thing; it is whether the production function creates a consumer-valued solution.

Practice — put the model to work

The central AP practice after this chapter is mixed: calculate profit correctly, choose output with MR = MC, and explain value creation in words. Try the compact practice set below before revealing the answers.

On the exam

The moves you can now make

Three question types at the level the exam actually asks: calculate economic profit, choose output, and explain how a firm creates value without a physical product.

Type A · accounting and economic profit

A firm earns $180,000 in total revenue. It pays $120,000 in explicit costs. The owner's time has an opportunity cost of $45,000, and the owner's invested savings could have earned $5,000 elsewhere. Calculate accounting profit and economic profit.

Show worked solution ▸

Accounting profit = 180,000 - 120,000 = $60,000 .

Implicit costs = 45,000 + 5,000 = $50,000 .

Economic profit = 180,000 - 120,000 - 50,000 = $10,000 . The firm is creating more value here than those resources would have created in their next-best use.

Type B · choose the profit-maximizing quantity

A price-taking firm sells each unit for $18. Its marginal costs for units 1 through 6 are $8, $11, $15, $18, $22, and $28. Identify the profit-maximizing quantity and explain your rule.

Show worked solution ▸

Because the firm is a price taker, P = MR = $18. Produce every unit whose MC is less than or equal to $18. Units 1, 2, 3, and 4 pass; unit 5 has MC = $22, so it would lower profit. The profit-maximizing quantity is Q = 4 .

Type C · conceptual explanation

A resale platform connects students who want used textbooks with students who want to sell them. It verifies listings, handles payment, and provides ratings. Explain how the platform creates value even though it does not print textbooks.

Show model answer ▸

The platform lowers transaction costs. It helps buyers and sellers find each other (triangulation), handles payment and exchange (transfer), and uses verification or ratings to make strangers more willing to trade (trust). Those lower costs are a real output. The product is not the textbook; the product is the coordination that makes the trade easier and safer.

Capstone · free response

Put the moves together

AP-style long free response · ~10 points · graph and calculations required · model answer and scoring below

A small meal-prep firm sells each meal for $12 in a competitive market. The owner pays $3,000 per month in explicit costs and gives up a $2,000 monthly salary by running the firm. The firm can produce meals according to the schedule below.

Q (meals) 100 200 300 400 500
MC of next 100 meals $6 $8 $12 $15 $20
  1. If the firm sells 300 meals, calculate total revenue. (1 pt)
  2. At 300 meals, calculate accounting profit and economic profit. (2 pt)
  3. Using the marginal cost schedule and price, identify the profit-maximizing quantity. (2 pt)
  4. Draw a correctly labeled competitive-firm graph showing P = MR, MC, and Q*. (2 pt)
  5. Explain one way the firm could improve its production function without lowering the quality of meals. (1 pt)
  6. Explain why a delivery platform that lowers search and trust costs can be a producer even if it cooks no meals. (2 pt)
Reveal a full-credit response ▸

(a) TR = P x Q = 12 x 300 = $3,600 .

(b) Accounting profit = TR - explicit costs = 3,600 - 3,000 = $600 . Economic profit = 3,600 - 3,000 - 2,000 = -$1,400 .

(c) Produce while P = MR is at least MC. The first 100 meals have MC $6, the second $8, and the third $12. The fourth batch has MC $15, greater than price. So Q* = 300 meals .

(d) The graph should have dollars per meal on the vertical axis and meals on the horizontal axis, a horizontal P = MR line at $12, an upward-sloping MC curve, and Q* marked where P = MC.

(e) The firm might redesign prep stations, improve scheduling, buy better storage, standardize recipes, or use software to reduce waste. Any of these can produce the same quality meals with fewer inputs or less error.

(f) A delivery platform can produce coordination: it helps customers and meal producers find each other, transfers orders and payments, and builds trust through tracking or ratings. Those lower transaction costs are a real output.

Reveal the scoring rubric ▸
  • 1 pt Correct TR calculation: $3,600.
  • 1 pt Correct accounting profit: $600.
  • 1 pt Correct economic profit: -$1,400, subtracting implicit cost.
  • 1 pt Uses P = MR = $12 and compares price to MC.
  • 1 pt Identifies Q* = 300 meals.
  • 1 pt Graph has correct labels and horizontal P = MR.
  • 1 pt Graph marks Q* where P intersects MC.
  • 1 pt Gives a plausible production-function improvement.
  • 1 pt Explains at least one transaction-cost reduction.
  • 1 pt Connects reduced transaction costs to real value creation.
Reveal what loses points ▸
  • Conceptual Calling accounting profit "economic profit" without subtracting the owner's foregone salary.
  • Precision Choosing 400 meals because "more output means more revenue." The rule is marginal: compare the next batch's revenue to the next batch's cost.
  • Exam craft Drawing a firm graph with an unlabeled vertical axis, no P = MR line, or no Q* marked.
Market thesis

Profit-seeking firms create wealth when they turn scarce inputs into solved problems consumers value more than the resources' next-best use. The production function is the machine; innovation improves it; economic profit scores it; MR = MC tells it how much solving to do.

If economists ruled the world...

we would stop saying middlemen "make nothing." A middleman who lowers transaction costs is a producer. A middleman who merely inserts himself and makes nothing easier is a tollbooth. The difference is not physical production; the difference is value creation.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • A production function maps inputs into output, including consumer-valued services and solutions.
  • Innovation improves production by lowering cost, increasing quality, reducing hassle, or some combination.
  • A profit-maximizing firm produces where MR = MC; for a price taker, P = MC.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • When profit appears, did the firm create value, or did it gain from market power, favoritism, fraud, or barriers to entry?
  • When an intermediary takes a fee, did it lower a real transaction cost, or merely place itself between buyer and seller?
Takeaways
In your life

If you want to have a better time at the party, stop monitoring yourself so closely. Notice the problem someone else is having - awkwardness, exclusion, uncertainty - and solve a little of it.

In your work

Do not confuse being busy with creating value. Ask what problem is actually being solved, what resources it uses, and whether the next unit of effort is worth its cost.

Learn more

Production functions: Practice sketching inputs on the horizontal axis and output or value on the vertical axis. Then ask what would shift the function upward.

Profit maximization: Drill small schedules where you calculate TR, MR, MC, and profit, then choose the quantity where MR = MC or P = MC.

Transaction costs: For any platform, broker, wholesaler, or marketplace, ask Munger's three questions: does it improve triangulation, transfer, or trust?
