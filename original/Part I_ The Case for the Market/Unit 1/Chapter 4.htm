Supply and Demand II:
Shifts, Shocks, and Price Controls

First, do no harm

Chapter 4 ~ 38 min read

The date: June 7th, 1971. The speaker: Richard M. Nixon, President of the United States of America. The occasion: a briefing for the public about a new concern and priority for the US government.

President Nixon said, "America's public enemy number one in the United States is drug abuse. In order to fight and defeat this enemy, it is necessary to wage a new, all-out offensive."

With those words, the war on drugs was born.

The last chapter gave us tools to investigate static concerns: snap-shots of supply and demand at one point in time. On that basis, American politicians crafted a very simple drug policy: squelch supply by punishing suppliers with fines, prison, and potentially death. And it worked: many drug kingpins were killed or captured, and the cost inflicted on the drug trade was severe. Escobar, the Cali bosses, then dozens of Mexican capos , removed one after another.

Why, then, are the results of the drug war so abysmal?

In the short run the price of cocaine jumped, just as you'd expect when supply is cut. This was the ideal outcome: with higher prices, fewer people would be involved in drugs.

And then, over the long run, through decades of seizures and fallen kingpins, the drug on American streets did not get scarcer or dearer. Adjusted for purity, it fell to a fraction of where it began. Choke the supply, and the drug got cheaper.

Meanwhile a very different intervention was failing in a very different way. A city, watching rents climb, does the humane and obvious thing: it caps them, so that no landlord may charge more than $1,000 for a one-bedroom. A year on, the tenants who kept their apartments pay less — and everyone else is worse off. Apartments vanish. Waiting lists stretch for years.

To understand this set of ideas, we need more than static snapshots; we need something to compare. This chapter introduces the idea of shifts: the things that make supply and demand move as a curve. It also explains the idea of comparative statics: what happens to the equilibrium price and quantity when there is a shift in either supply or demand?

With these tools in hand, you are ready to understand two key things: in this chapter, we will build on the insight that a market that does not leave prices alone will continue to create distortions. Second, the drug war failed in part because higher prices were an incentive to would-be kingpins. After every capture of a drug lord, another stepped up to the plate as CEO. Squelching supply without squelching demand led to skyrocketing prices, which provided enough incentive for drug runners to keep running drugs.

The big picture
A price is a signal wrapped in an incentive: it tells strangers how scarce a thing is and pays them to act on it. Interfere with that price — by shocking supply, by capping the number, by any of a dozen means — and you push the market off the equilibrium that was coordinating it. Sometimes the market absorbs the blow and re-settles; sometimes a pinned price forbids it to, and the distortion hardens into a permanent shortage. By the end you should be able to shift a curve the right way, predict the new equilibrium for one shift and for two at once, and explain why interfering with a price doesn't erase scarcity — it moves it, hides it, or hands the rationing to something worse.

Major concept 1

Shifts in supply and demand

The one distinction that trips up half of all beginners.

In the last chapter we held the curves perfectly still and let only the price move, sliding up and down fixed lines. We kept promising that the day would come when the lines themselves move. This is that day — and the first line to move is the one from the rooftop. When enforcement removes the sellers of a drug, the whole supply curve picks up and shifts; watching exactly how is the machinery this chapter is made of.

Recall the discipline from Chapter 3: a change in the good's own price never moves a curve — it slides you along one. Everything else is fair game. When something other than the apartment's own rent changes what renters want or what landlords offer, the entire curve picks up and moves. We call that a shift — a change in demand or a change in supply, as opposed to a change in quantity demanded or supplied.

What shifts demand? Anything that changes how much buyers want at every price : their income , the price of a substitute or complement , the number of buyers, tastes, and expectations about future prices. Income cuts two ways: you buy more of a normal good as you grow richer (most things — restaurant meals, travel), but less of an inferior good — instant noodles, the cross-town bus — as you can finally afford the nicer substitute. A big employer opens downtown — more buyers, demand shifts right. Remote work lets people leave — fewer buyers, demand shifts left.

What shifts supply? Anything that changes sellers' costs or numbers: the price of inputs (lumber, labor), technology, taxes and subsidies, the number of sellers, and expectations. A new modular-construction method cuts building costs — supply shifts right. A spike in lumber prices — supply shifts left. And the case from the rooftop: when enforcement jails suppliers, seizes shipments, and raises the risk of dealing, the number of sellers willing to supply at any given price falls. Nothing about buyers changed, and the drug's own price didn't do the moving — the whole supply curve slid left. Interdiction is a supply shock, and now we can read what it does.

One name ties this together. The triggering event itself — the frost, the lumber spike, the new employer downtown — is a shock : a sudden, often unexpected change in one of these underlying forces. A supply shock jolts costs or the number of sellers; a demand shock jolts buyers. Keep the two words straight: the shock is the event out in the world, the shift is what it does to the curve on the page.

The direction is the whole game in this concept. "Right" always means more at every price ; "left" means less at every price. It's worth practicing enough to have that nailed down before moving on.

A fun aside · two goods that break the rule

One caution before we move on: everything above is about what shifts a demand curve. A different question is whether demand always slopes the usual way — less bought as price rises. Almost always, yes. But two famous oddballs break it, and they're too fun to skip.

A Veblen good is wanted because it's expensive: a $30,000 watch, a handbag whose whole point is the price tag. Cut the price and you can kill the appeal — so demand can rise with price. (Named for Thorstein Veblen's "conspicuous consumption.") A Giffen good is rarer and stranger: a cheap staple so central to a poor household's budget that when its price rises, the household is now too poor to afford the pricier foods it used to mix in — so it buys more of the staple. The textbook cases are potatoes or rice for the very poor, though clean real-world Giffen goods are nearly impossible to pin down, and economists still argue over the few candidates. You could imagine rice next to costly meat such that when the price of rice goes up, the family buys more precisely because they can no longer afford meat.

While the ideas behind Veblen and Giffen goods are interesting, you should treat both as curiosities — we flag them so the rule's exceptions don't ambush you, not because they'll be on the exam. What's more, they are very hard to identify in real life. Rather, they are evidence that economists know that their models have limits, and a good economist is willing to investigate just how far their assumptions will hold.

Retrieval practice Give a headline that would shift the supply of apartments left, and one that would shift demand right. Name the shifter in each. ▸

Many answers work. Supply left: "Lumber and labor costs surge" (input costs) or "City adds new building fees" (tax). Demand right: "Tech firm relocates 5,000 jobs downtown" (number of buyers) or "Incomes climb after a boom" (income, normal good). The test is always the same: is the cause something other than the apartment's own rent? If yes, it's a shift, and you only need to decide which curve and which direction. Naming the wrong curve is the usual slip — a tax falls on sellers, so it moves supply , even though buyers feel the resulting price.

Major concept 2

Comparative statics

When two forces move at once, one answer goes dark.

Knowing a curve shifted is half the job. The other half is predicting what happens to equilibrium price and quantity once a curve has shifted. Economists call this comparative statics : compare the market before and after, and read off the new crossing. We'll do it on the same apartment market from Chapter 3 (equilibrium $1,200, 300 units), so you're watching your own market change.

We'll begin with one curve moving . Here the answer is always clean — both price and quantity are determinate. We'll take two single shifts: the supply shock from the rooftop, then a demand shift on your apartment market from Chapter 3.

Worked example Interdiction: a supply shift with a sting in the tail

In this example, we'll use the drug market. To keep the arithmetic clean, say a gram trades at $100, and buyers take 100 units. The government introduces significant enforcement measures, leading suppliers to leave the market out of fear, capture, or death. Supply shifts left. Suppose the new supply crosses that steep demand at $150 and 90 units.

  1. Supply shifts left: at every price, fewer sellers. The crossing climbs the demand curve to a higher price.
  2. Price: $100 → $150 (up 50%). Quantity: 100 → 90. (Drug users won't quit drugs over a mere 50% increase in price, that's not how addictive drugs work.)
  3. Now the sting. Revenue is price × quantity. Before: $100 × 100 = $10,000. After: $150 × 90 = $13,500.
Cutting the supply of drugs raises the revenue flowing to the sellers who remain. Enforcement didn't shrink the prize; it grew it — and hung a larger prize over the market for anyone willing to step in.
Figure A leftward supply shift against steep (inelastic) demand. Price rises far, quantity falls little — and the revenue rectangle for the sellers who remain grows. Cutting supply made the business more lucrative.
Worked example A single shift: the employer arrives (demand right)

A large firm opens downtown, adding renters at every rent. Demand shifts right; suppose quantity demanded rises by 200 at every price, so the new demand is Q d = 1100 − 0.5P. Supply is unchanged at Q s = −300 + 0.5P.

  1. Set new quantity demanded equal to quantity supplied: 1100 − 0.5P = −300 + 0.5P.
  2. Collect terms: 1100 + 300 = P, so 1400 = P.
  3. New equilibrium rent: P = $1,400 (up from $1,200).
  4. Substitute back: Q = −300 + 0.5(1400) = 400 (up from 300).
Demand right → rent rises ($1,200 → $1,400) and quantity rises (300 → 400). With one curve moving, both price and quantity move in known directions.

Now the harder, and more interesting, case: both curves shift at once . This happens constantly in the real world — the employer arrives and construction gets cheaper in the same year. The surprise is that when both move, you can usually pin down only one of price and quantity for certain. The other is indeterminate — its direction depends on which shift is bigger, which the model alone doesn't tell you.

Take both shifting right — more renters and more units. Quantity clearly rises (both forces push it up). But rent? The demand increase pushes rent up; the supply increase pushes it down. Try to predict rent and quantity yourself before opening the example.

Worked example A double shift: both curves move right

Demand right by 200 (Q d = 1100 − 0.5P) and supply right by 200 (Q s = −100 + 0.5P) — equal-sized shifts.

  1. Set equal: 1100 − 0.5P = −100 + 0.5P.
  2. Collect: 1100 + 100 = P, so 1200 = P.
  3. Rent is unchanged at $1,200 — the two pressures exactly cancel.
  4. Substitute: Q = −100 + 0.5(1200) = 500 (up from 300).
Both right, equal size → quantity rises (300 → 500), rent unchanged. But that "unchanged" is an accident of equal shifts. If demand had grown more , rent would rise; if supply had grown more, rent would fall. Quantity is determinate (up); rent is indeterminate .

That is the key AP move, and it generalizes into one small table you should be able to reconstruct from scratch. When the two curves move the same way, quantity is pinned down and price is ambiguous. When they move opposite ways, price is pinned down and quantity is ambiguous.

Retrieval practice Without looking, rebuild the rule: when is price determinate, and when is quantity? ▸

Same-direction shifts (both right or both left) → quantity is determinate, price ambiguous. Opposite-direction shifts (one right, one left) → price is determinate, quantity ambiguous. The intuition: when both forces push quantity the same way, quantity can't be in doubt — but they tug price in opposite directions, so price is the casualty. Flip it for opposite shifts. The single most common error is trying to pin down both variables; with a double shift, one of them is genuinely unknowable from the model alone, and saying so is the correct answer, not a cop-out.

Major concept 3

Price as incentive: Entry and Exit

Why a price that's wrong won't stay wrong for long.

A price does two jobs at the same instant. It is a signal : a single number that compresses an ocean of dispersed facts — how scarce apartments are, how badly people want them, how costly they are to build — into one figure everyone can read at a glance. And it is an incentive : that same number rewards anyone who acts on the information. A high rent doesn't just say "this city is short of housing"; it pays developers to build more and nudges some renters to economize, double up, or look elsewhere. Information and motive, fused into one number. The rent isn't the disease — it's the fever, the body's signal that something needs attention.

Of the two jobs, the incentive is the one that makes the market actually move . Information just sits there unless someone is paid to act on it — and they are. When a price sits above where supply and demand would meet, the reward for selling is unusually fat, so sellers pour in and offer more: owners list the unit they'd been sitting on, landlords convert buildings back to rentals, developers break ground. Economists call this entry . All that new supply piles up as a surplus, and the surplus pushes the price back down . When a price sits below equilibrium, the reward is too thin to bother: sellers cut back, mothball units, or leave the market — exit — and the resulting shortage pushes the price back up . (Entry and exit return as the engine of long-run competition, where they grind profit down to zero in later chapters.)

Here's the key idea: a high, fat price is not a wall around a market, it is a recruitment poster. The reward pulls new sellers in exactly as the model says it must: a captured route is replaced, a fallen supplier's territory is taken up by others, production migrates to wherever enforcement is weakest. Entry swells supply back toward — and, over years, past — where it began, and the price sinks with it. That is why, across decades of rising seizures, purity-adjusted cocaine prices did not climb but fell by roughly four-fifths: the entry the high price paid for eventually swamped the enforcement that raised it.

But watch what the model does not price, in the spirit of the unseen cost from Chapter 1. Entry here is not a peaceful queue of new shopkeepers. When a kingpin is removed, the territory he held does not stay orderly; it fragments, and the fragments fight to inherit it. By one count the number of major organized-crime groups in Mexico rose from roughly five in 2006 to around sixty by 2012, and studies of the "kingpin strategy" find that removing a leader tends to be followed, on average, by about one additional group operating in his former territory — and by a spike in killings. The seen result was an arrest. The unseen result was a war, and a supply chain that barely flinched. That gap between the seen and the unseen is the whole cost of reading a market by its intentions instead of its incentives.

None of this says enforcement is pointless or that the model settles what policy should be — the historical verdict is genuinely contested, and the market's answer to a supply shock is a positive fact, not a recommendation. What the model does say, cleanly, is what to expect : cut the supply of an inelastic good and the short-run price rises, the revenue rises, and the long-run answer is entry. A policy that didn't see that coming was reading the wrong page.

This is why a "wrong" price doesn't stay wrong for long: the gap between the price and equilibrium is itself the incentive that closes it. But notice the fine print — all of that self-correction runs on one condition, that the price is free to move .

The thread from Chapter 3

This is the payoff of Hayek's knowledge problem. The reason no planner can simply set the right price is that the price isn't storing the relevant knowledge anywhere a planner could copy it — the knowledge lives, scattered and fleeting, in millions of separate heads, and the price is the only device that gathers it. Switch the price off and the knowledge doesn't transfer to the planning office. It simply stops being expressed at all.

Retrieval practice The price of a metal suddenly doubles. Name the two jobs that price is now doing. ▸

Signal: the higher price tells everyone, without anyone announcing it, that the metal has become scarcer or more valued — users should economize, find substitutes, recycle. Incentive: the same higher price rewards exactly those responses — a miner who brings more to market profits, a manufacturer who switches to a cheaper alloy saves. The point is that one number does both at once, and to everyone simultaneously, with no central office collecting the information or issuing the orders. A wrong answer usually names only the signal ("it tells people it's scarce") and forgets the incentive — but information no one is paid to act on changes nothing.

Major concept 4

Price controls

You can't cure a fever by breaking the thermometer.

The drug market swallowed the shock: cut its supply and, given time, the reward pulled entry in until the price sank back and then below. This is a price ceiling: a law forbidding any price above some level. Rent control is the classic case. The intuition behind it is humane: rents are too high, so cap them. But recall the two things we've built. A price is a signal (Concept 3), and a shortage cures itself only because the price is free to rise toward equilibrium (Chapter 3). A binding ceiling forbids exactly that rise. So the shortage it creates cannot self-correct. It is permanent.

First, a crucial distinction: a ceiling only does something if it's set below the equilibrium price. A ceiling above equilibrium — say, a $1,400 cap on a market that clears at $1,200 — is non-binding : the market never wanted to go that high, so nothing changes. The cap bites only when set below where the market would clear.

Worked example A binding rent ceiling and the shortage it freezes in

Our market clears at $1,200 (300 units). The city caps rent at $1,000. Using the Chapter 3 schedules: at $1,000, Q d = 900 − 0.5(1000) = 400 and Q s = −300 + 0.5(1000) = 200.

  1. At the $1,000 ceiling, renters want 400 apartments but landlords offer only 200.
  2. Shortage = 400 − 200 = 200 apartments.
  3. In a free market this shortage would bid rent up toward $1,200, erasing itself. The ceiling forbids any rent above $1,000.
  4. So the price can't rise, the 200 missing units never appear, and the shortage stays — month after month.
A binding ceiling ($1,000, below the $1,200 equilibrium) creates a permanent shortage of 200 apartments. The self-correction from Chapter 3 is switched off, because it ran entirely on the price being free to move.
Fig 4.3 A binding rent ceiling at $1,000. Quantity supplied (200) falls short of quantity demanded (400); the gap can't close because rent can't rise.
What to notice: in Chapter 3 a shortage came with an arrow pushing price up toward E. Here the ceiling is a wall the price can't climb — so the arrow is gone and the gap stays open.

If price can't ration the 200 scarce apartments, something else must. That something is non-price rationing , and it is never pretty: years-long waiting lists, apartments passed through personal connections, landlords letting buildings decay because demand far exceeds supply anyway, under-the-table "key money," and black markets. The 1970s gave a vivid cousin of this — when gasoline prices were capped, the shortage showed up as hours-long lines and stations simply running dry.

Step back for the deeper lesson, because it outlasts apartments. A scarce thing will be rationed — that part is not optional. You can ration by price, by queue, by lottery, or by who-you-know, but ration you must, because there isn't enough to go around. Capping the price doesn't abolish the rationing; it only changes the currency you pay in — from dollars to hours in line, favors, and luck. And price has one power none of its replacements share: because it is also an incentive, it doesn't merely decide who gets today's apartments — it pays builders to make more of them tomorrow. A waiting list rations the shortage and freezes it there; a free price rations it and goes to work erasing it. That is the quiet cost of breaking the thermometer.

The mirror image is a price floor — a legal minimum . It binds only when set above equilibrium, and then it produces a persistent surplus instead of a shortage. Agricultural price supports are the textbook case: set a floor under the price of milk above what the market would pay, and you get year-after-year mountains of unsold dairy the government must buy up or store. The most famous floor of all is the minimum wage — a floor under the price of labor — but its effects are genuinely debated and we give it its own chapter.

Common misconception "A price ceiling makes apartments cheaper for everyone." ▸

It makes them cheaper for the people who already have one — and unavailable for everyone still searching. At the $1,000 cap, only 200 apartments are supplied where 400 are wanted; 200 would-be renters who'd happily pay get nothing at all. For them the price didn't fall, it became infinite.

And the lucky 200 don't escape free either. The "true price" they pay re-emerges in forms money can't measure: months on a waiting list, an apartment that never gets repaired, a finder's fee paid in cash, a landlord who rents only to friends. Add up the waiting and the side payments and the decay, and the real cost of a rent-controlled apartment often exceeds what the market rent would have been. The cap didn't lower the price of scarcity. It just changed who pays it, and in what currency — and froze the shortage in place so it never heals.

Research study What rent control actually does — the San Francisco evidence

A widely cited study of San Francisco's 1994 expansion of rent control (Diamond, McQuade & Qian, 2019) found the policy did what the model predicts on both sides. Tenants in covered units were substantially more likely to stay put — real protection, the intended effect. But landlords responded by converting and redeveloping to escape the rules, reducing the supply of rental housing by around 15% — which pushed market rents up citywide for everyone outside the controlled units.

The honest reading: rent control is not a free lunch and not a pure villain either. It transfers real benefits to incumbent tenants while shrinking supply and raising rents for newcomers — exactly the "who pays the scarcity, and in what form" question above. Whether that trade is worth making is a values question, not a settled one, and economists who agree on the mechanism still disagree on the policy . That disagreement is where the readings and policy prompt pick up — it is not something the model alone can resolve.

On the exam

The moves you can now make

Three questions at the level the exam will actually ask — application, not recall. Try each on paper first, then reveal the worked solution. The trying is where the learning happens.

Type A · single shift & comparative statics

In the market for coffee, a frost destroys much of the bean harvest. Identify which curve shifts and in which direction, then state what happens to the equilibrium price and quantity of coffee, and explain why.

Show worked solution ▸

The frost raises the cost of producing coffee (fewer beans, pricier inputs), so it shifts the supply curve left — not demand, and not a slide along supply, because the trigger isn't coffee's own price.

A leftward supply shift moves the equilibrium up the unchanged demand curve: price rises and quantity falls . Intuition: less is available at every price, so buyers bid the price up, and at the higher price fewer cups are bought. The tell that it's a supply story, not a demand story, is that price and quantity move in opposite directions.

Type B · double shift & indeterminacy

In the smartphone market, consumer incomes rise (phones are a normal good) and at the same time a manufacturing breakthrough lowers production costs. Predict the effect on equilibrium price and quantity. If either is indeterminate, say so and explain why.

Show worked solution ▸

Higher incomes shift demand right ; the cheaper process shifts supply right . Both curves move the same direction.

Quantity is determinate : both forces push it up, so equilibrium quantity rises. Price is indeterminate : the demand increase pushes price up while the supply increase pushes it down, and which wins depends on the relative sizes of the two shifts — which the model alone doesn't give us. The correct, full-credit answer names quantity as rising and states explicitly that price cannot be pinned down without knowing which shift is larger. Trying to call price anyway is the classic error.

Type C · price controls

A campus textbook market is described by the equations below, and clears at a price of $80 with a quantity of 500 books:

  • • Demand: Q d = 1100 − 7.5P
  • • Supply: Q s = −100 + 7.5P
The textbook market, with the proposed $60 price cap.

A student government, calling prices unfair, caps the price at the $60 shown above. Determine whether the cap is binding, compute the resulting shortage, and name one form of non-price rationing likely to appear.

Show worked solution ▸
  1. The cap ($60) is below the equilibrium price ($80), so it is binding — it forces the price below where the market would clear.
  2. Quantity demanded at $60: Q d = 1100 − 7.5(60) = 1100 − 450 = 650.
  3. Quantity supplied at $60: Q s = −100 + 7.5(60) = −100 + 450 = 350.
  4. Shortage = Q d − Q s = 650 − 350 = 300 textbooks.
Binding cap → permanent shortage of 300 books — on the graph, the horizontal gap between supply and demand along the $60 line. Non-price rationing fills it: first-come waiting lines, sellouts and back-orders, favoritism, or a resale (black) market above $60. The cap didn't make books cheaper for everyone — only for the 350 who manage to get one.
Capstone · free response

Put the moves together

AP-style long free response · ~10 points · graph required · model answer and scoring below

A coastal city's market for one-bedroom apartments is initially in equilibrium. Then a large technology firm relocates 5,000 employees downtown. Alarmed by rising rents, the city council responds with a rent ceiling.

  1. Draw a correctly labeled supply-and-demand graph for apartments, showing the initial equilibrium rent (R 1 ) and quantity (Q 1 ). (2 pts)
  2. Show the effect of the firm's relocation on your graph, and state what happens to the equilibrium rent and quantity. (2 pts)
  3. Suppose after the relocation demand is Q d = 1100 − 0.5R and supply is Q s = −300 + 0.5R. Calculate the new equilibrium rent and quantity. Show your work. (2 pts)
  4. The council caps rent at $1,000. On your graph, show the ceiling and identify the resulting shortage. Is the ceiling binding? Explain. (3 pts)
  5. Identify one form of non-price rationing likely to emerge under the ceiling. (1 pt)
Reveal a full-credit response ▸

(a) A standard graph: vertical axis Rent ($), horizontal axis Quantity of apartments, downward-sloping D and upward-sloping S crossing at E 1 , with R 1 dropped to the price axis and Q 1 to the quantity axis. (See figure.)

(b) The relocation adds buyers, so demand shifts right to D′. Equilibrium rent rises (R 1 →R 2 ) and equilibrium quantity rises (Q 1 →Q 2 ). Both move up because a demand increase slides the crossing up the unchanged supply curve.

(c) Set Q d = Q s : 1100 − 0.5R = −300 + 0.5R → 1400 = R → R = $1,400. Substitute: Q = −300 + 0.5(1400) = 400 .

(d) The $1,000 ceiling is drawn as a horizontal line below E′ ($1,400). At $1,000, Q d = 1100 − 0.5(1000) = 600 and Q s = −300 + 0.5(1000) = 200, a shortage of 400 (the horizontal gap between S and D′ at the ceiling). The ceiling is binding because it is set below the equilibrium rent of $1,400 — it forces rent under where the market would clear, so the price can't rise to erase the shortage.

(e) Any one of: waiting lists / queues, landlord favoritism, deterioration of unmaintained units, "key money" side payments, or a black market in leases.

Model graph Demand shifts right to D′ (parts a–b), the new equilibrium is computed at $1,400 / 400 (part c), and the $1,000 ceiling sits below it, opening a 400-unit shortage (part d).
Reveal the scoring rubric ▸
  • 1 pt (a) Axes labeled (Rent on vertical, Quantity on horizontal) with a downward D and upward S.
  • 1 pt (a) Initial equilibrium marked where D and S cross, with R 1 and Q 1 indicated.
  • 1 pt (b) Demand shown shifting right (a new curve D′, not a movement along D).
  • 1 pt (b) States both equilibrium rent AND quantity rise .
  • 1 pt (c) Correct equilibrium rent R = $1,400 with work shown (Q d set equal to Q s ).
  • 1 pt (c) Correct equilibrium quantity Q = 400.
  • 1 pt (d) Ceiling drawn as a horizontal line below the new equilibrium.
  • 1 pt (d) Shortage identified as the gap between Q s and Q d at the ceiling (here 400, or shown on the graph).
  • 1 pt (d) States the ceiling is binding AND explains it is below equilibrium.
  • 1 pt (e) Names a valid form of non-price rationing.

Ten independent points — each earned on its own, the way a real reader scores it. A wrong number in (c) doesn't cost you the (d) points if your shortage logic is right given your own equilibrium.

Reveal what loses points ▸
  • Conceptual In (b), drawing a movement along demand instead of a rightward shift — the Chapter 1-of-this-unit trap. A relocation changes the number of buyers, which moves the whole curve, not a point on it.
  • Precision In (c), writing the answer "$1,400" with no equation set up. AP "Calculate" prompts require shown work; a correct number with no work routinely earns nothing.
  • Exam craft In (d), saying "the ceiling causes a shortage" without stating it is binding because it is below equilibrium . The "Explain" verb wants the mechanism, not just the outcome — and an unlabeled ceiling line (no value, no horizontal placement) forfeits the graph point even when the prose is right.
Market thesis

Interfere with a market — by any of many means — and you push it off the equilibrium that was coordinating it. Sometimes the market absorbs the blow, as the drug trade did through entry; sometimes a pinned price forbids it to, and the distortion gets a whole lot worse.

If economists ruled the world…

they'd treat a price as information before treating it as a target — because a supply shock, a cap, a tax, a quota all push a market off equilibrium, and the distortion is often not the one intended. Drugs, gas, and rent are three of many cases; the chapters ahead measure and catalogue the rest.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Every intervention moves a market off equilibrium and pays for it in distortion; a binding ceiling makes a lasting shortage, a floor a lasting surplus, and a supply shock a price the market then unwinds through entry.
  • Rent control protects today's tenants — at the cost of less housing and higher rents for everyone else.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Is protecting today's tenants worth shrinking tomorrow's housing?
  • Distortion is not the same as "bad": some are worth their cost. That is Chapter 3's rule — override a price only with a reason — and Chapter 6 puts a number on the cost so the reason can be weighed.
