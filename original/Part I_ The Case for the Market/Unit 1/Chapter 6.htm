Trade Part II:
Welfare Analysis, Shortages and Surpluses

Also, the deadweight loss of Christmas

Chapter 6 ~ 46 min read

You spend $50 on a sweater for your cousin. He smiles, says thanks, and quietly would never have paid more than $30 for it himself. Something just happened that's worth pausing on: $50 of resources went in, $30 of value came out, and $20 simply… evaporated. Not stolen, not transferred to anyone — gone. No one is $20 richer for it. The world is poorer by twenty dollars and a sweater nobody quite wanted.

In 1993 an economist named Joel Waldfogel did the arithmetic on this for the whole holiday season and published it under the magnificently grumpy title "The Deadweight Loss of Christmas." His finding: gift recipients typically value what they're given at something like a tenth to a third less than the giver paid — and across an economy, that adds up to tens of billions of dollars of value destroyed every December. He later called the holiday "an organized institution for value destruction." Bah, humbug, with a regression.

This is a Scrooge-ish way to open a chapter, but it points at something profound and genuinely useful: economists can measure the value a trade creates, in dollars, off a supply-and-demand graph. Last chapter you watched trade create value but could only describe it; this chapter hands you the ruler. And once you can measure the value created, you can measure the value destroyed when the right trades don't happen — by a clumsy gift, a price control, a tax, a monopoly. That measurement is the payoff of everything in Unit 1. It's how we put a number on "markets create value," and on what it costs when they're blocked.

The big picture
Last chapter showed that every voluntary trade creates value; this chapter measures it. A buyer who'd have paid more than the price pockets the difference (consumer surplus); a seller who'd have accepted less keeps the rest (producer surplus); together they are total surplus, the value the market creates. The market equilibrium maximizes that total — this is what "efficiency" means, and the equilibrium turns out to be efficient in two senses at once: it maximizes total surplus and it is Pareto efficient. But efficiency is only about the size of the pie, never about how fairly it's sliced. And when something blocks trades that should happen — most starkly, a price the law won't let move — total surplus shrinks: the lost value is deadweight loss, gone to no one. By the end you should be able to compute consumer and producer surplus as areas, explain why equilibrium is efficient, and measure the deadweight loss of a distortion — including a binding price ceiling or floor.

Major concept 1 · This is how we measure it all

Consumer and producer surplus

Last chapter ended on a promise: that the gains from trade, which we could only describe, could be measured — in dollars, right off a supply-and-demand graph. This is the chapter that measures them. It builds the one ruler the rest of Unit 1 runs on: lay it against a working market and it reads off the value created; lay it against a blocked one and it reads off the value destroyed. Surplus is that ruler.

One thing to settle first, because the phrase "gains from trade" has quietly grown. Last chapter the gains came from specialization — Crusoe fishing, Friday gathering, each doing what they sacrifice least to make. Ch 5 But value is also created whenever a buyer and seller simply exchange , with no specializing at all: if you'd happily pay $90 for a thing and I'd let it go for $45, the trade manufactures value out of the gap between us. These are two engines — gains from specialization and gains from exchange — and they are not rivals; they compound. You specialize to make a thing at low cost, then exchange it with someone who values it more than you do, and value is created at both steps. The tool we build now measures both , because both arrive on the graph as the same shape: a wedge between what a unit is worth to a buyer and what it costs a seller.

Start with the demand curve, and recall what Chapter 2 taught us it really is. Ch 2 Each point on a demand curve is somebody's willingness to pay — the marginal value of that unit to that buyer. The curve slopes down because, one buyer to the next and one unit to the next, that value falls. So the demand curve is a ranked list of what each unit is worth to the people who want it.

Now suppose the market price is $60 and you'd have happily paid $90. You buy, hand over $60, and walk away with $30 of value you didn't have to pay for. That $30 is your consumer surplus — the value you captured beyond what you spent. Add up that gap for every buyer in the market and you get total consumer surplus: on the graph, it's the area below the demand curve and above the price .

Sellers have a mirror-image story. The supply curve is each seller's willingness to accept — the least they'd take, which is their cost of making that unit. A seller who'd have sold for $45 but receives $60 keeps $15 of producer surplus . Summed across sellers, that's the area above the supply curve and below the price . Put the two together — consumer surplus plus producer surplus — and you have total surplus : the entire value the market created by letting these trades happen.

Fig 6.1 The anchor market: demand P = 100 − 2Q, supply P = 40 + Q, clearing at $60 and 20 units. Consumer surplus is the upper triangle; producer surplus the lower.
What to notice: the two triangles together are every dollar of value this market created. The wedge between what buyers would pay (demand) and what sellers would accept (supply), out to the equilibrium quantity — that whole area is the gains from trade, made visible.
Worked example Computing surplus as triangle areas

Using the anchor market — demand P = 100 − 2Q, supply P = 40 + Q, equilibrium at P = $60, Q = 20 — find consumer and producer surplus.

  1. Each surplus is a right triangle, so use area = ½ × base × height. The base is the equilibrium quantity, 20.
  2. Consumer surplus height = (demand's price-intercept) − (price) = 100 − 60 = 40. So CS = ½ × 20 × 40 = $400.
  3. Producer surplus height = (price) − (supply's price-intercept) = 60 − 40 = 20. So PS = ½ × 20 × 20 = $200.
  4. Total surplus = CS + PS = 400 + 200 = $600.
CS = $400, PS = $200, total surplus = $600. The base is the same for both triangles (the quantity traded); only the heights differ — each height is the vertical distance from the price to that curve's intercept.
Quick check A buyer would pay $25 for a concert ticket and gets in for $18. What's their consumer surplus? ▸
$25 − $18 = $7. They captured $7 of value beyond what they paid.
Quick check Solo: A market clears at P = $50, Q = 30. The demand price-intercept is $110. What is consumer surplus? ▸
Height = 110 − 50 = 60; base = 30. CS = ½ × 30 × 60 = $900.
Quick check Solo: Same market (P = $50, Q = 30). The supply price-intercept is $20. Find producer surplus and total surplus. ▸
PS height = 50 − 20 = 30; PS = ½ × 30 × 30 = $450. Total surplus = 900 + 450 = $1,350.
Retrieval practice Why is the demand curve the right tool for measuring the value buyers get — and how does Chapter 2 explain its shape? ▸

Because the height of the demand curve at each quantity is a buyer's willingness to pay — the marginal value of that unit to them, which Chapter 2 showed is just its marginal utility expressed in dollars. Ch 2 So the vertical gap between the demand curve and the price is, unit by unit, value received minus value paid: exactly the surplus a buyer captures. The curve slopes down because of diminishing marginal utility — each additional unit is worth less — which is why the surplus triangle narrows toward the equilibrium. A wrong answer often confuses willingness to pay with the price actually paid; the whole point of surplus is that they differ, and the gap is the value the market hands the buyer for free.

Major concept 2 · Why the market maximizes the pie

Efficiency — and what it deliberately ignores

Here is the quietly remarkable thing about the equilibrium quantity: it maximizes total surplus. Not approximately — exactly. To see why, think about each unit. As long as a buyer values the next unit more than it costs a seller to make it (demand above supply), that trade creates surplus, so it should happen — and at the market price, it does. The moment the cost of making one more exceeds what anyone will pay for it (supply above demand), the trade would destroy value, so it shouldn't happen — and at the market price, it doesn't. The equilibrium is precisely the point where every value-creating trade has occurred and no value-destroying one has. Produce less and you leave good trades on the table; produce more and you force bad ones. Either way total surplus falls.

It's worth being precise about the word, because "efficiency" names a small family of related ideas. What we just described — producing exactly the quantity where the marginal benefit of the last unit equals its marginal cost, so total surplus is maximized — is allocative efficiency : the right amount is made, and it flows to the people who value it most. (A cousin, productive efficiency , asks a different question — whether each unit is made as cheaply as possible; that's a story for the chapters on firms. Ch 9 )

There's a second lens that lands on the very same point. Last chapter we met the Pareto test: an outcome is Pareto efficient when no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off — when every gain from trade has been captured and no costless improvement remains. Ch 5 The competitive equilibrium passes this test. At any other quantity, an unrealized trade sits on the table — a buyer who values the good above its cost — so you could make that buyer and a seller better off while harming no one: a Pareto improvement still available. Only at the equilibrium are they all used up. So the market's crossing point is efficient in both senses at once — it maximizes total surplus and it is Pareto efficient. That an unguided market reaches this is striking enough to be one of the few results economists dignify with the word "theorem." But hold the wonder, because the caveat from last chapter returns at full force.

This is the measurable version of the gains-from-trade idea from Ch 5 and the spontaneous order of Ch 3 , now with a dollar figure attached: Adam Smith's invisible hand, weighed and counted.

Efficiency is the size of the pie — never how it's sliced

This is the most important caveat in Unit 1, and economists state it plainly: efficiency is only about the size of the pie. It says the market produces the largest possible total surplus — and absolutely nothing about whether the people who get that surplus deserve it, need it, or already have plenty. An outcome can be perfectly efficient and grotesquely unfair. A market that efficiently allocates life-saving medicine to whoever can pay maximizes total surplus while a poorer, sicker person goes without; the pie is as big as possible, and the slicing may be monstrous. The same is true of the Pareto test, despite its reassuring name: an allocation in which one person holds nearly everything can still be Pareto efficient, because you can't make anyone else better off without taking from them. Efficiency, in either sense, simply does not see fairness.

Economists lean hard on efficiency anyway, and for a real reason: a bigger pie means there is more to go around, so in principle everyone could be made better off. Growth-first instincts come from there. But "could be" is doing heavy lifting — efficiency guarantees the pie is big, not that anyone actually shares it. That second question — equity , how the pie is divided — is partly a question of values, not something a surplus calculation can settle, which is exactly why reasonable people land in different places on it. Keep the two apart and you'll think clearly about policy for the rest of your life: is this efficient? and is this fair? are different questions, and a serious answer needs both. We'll feel this tension at full strength when we reach the genuinely hard cases. Ch 18 Ch 20

Quick check A market produces fewer units than the equilibrium quantity. Is total surplus larger or smaller than at equilibrium, and why? ▸
Smaller. Some trades where buyers value the unit more than it costs to make are not happening — value-creating trades left undone. Total surplus is maximized only at the equilibrium quantity.
Quick check At a quantity below equilibrium, is a Pareto improvement still available? ▸
Yes — there's an unmade trade where the buyer values the unit above the seller's cost. Letting it happen makes both better off and harms no one. Only at the equilibrium are all such improvements exhausted, which is why it's Pareto efficient.
Quick check "This outcome is efficient, so it must be fair." What's wrong with that sentence? ▸
Efficiency — total surplus or Pareto — is only about the size and exhaustion of the gains, not their distribution. An outcome can maximize the pie and still divide it in a way many would call unfair. Efficient and fair are separate questions.
Retrieval practice Why do economists put so much weight on efficiency if it ignores fairness entirely? ▸

Because a larger total surplus means there is more value in existence to potentially share — an efficient outcome makes it possible for everyone to be better off than an inefficient one, even if it doesn't guarantee they will be. Inefficiency, by contrast, destroys value that no one gets, which is hard to defend on any grounds: it makes the losers worse off without making anyone better off. So efficiency is a sensible first screen — "are we wasting value?" — and a natural starting point for analysis. But it's only a first screen. It deliberately brackets the question of who ends up with the surplus, and that question — equity — depends on values a calculation can't supply. The honest position is that economics is unusually good at measuring the size of the pie and has no special authority over how it should be divided.

Major concept 3 · Value that vanishes

Deadweight loss: the triangle of trades that didn't happen

If the equilibrium quantity maximizes total surplus, then anything that pushes the market away from it must shrink the pie. The lost surplus has a name: deadweight loss — value that disappears, captured by no one. This is the crucial difference from a simple transfer. When a tax moves $10 from your pocket to the government's, that's a transfer: you lose, they gain, the surplus still exists somewhere. Deadweight loss is worse — it's surplus that stops existing at all, because the trade that would have created it never happens.

Picture our anchor market with something holding the quantity at 10 instead of the efficient 20. Every unit between 10 and 20 was a trade where the buyer valued the good more than it cost to make — a trade that should have happened and created surplus. Block them, and that surplus is simply gone. It shows up on the graph as a triangle between the demand and supply curves, from the restricted quantity out to the efficient one. (In the next concept we'll see that "something holding the quantity at 10" is, concretely, a price the law won't let move.)

Fig 6.2 The same market, quantity choked to 10. The shaded triangle between demand and supply — the surplus those lost trades would have created — is deadweight loss.
What to notice: at the restricted quantity, buyers still value the good ($80) far above what it costs to make ($50) — a $30 gap of unrealized gains. Those trades just don't happen, so the surplus is gone, transferred to no one.
Worked example Computing deadweight loss as a triangle

The anchor market (demand P = 100 − 2Q, supply P = 40 + Q, efficient Q = 20) is restricted to Q = 10. Find the deadweight loss.

  1. Find the gap between willingness to pay and cost at the restricted quantity. At Q = 10: demand price = 100 − 2(10) = 80; supply price = 40 + 10 = 50.
  2. That vertical gap is the triangle's height: 80 − 50 = 30.
  3. The base is the quantity of lost trades — from the restricted 10 out to the efficient 20: 20 − 10 = 10.
  4. Deadweight loss = ½ × base × height = ½ × 10 × 30 = $150.
Deadweight loss = $150. Total surplus was $600 at the efficient quantity; the restriction destroys $150 of it — value that simply ceases to exist, benefiting no one. (The triangle always points to the efficient equilibrium, where the gap closes to zero.)
Quick check Solo: A market's efficient quantity is 50. A regulation holds it at 35. At Q = 35, willingness to pay is $40 and marginal cost is $22. Find the deadweight loss. ▸
Height = 40 − 22 = 18; base = 50 − 35 = 15. DWL = ½ × 15 × 18 = $135.
Quick check Why is deadweight loss worse than a tax that simply transfers money from buyers to the government? ▸
A transfer moves surplus from one party to another — it still exists. Deadweight loss is surplus that stops existing entirely, because the value-creating trade never happens. Nobody captures it.
Quick check Back to the hook: you pay $50 for a gift your cousin values at $30. Where's the deadweight loss, and why did it happen? ▸
The $20 gap is destroyed value — your cousin would have spent the $50 on something worth at least $50 to him, but the gift is worth only $30. The cause is information: you don't know his preferences as well as he does. Cash (or a gift card) avoids it by letting him choose.

That clumsy gift is deadweight loss in a Santa hat: a "trade" where the giver guesses wrong about the recipient's willingness to pay, so value is destroyed. The fix is the one Chapter 2 implied — let people choose for themselves, because no one knows your marginal utility better than you. Cash isn't unromantic; it's efficient. (Whether efficiency is what Christmas is for is, fittingly, a question economics can't answer.) But the gift is the gentle case. The next concept turns the same triangle on the policy that produces it most reliably — a price the government won't let move.

Major concept 4 · Forcing the price

Price controls: locking the inefficiency in place

Begin with a question the last three concepts now let us answer in a sharper way: what happens when the price is simply wrong ? Suppose it sits above the equilibrium — too high. Sellers, tempted by the high price, want to sell more than buyers want to buy; unsold units pile up as a surplus . Or suppose it sits below equilibrium — too low. Buyers want more than sellers will provide, and a shortage opens. We met the mechanics of both back in Chapter 3. Ch 3 What we can add now is the welfare reading: a price away from equilibrium isn't just out of balance — it's inefficient . Value-creating trades are going unmade, total surplus sits below its maximum, and a deadweight-loss triangle has opened.

In a free market, none of this lasts. A surplus has sellers shading their prices down to move stock; a shortage has buyers bidding the price up. The price slides back to the crossing, the triangle closes, and efficiency is restored — that was Chapter 3's lesson, now with a welfare price tag. Disequilibrium is a force, not a resting place. So the inefficiency of a "wrong" price is, in a free market, temporary: the market corrects itself.

Unless it can't. A price control is a law that forbids the price from moving — and it is precisely the case where the inefficiency does not self-correct. It is held open, by force of law, indefinitely. There are two kinds, mirror images of each other:

Price ceiling — a legal maximum
"You may not charge more than this." Bites only when set below equilibrium (a ceiling above the market price is irrelevant — non-binding). A binding ceiling locks in a permanent shortage . Rent control is the classic case. Ch 4
Price floor — a legal minimum
"You may not charge less than this." Bites only when set above equilibrium. A binding floor locks in a permanent surplus . Agricultural price supports and the minimum wage (a floor on the price of labor) are the classic cases.

Either way, the controlled price stops the two quantities from meeting, and one side ends up rationed. The rule for how much actually trades is simple and worth memorizing: the short side wins. Whichever quantity is smaller at the controlled price — what sellers will supply under a ceiling, or what buyers will buy under a floor — is the amount that actually changes hands. Because that amount is always below the efficient quantity, a binding control always destroys surplus. The unmade trades between the controlled quantity and the efficient one are a deadweight-loss triangle, exactly the one from the last concept — only now we know its cause has a name.

Fig 6.3 A binding price ceiling of $50 on the anchor market. Sellers supply only 10; buyers want 25; a shortage of 15 opens, and just 10 units trade — the gold triangle is the deadweight loss.
What to notice: the "something holding quantity at 10" from the last concept was this — a $50 ceiling. The short side (sellers, at 10) sets the quantity traded; the gap to the efficient 20 is the same $150 of destroyed surplus.
Worked example The deadweight loss of a binding price ceiling

Anchor market (demand P = 100 − 2Q, supply P = 40 + Q, equilibrium $60/20). The government sets a price ceiling of $50. Find the quantity traded, the shortage, and the deadweight loss.

  1. Is it binding? $50 is below the $60 equilibrium, so yes — it bites.
  2. Quantity supplied at $50 (from supply): 50 = 40 + Q → Q s = 10. Quantity demanded at $50 (from demand): 50 = 100 − 2Q → Q d = 25.
  3. Shortage = Q d − Q s = 25 − 10 = 15. The short side (sellers) wins, so only 10 units actually trade.
  4. Deadweight loss: at the traded quantity 10, demand price = 80, supply price = 50, so height = 30; base = efficient 20 − traded 10 = 10. DWL = ½ × 10 × 30 = $150.
10 units trade, a shortage of 15, deadweight loss = $150. Note what else happens but isn't deadweight loss: the buyers who still get the good now pay $50 instead of $60, so surplus shifts from sellers to them — a transfer, not destruction. The $150 triangle is the part that benefits no one.
Worked example (faded) The mirror case: a binding price floor

Same market. Now a price floor of $70 (think an agricultural support price). The mechanical setup is filled; you finish the deadweight loss.

  1. Binding? $70 is above the $60 equilibrium → yes, it bites, and creates a surplus.
  2. At $70: quantity demanded 70 = 100 − 2Q → Q d = 15; quantity supplied 70 = 40 + Q → Q s = 30. Surplus = 30 − 15 = 15.
  3. Short side now is buyers (15), so 15 units trade. Height at Q = 15: demand price 70 − supply price 55 = 15. Base = 20 − 15 = 5.
Deadweight loss = ½ × base × height = ½ × 5 × ______ = $______. (Answer: ½ × 5 × 15 = $37.50. A surplus of 15 piles up; only 15 trade instead of the efficient 20; the unmade 5 trades are the destroyed surplus.)
Quick check A city sets a rent ceiling above the current market rent. What happens? ▸
Nothing — it's non-binding. A ceiling only bites when it's below equilibrium; set above, the market price is already legal, so the control does nothing. (The mirror holds for floors: a floor below equilibrium is non-binding.)
Quick check Under a binding price ceiling, which side determines how much actually trades — buyers or sellers? ▸
Sellers. At the low capped price, the quantity supplied is the smaller of the two, and the short side wins — you can't buy what isn't offered. (Under a binding floor, it flips: buyers are the short side.)
Quick check Solo: Demand P = 120 − 2Q, supply P = 30 + Q (equilibrium P = $60, Q = 30). A price ceiling of $45 is imposed. Find the quantity traded and the deadweight loss. ▸
At $45: Q s = 45 − 30 = 15 (trades); Q d = (120 − 45)/2 = 37.5. Height at Q = 15: demand 120 − 30 = 90, supply 45, gap = 45; base = 30 − 15 = 15. DWL = ½ × 15 × 45 = $337.50.
If controls are inefficient, why do governments use them?

Because efficiency was never the only goal — and Concept 2 already told us it can't be. Price controls are almost always reached for on equity grounds: to protect tenants from rising rents, farmers from collapsing crop prices, workers from low wages. The deadweight loss is the efficiency cost; whether the fairness gain is worth that cost is exactly the efficiency-versus-equity judgment, and it turns on values a surplus triangle can't supply. An honest analyst names the cost and takes the goal seriously, rather than pretending one of them away.

Two more honest caveats. First, the model assumes a competitive market with many price-taking buyers and sellers; where that fails — a single dominant employer setting wages, for instance — a floor can behave differently, which is why the minimum wage is a genuine empirical dispute rather than a settled diagram. Ch 17 Ch 18 Second, a shortage doesn't vanish just because the price can't ration it — it gets rationed some other way: queues, waiting lists, declining quality, favoritism, black markets. Those substitutes burn real resources (hours in line are gone for good), so the true cost of a binding ceiling is often larger than the triangle alone.

Common misconception "A price ceiling on rent helps tenants — it makes housing cheaper." ▸

It helps some tenants — the ones who already hold a rent-controlled apartment now pay less. But "cheaper" hides what the diagram shows: at the capped price, landlords supply fewer units (convert to condos, stop building, let quality slide) while more renters want in, so a chronic shortage opens. The tenant lucky enough to have a lease wins; the renter who can't find one at any legal price loses entirely, and the housing that's never built is pure deadweight loss.

So the honest verdict is mixed, not "helps tenants" full stop: it redistributes from landlords to incumbent tenants (a transfer, arguably the point), while shrinking the total supply of housing and destroying value through shortage and rationing. Whether that trade is worth it is the equity-versus-efficiency question — but you can't even weigh it if you believe a ceiling simply makes housing cheaper for everyone. It doesn't; it makes housing scarcer , and cheaper only for whoever's already inside.

Retrieval practice Why does every binding price control create deadweight loss — ceiling or floor, no exceptions? ▸

Because both push the quantity actually traded below the efficient quantity. A binding ceiling sits below equilibrium, so sellers supply less; a binding floor sits above equilibrium, so buyers buy less; either way the short side rules and fewer units change hands than at the crossing. The trades that vanish — between the controlled quantity and the efficient one — are all value-creating: the buyer valued the unit above the seller's cost. That surplus is destroyed, captured by no one, which is the definition of deadweight loss. The control may also transfer surplus between buyers and sellers (that's the redistribution it's usually after), but the shrunken quantity guarantees a triangle of pure loss on top of any transfer. The only way a control avoids deadweight loss is to be non-binding — i.e., to do nothing at all. Ch 4

Market thesis

The wedge between what buyers will pay and what sellers will accept is the value a market creates — and we can measure it to the dollar, including the value that vanishes when a price is forced to stand still.

If economists ruled the world…

they would let supply and demand set the price — and could tell you, in dollars, exactly what is lost every time a law freezes it in place.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Total surplus is maximized at the competitive equilibrium — that's what efficiency means, and it's Pareto efficient too.
  • Any binding price control pushes quantity below the efficient level, destroying surplus as deadweight loss.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • If we destroy some surplus to steer resources toward people we think need them more, how big a loss is too big to be fair?
  • Efficiency makes the pie biggest; it's silent on who deserves the slices. When is taking from some to help others worth the value lost?
Takeaways
In your life

When you pay less for something than it's worth to you, that gap is consumer surplus — a quiet reason trade keeps making you richer, even when you never see the bill for it.

In your work

When a policy blocks or forces trades — cap, floor, quota, mandate — look for the deadweight-loss triangle: the value-creating trades that now won't happen. That's the real cost, separate from money that merely changes hands.
