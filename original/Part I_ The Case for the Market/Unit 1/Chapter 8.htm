Distortions II & Unit Capstone:
Subsidies and the Case of Education

Signals, Subsidies, or Scholarship?

Chapter 8 ~ 40 min read

College is expensive, and college is good, so the government helps students pay for it — tens of billions of dollars a year in subsidized loans and grants. The logic is so obvious it feels rude to question: lower the cost of college for students, and more of them can afford it. Who could object?

Here is the uncomfortable possibility. Over the same decades that aid expanded, the sticker price of college didn't fall — it roughly tripled, far outpacing inflation. In 1987, President Reagan's education secretary William Bennett wrote a provocative op-ed arguing this was no coincidence: increases in federal aid, he claimed, "enabled colleges and universities blithely to raise their tuitions," confident the loans would cushion the blow. The aid meant to help students, he argued, was being quietly captured by the schools as higher prices — leaving students with more debt against a bigger bill.

Whether Bennett was right about how much is genuinely disputed, and we'll be honest about that. But the mechanism he described is not mysterious or ideological — it's the exact machinery of Chapter 7, run in reverse. A subsidy is a tax with the sign flipped, and it obeys the same iron law: the side that can't escape captures the benefit, just as the side that couldn't escape bore the tax. When you subsidize demand for something whose supply can't easily grow, you don't make it cheaper for buyers. You bid up its price, and the sellers pocket the difference. This chapter — the capstone of our case for markets — is about why "it's good, so let's subsidize it" can defeat itself, and what the market would do instead if we let it.

The big picture
A per-unit subsidy is the mirror image of a tax: it drives a wedge where sellers receive more than buyers pay, the government covers the gap, and the quantity traded rises . Who captures the benefit follows the same elasticity rule as tax incidence, flipped — the more inelastic (stuck) side captures the larger share. So subsidizing demand against inelastic supply mostly raises the price and enriches sellers rather than helping the intended buyers. By the end you should be able to compute a subsidy's effect on quantity and prices, find the government's cost, determine who captures the subsidy from the elasticities, and reason about why subsidizing a supply-constrained market can backfire — the strongest version of the case for letting markets work.

Major concept 1 · The mirror

A subsidy is a tax, run in reverse

Everything you learned about taxes, pointed the other way.

You already know how this works — you just learned it pointed the other way. In Chapter 7, a tax drove a wedge that made buyers pay more than sellers received, and shrank the quantity. Ch 7 A subsidy does the exact opposite: it drives a wedge that lets sellers receive more than buyers pay, with the government covering the gap, and the quantity grows . Where the tax wedge pushed the two prices apart with a gap the government collected , the subsidy wedge pushes them apart with a gap the government pays .

Watch it on the same market we've used all unit — demand P = 100 − 2Q, supply P = 40 + Q, which cleared at $60 and 20 units. Ch 6 A $15-per-unit subsidy means sellers now get $15 more than buyers hand over, so they're willing to supply at any buyer-price $15 below their old cost. The effective supply shifts down by $15 — the mirror of the tax, which shifted it up. The market grows to a larger quantity, buyers pay less, sellers receive more, and the government pays the difference on every unit.

Fig 8.1 A $15 subsidy on the same market. Quantity rises from 20 to 25; sellers receive $65, buyers pay $50. Compare Fig 7.1 — the wedge is identical, just flipped.
What to notice: the subsidy splits just like a tax. Here buyers gain $10 (60 → 50) and sellers gain $5 (60 → 65) — the same $10/$5 split as the tax in Ch 7, because the elasticities are the same. The side that bore more of the tax now captures more of the subsidy.
Worked example A subsidy's effect — mind the sign flip

Market: demand P = 100 − 2Q, supply P = 40 + Q (equilibrium $60, 20 units). Apply a $15-per-unit subsidy. Find the new quantity, the price buyers pay, the price sellers receive, and the government's cost.

  1. A subsidy lowers the price sellers need from buyers by $15 — so subtract from the supply intercept (the mirror of a tax, which adds): effective supply P = 40 + Q − 15 = 25 + Q.
  2. New quantity where demand meets subsidized supply: 100 − 2Q = 25 + Q → 75 = 3Q → Q = 25. (Quantity rose, as a subsidy should.)
  3. Price buyers pay (on demand): P = 100 − 2(25) = $50. Price sellers receive: buyers' $50 + $15 subsidy = $65. (Check on original supply: 40 + 25 = $65. ✓)
  4. Government's cost = subsidy × quantity = $15 × 25 = $375 (a rectangle the government pays out ).
Q = 25; buyers pay $50, sellers receive $65; government spends $375. The one trap: a subsidy subtracts from the supply price where a tax adds. Get the sign right and the rest is identical to Chapter 7.
Quick check A tax raises the price gap the government collects; a subsidy opens a gap the government does what with? ▸
Pays out. With a subsidy, sellers receive more than buyers pay, and the government covers the difference on every unit — the mirror of collecting tax revenue.
Quick check Solo: Demand P = 120 − 3Q, supply P = 30 + Q, with a $16 subsidy. Find the new quantity and the price buyers pay. (Hint: subtract the subsidy from the supply intercept.) ▸
Subsidized supply: P = 14 + Q. Set 120 − 3Q = 14 + Q → 106 = 4Q → Q = 26.5. Buyers pay P = 120 − 3(26.5) = $40.50. (Sellers receive $40.50 + $16 = $56.50.)
Quick check Solo: In the worked example (subsidy $15, new Q = 25), what is the government's total cost, and is it collected or paid? ▸
$15 × 25 = $375, paid out by the government. (Contrast the tax in Ch 7, where the same-size rectangle was revenue collected.)
Retrieval practice In what ways is a subsidy the exact mirror of a tax, and where's the one sign you must flip? ▸

Both open a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive, and in both cases who captures the benefit (or bears the burden) is set by relative elasticities, not by law. The mirror: a tax makes buyers pay more than sellers get, shrinks quantity, and the government collects a revenue rectangle; a subsidy makes sellers receive more than buyers pay, grows quantity, and the government pays a cost rectangle. The one sign to flip in the algebra is the supply intercept: a tax adds to the price sellers require (supply shifts up), a subsidy subtracts (supply shifts down). Everything else — find the new quantity by setting demand equal to the adjusted supply, read the buyer price off demand, recover the seller price by adding back the subsidy — is identical. The deep continuity is that both are just wedges, and a wedge always splits according to who's stuck. Ch 7

Major concept 2 · Who captures it

Subsidize the stuck side, and the stuck side wins

Worthiness doesn't decide who gets the money. Elasticity does.

Here's the rule that makes this chapter matter, and it's just Chapter 7's incidence rule wearing different clothes: the more inelastic side captures the larger share of a subsidy — exactly the side that would have borne the larger share of a tax. Stuck means you can't walk away, and the side that can't walk away both eats the tax and pockets the subsidy. So the entire question "will this subsidy actually help the people it's aimed at?" reduces to one prior question: which side here is stuck?

Now the case that runs the whole chapter. Subsidize buyers — students, with loans — in a market where supply is inelastic — universities that can't or won't add seats quickly, their prestige bound up in scarcity. Buyers can spend the aid; sellers can't easily produce more in response. So the stuck side is the sellers, and the sellers capture the subsidy: the extra demand bids up the price, tuition rises, and much of the aid lands in the schools' budgets rather than lowering the real cost to students. The intended beneficiary is not the actual beneficiary. Watch the same wedge, now in a steep-supply market:

Fig 8.2 A $10 subsidy where supply is steep (inelastic). The price sellers receive jumps from $82 to $90; buyers' price barely falls, $82 to $80.
What to notice: of the $10 subsidy, sellers capture $8 and buyers get just $2. Steep (inelastic) supply means the stuck sellers pocket almost the whole benefit — the demand subsidy mostly inflates the price. This is the Bennett mechanism, drawn.
Worked example (faded) Splitting a subsidy by elasticity

In Fig 8.2, a $10 subsidy moves the price sellers receive from $82 to $90 and the price buyers pay from $82 to $80. The arithmetic is filled; you draw the conclusion.

  1. Seller capture = new seller price − old price = 90 − 82 = $8.
  2. Buyer benefit = old price − new buyer price = 82 − 80 = $2. (Check: 8 + 2 = $10, the whole subsidy. ✓)
Because supply is ______ (inelastic/elastic), the ______ capture the lion's share — $8 of the $10 — while buyers get only $2. (Answer: inelastic; sellers. When the stuck side is the seller, a subsidy aimed at buyers mostly raises the price and enriches sellers.)
Quick check Solo: A subsidy is given to home buyers in a city where almost no new housing can be built (very inelastic supply). Who captures most of the subsidy, and what happens to house prices? ▸
Sellers (existing homeowners) capture most of it; prices rise. With supply unable to grow, the extra demand bids up prices, so the subsidy mostly inflates home values rather than helping buyers afford homes.
Quick check Solo: The same homebuyer subsidy, but now in a city that can build new housing freely (elastic supply). Who benefits now? ▸
Buyers. With elastic supply, builders respond to the subsidy by adding homes rather than letting the price jump, so the subsidy reaches buyers as intended and prices stay close to before.
Quick check State the general rule connecting this chapter to Chapter 7 in one sentence. ▸
The inelastic (stuck) side bears the larger share of a tax and captures the larger share of a subsidy — it's the same rule, because both are just wedges that split by who can't escape.
Research study Was Bennett right? Mechanism vs. magnitude

The mechanism above isn't in dispute — economists agree that subsidizing demand against inelastic supply pushes up price. What's genuinely contested is how much of the real-world tuition spiral the Bennett effect explains. A widely cited 2015 Federal Reserve Bank of New York study found that colleges raised posted tuition by roughly 55–65 cents for each additional dollar of subsidized loan or Pell aid, with the biggest effects at expensive private nonprofits — substantial pass-through, consistent with Bennett.

But the honest picture is messier. A 2022 Richmond Fed analysis found the pass-through rate is not a fixed number — it was high in the late 1980s, near zero in the late 1990s and again in the late 2000s — so there is "no such thing as the passthrough rate." Several earlier studies found little or no link, and the full tuition increase is too large for aid alone to explain; competing causes include declining state funding, administrative growth, and rising demand for college. The defensible takeaway: the mechanism is real and the effect is sometimes large, but aid is one contributing factor among several, not the whole story. We teach the mechanism with confidence and hold the magnitude loosely — exactly the discipline this course asks for.

Retrieval practice Someone proposes subsidizing demand for a good "because it's important." What single question tells you whether the subsidy will help buyers or just raise the price? ▸

Ask: how elastic is supply? If supply is elastic — sellers can readily produce more — the subsidy mostly expands quantity and reaches buyers as a lower price, doing roughly what its champions intend. If supply is inelastic — sellers can't or won't expand (fixed seats, limited land, capacity bottlenecks) — the extra demand bids up the price and the sellers capture most of the subsidy, leaving the intended buyers little better off and sometimes worse, now facing a higher sticker price. The intuition is the stuck-side rule: a subsidy is a wedge, and the inelastic side pockets it. So "it's good, so let's subsidize it" is incomplete; the right question is "subsidize what , in a market where supply can respond how ?" A wrong answer focuses on how worthy the good is — but worthiness doesn't determine who captures the money. Elasticity does. Ch 7

Major concept 3 · The capstone argument

Let the market work

When something good is scarce, ask what's keeping supply small.

This is the capstone of our case for markets, so let's state the lesson at full strength. When a good is expensive and important, the reflex is to intervene by subsidizing demand. But if the reason it's expensive is that supply is constrained , that intervention predictably backfires — it inflates the price and enriches incumbents, defeating its own purpose. The deeper economics points the other way: if the problem is that supply can't grow, the answer is to free supply to grow — to let the market do what markets do.

College makes the point twice over. On the demand side, decades of expanding aid against a slow-moving supply of seats is exactly the Bennett setup. But look at the supply side and you find the market suppressed in a second way. For most families, which school a child attends is decided by their home address — a local monopoly, assigned, with no competition, no entry, no price signal, no rival down the street fighting for the student. Economics has a sharp prediction about any monopoly insulated from competition: it will be less responsive, less efficient, and slower to improve than it would be under rivalry. The stagnation many feel in assigned schooling is what the market model predicts when you remove competition.

School choice — vouchers, charters, open enrollment — is the proposal to let that market function: let families choose, let schools compete for them, let good ones grow and weak ones feel pressure to improve. Milton Friedman proposed it in 1955 on exactly this logic. Ch 1 The market prediction is not mainly that any one student's test scores will jump; it's that competition disciplines producers — that schools forced to compete for families will get better and use resources more carefully, just as competition does in other markets. That is the genuine, market-based alternative to subsidizing a suppressed system: don't pour more demand into a market you've prevented from working — let it work.

Research study School choice: what the competition evidence actually shows

The market prediction — competition pressures public schools to improve — has real support, though it's not a clean victory. Caroline Hoxby's influential work found that public schools facing more competition raised student achievement per dollar spent — higher productivity under competitive pressure. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis (Jabbar and colleagues) pooling the competitive-effects literature found, overall, small positive effects of competition on student achievement, varying with program design and student population.

Honesty requires the other half. The direct effect of vouchers on the students who use them is genuinely mixed — some studies positive, some null, and a few large programs (notably Louisiana's) showed negative short-run test-score effects. There are real concerns about sorting and segregation, too. So the evidence supports the competitive mechanism more than the simple claim "choice raises every score." The fair conclusion: school choice is no panacea, but the market's core prediction — that competition improves the incumbents — holds up modestly in the data. This is a live empirical dispute, which is why it belongs in the policy conversation, not on an answer key.

Quick check If college tuition is high because the supply of seats can't grow, what does this chapter suggest is the more promising fix — subsidize demand further, or expand supply? ▸
Expand supply. If inelastic supply is the bottleneck, more demand subsidy just raises the price; the market-based answer is to let supply grow so the extra demand is met with more seats rather than higher prices.
Quick check What's the central market prediction for school choice — and what does the evidence actually support most strongly? ▸
Prediction: competition pressures schools to improve and use resources efficiently. The evidence most strongly supports the competitive effect (modest positive), while the direct effect on voucher users is mixed.
Retrieval practice Explain how Bennett and school choice are two halves of one argument about letting markets work. ▸

Both are about what happens when you intervene in, or suppress, a market instead of letting it function. The Bennett side shows the cost of intervening the wrong way: subsidizing demand against inelastic supply inflates the price and is captured by sellers, so the intervention defeats its own goal. The school-choice side shows the cost of suppressing the market: assigning students by address removes competition, and economics predicts a monopoly with no rivalry will stagnate — which is what we observe. The unifying lesson, and the capstone of the case for markets, is that prices and competition do real coordinating work, and policies that override them — by pumping subsidies into a constrained market, or by blocking competition entirely — tend to produce worse outcomes than letting the market operate. This isn't a claim that markets are flawless; it's the strongest version of the case for them, which the next units will test against the cases where they genuinely fail. Ch 15

On the exam

The moves you can now make

Three questions at the level the exam will actually ask — application, not recall. Try each on paper first, then reveal the worked solution. The trying is where the learning happens.

Type A · comparative statics of a subsidy

A government introduces a per-unit subsidy paid to producers of rooftop solar panels. Identify how the subsidy shifts the relevant curve, then state what happens to the equilibrium quantity, the price buyers pay, and the price sellers receive — and explain why the government's budget is affected.

Show worked solution ▸

A producer subsidy lowers the price sellers need from buyers at every quantity, so effective supply shifts down (equivalently, right) by the amount of the subsidy — the mirror of a tax, which shifts supply up. It is not a movement along supply, because the trigger isn't the panel's own price.

Moving down the unchanged demand curve, equilibrium quantity rises , the price buyers pay falls , and the price sellers receive rises — the two prices split apart by exactly the subsidy. The government pays that wedge on every unit traded, so its budget falls by subsidy × quantity. The tell that it's a subsidy and not a demand increase: buyers pay less while quantity rises (a demand increase would raise both price and quantity).

Type B · incidence & capture by elasticity

To make insulin more affordable, a state subsidizes buyers of insulin. Supply is highly inelastic in the short run. Predict who captures most of the subsidy and what happens to the price sellers receive. If the answer depends on something the prompt doesn't fix, say what.

Show worked solution ▸

Who the subsidy is formally "given to" does not determine who captures it — that's the trap. Capture follows elasticity: the more inelastic (stuck) side captures the larger share. Here supply is highly inelastic, so sellers capture most of the subsidy : the price they receive rises sharply, while the net price buyers pay falls only a little. A subsidy routed through buyers still ends up largely in sellers' pockets when sellers are the stuck side.

The full-credit answer also names the dependency: how much buyers benefit depends on the relative elasticities of supply and demand, which set the split — in the limit of perfectly inelastic supply, buyers gain nothing and the entire subsidy raises the price. Asserting "buyers benefit because the subsidy goes to buyers" is the classic error.

Type C · computation

A market is described by the equations below and clears at $60 with a quantity of 20 :

  • • Demand: P = 100 − 2Q
  • • Supply: P = 40 + Q

The government adds a $15-per-unit subsidy. Compute the new quantity, the price buyers pay, the price sellers receive, and the total cost to the government. Show your work.

Show worked solution ▸
  1. Subsidy subtracts from the supply intercept: effective supply P = 40 + Q − 15 = 25 + Q.
  2. Set demand equal to subsidized supply: 100 − 2Q = 25 + Q → 75 = 3Q → Q = 25 .
  3. Price buyers pay, off demand: P = 100 − 2(25) = $50 . Price sellers receive = $50 + $15 = $65 (check on original supply: 40 + 25 = $65 ✓).
  4. Government cost = subsidy × quantity = $15 × 25 = $375 , paid out.
Q = 25; buyers pay $50, sellers receive $65; the government spends $375. Quantity rose and the two prices split by the $15 subsidy — the sign-flipped image of a tax.
Capstone · free response

Put the moves together

AP-style long free response · ~9 points · graph required · model answer and scoring below

A state wants to make college more affordable and introduces a per-student tuition subsidy, paid to students, in the market for university seats. In the short run the supply of seats is hard to expand.

  1. Draw a correctly labeled supply-and-demand graph for university seats, showing the initial equilibrium tuition (T 1 ) and quantity of seats (Q 1 ). (2 pts)
  2. Show the effect of the per-student subsidy on your graph, and state what happens to the equilibrium quantity, the tuition students pay, and the tuition schools receive. (2 pts)
  3. Suppose demand is P = 100 − 2Q and supply is P = 40 + Q, and the subsidy is $15 per seat. Calculate the new quantity, the price students pay, the price schools receive, and the total cost to the state. Show your work. (3 pts)
  4. Now suppose the supply of seats were far more inelastic than in part (c). Explain how the division of the $15 subsidy between students and schools would change, and why — and what that implies about using student aid to make college more affordable. (2 pts)
Reveal a full-credit response ▸

(a) A standard graph: vertical axis Tuition ($), horizontal axis Quantity of seats, downward-sloping D and upward-sloping S crossing at E 1 , with T 1 dropped to the price axis and Q 1 to the quantity axis. (See figure.)

(b) The subsidy drives a wedge: effective supply shifts down by the subsidy. Equilibrium quantity rises (Q 1 →Q 2 ), the tuition students pay falls , and the tuition schools receive rises — the two prices split apart by the subsidy, with the state paying the gap.

(c) Subsidized supply P = 40 + Q − 15 = 25 + Q. Set Q d = Q s : 100 − 2Q = 25 + Q → 75 = 3Q → Q = 25 . Students pay P = 100 − 2(25) = $50 ; schools receive $50 + $15 = $65 . State cost = $15 × 25 = $375 .

(d) The more inelastic supply is, the larger the share schools capture and the smaller the share students keep: the price schools receive rises more, and the net price students pay falls less. Intuition: stuck supply can't add seats in response to the aid, so the extra demand bids tuition up instead of expanding access — in the limit of perfectly inelastic supply, the entire $15 becomes higher tuition and students are no better off. Implication: where the supply of seats is fixed, student aid mostly inflates tuition and is captured by schools (the Bennett mechanism), so aid alone is a poor tool for affordability unless supply can also grow.

Reveal the scoring rubric ▸
  • 1 pt (a) Axes labeled (Tuition on vertical, Quantity of seats on horizontal) with a downward D and upward S.
  • 1 pt (a) Initial equilibrium marked where D and S cross, with T 1 and Q 1 indicated.
  • 1 pt (b) Subsidy shown as supply shifting down (a new curve, not a movement along S).
  • 1 pt (b) States quantity rises , tuition paid falls , and tuition received rises .
  • 1 pt (c) Correct quantity Q = 25 with work shown (demand set equal to subsidized supply).
  • 1 pt (c) Correct prices: students pay $50, schools receive $65.
  • 1 pt (c) Correct state cost $375 (subsidy × quantity).
  • 1 pt (d) States that more inelastic supply means schools capture a larger share / students a smaller share.
  • 1 pt (d) Explains why (stuck supply can't add seats, so the aid raises price) and draws the affordability implication.

Nine independent points — each earned on its own. A wrong number in (c) doesn't cost you the (d) points if your elasticity logic is right.

Reveal what loses points ▸
  • Conceptual In (b), shifting demand instead of supply because the subsidy is "paid to students." A per-unit subsidy is modeled as a vertical shift of the curve by the subsidy amount — the side it's routed through doesn't change the geometry or who ends up capturing it.
  • Precision In (c), writing "$65" with no equation set up. "Calculate" prompts require shown work; a correct number alone routinely earns nothing. And forgetting to add the subsidy back to recover the seller price — reporting $50 for both sides — drops the price point.
  • Exam craft In (d), asserting "schools capture more" without the mechanism. The "Explain" verb wants why — stuck supply can't expand, so the extra demand raises price rather than quantity — not just the direction of the result.
The case so far · Unit 1 closes

Unit 1 made a single argument in eight moves. Scarcity forces every choice to cost something Ch 1 , and we make those choices at the margin, weighing the next unit rather than the whole Ch 2 . Out of millions of such choices, supply and demand settle on a price no one sets and everyone reads — the invisible hand coordinating strangers Ch 3 . Push against that price with a binding control and you freeze the very shortage you meant to cure Ch 4 . Left free, the same coordination lets people specialize and trade, and the wealth of nations flows from that cooperation Ch 5 .

Surplus let us measure the value all this creates, and deadweight loss let us see what's destroyed when trades that should happen don't Ch 6 . A tax drives a wedge into the machinery, and the side that can't walk away bears it Ch 7 . And a subsidy — the case we just made — is that same wedge with the sign flipped: the side that can't walk away captures it, so helping the wrong side of a stuck market can quietly defeat itself. That is the strongest honest case for letting markets work. It is not the whole story — markets also fail, sometimes badly, and the next units take that up at full strength, starting where the magic begins to break. Ch 15

Market thesis

A subsidy is a tax with the sign flipped — and the stuck side pockets it. Subsidize demand where supply can't grow, and you fund the seller, not the buyer.

If economists ruled the world…

we'd judge every subsidy by one question — does it make supply compete , or let it coast? We'd fund seats, not just tuition, and let schools compete for students instead of inheriting them by address.

In your life
