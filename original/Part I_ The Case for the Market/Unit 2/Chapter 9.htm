Firm Model I: Costs

Where the firm meets reality

Chapter 9 ~ 38 min read

On a sweltering day in 1999, Coca-Cola let slip an idea that would haunt its public relations for years: vending machines fitted with a thermostat and a chip, quietly programmed to raise the price of a cold Coke as the temperature climbed. The company's chairman, Douglas Ivester, defended it to a Brazilian magazine — a cold drink is simply worth more in a heat wave, he reasoned, so, in his words, "it is fair that it should be more expensive." The reaction was instant and brutal. Critics called it a scheme to squeeze loyal customers for the crime of being thirsty, and within days the company was backpedaling and shelving the machines. The strangest part: they had never actually reached the street. The revolt was one of pure reputation — which, for a consumer brand, can be worse than a broken window.

What had Ivester gotten wrong? Not the economics, exactly — prices rise with demand all the time, and nobody storms the airport when holiday airfares jump. What enraged people was subtler: they saw a price climbing with no new cost behind it. A Coke is no cheaper or dearer to make when it's hot out; you were simply thirstier, and the machine knew it. The price had come unhooked from cost and latched onto need. And that exposes the question this whole chapter exists to answer: what is a price actually supposed to track — and what, when you look closely, is a cost?

Until now this textbook has lived inside the buyer's head — demand, willingness to pay, the way a consumer meets scarcity as a budget that won't stretch. Cross now to the other side of the market, into the producer, and scarcity shows a different face: cost . Cost is what scarcity looks like from the supply side, and it is where reality exerts discipline on every firm that is trying to sell something to a consumer.

Was Ivester right? Should prices rise on Coke machines in the blazing hot of summer? And if so, what does it have to do with cost?

The big picture
Costs communicate information — due to scarcity, the idea this course began with, and how it reveals itself to the people who produce. Price is the meeting of supply and demand, but where does supply come from? It is simply the marginal cost curve for the full market. A firm that listens to its costs stays in contact with reality; a firm that ignores them drifts into wishful thinking, and reality eventually collects. By the end of this chapter, you will be able to name the various meanings of the word "cost," graph them, read them from a table, and understand that costs are both a tragedy and an opportunity for every businessperson in the world; and every human being, too.

Major concept 1

Cost as Tragedy and Opportunity

A high cost is reality talking. The question is always: is it a wall, or a dare?

Before any graphs, sit with what a cost is . We begin with two ways to think about the term.

Cost as tragedy. The economist William Baumol noticed that some things resist getting cheaper no matter how clever anyone is. He called it cost disease . (Note that many economists prefer the term "Baumol effect".) Baumol taught that a factory can become more productive with fewer inputs, but a string quartet will always need four musicians to play a quartet, just as it did in 1820; a classroom still needs a teacher's attention; a counseling hour is still an hour; a nurse can only care for so many patients at once. In manufacturing, a worker makes vastly more per hour than a century ago — so factory goods got cheap. But the quartet, the class, the therapy session can't be sped up without becoming a different, lesser thing. So their cost climbs relative to everything else, even under fierce competition . The lesson is humbling: reality pushes back. Not every rising price is waste, and not every expensive service is run by fools. Some costs are simply the shape of a real constraint, and pretending otherwise — demanding the quartet play twice as fast — just breaks the thing.

Cost as opportunity. Clayton Christensen looked at the same constraints and saw a provocation. Many organizations are expensive not because the work is hard but because they keep solving it the old, comfortable way. For Christensen, a constraint can be an engine for innovation: simplification and ruthless prioritization can improve the value proposition for customers. Southwest flies one aircraft type — the Boeing 737, every plane in a fleet of around 810 — so its mechanics, crews, and spare parts are interchangeable and its jets turn around in roughly twenty-five minutes instead of an hour. Aldi stocks about 1,400 items where a full supermarket carries 30,000, almost all of them its own label, which buys it brutal bargaining power and a fraction of the overhead. In-N-Out sells four burgers, fries, and shakes — and little else. IKEA made you the assembler. None of them found more resources; each accepted a hard constraint and rethought everything around it, and the constraint was the spur. The lesson is hopeful: a wall to one firm is a dare to another, and the costs everyone treats as fixed are often the ones most worth attacking.

Both readings are true, which is why the chapter holds them together rather than picking one.

Synthesis What a cost is, in three lines
  • Costs are manifestations of reality. A price is built on supply and demand, and both reflect scarcity: demand, the scarcity facing consumers, and supply the scarcity facing producers.
  • They should be confronted honestly. Baumol's warning: some constraints are real and won't yield to wishful thinking. Pretending a cost away doesn't lower it; it just hides the bill.
  • They provide profound opportunity. Christensen's invitation: a constraint, faced squarely, is the most common birthplace of a better way. The cost you resent is often the one to reinvent.

The entrepreneur's whole skill is telling a Baumol cost from a Christensen one — knowing which wall is load-bearing and which is just waiting to be knocked through.

Rules of business success · Rule #1: costs matter

This unit follows a firm trying to win, and each chapter adds one rule to the list. Here is the first: a firm that consistently ignores its costs loses to one that doesn't. That is not the same as saying cheap is the goal — quality, innovation, and customer value all matter, and the real objective, as the next chapter shows, is profit, not penny-pinching. But costs are the firm's contact point with reality. A firm that loses track of what things actually cost has let strategy drift into fantasy, and the market always sends the bill.

A principle worth carrying · the short menu

"The best restaurants have short menus." The old saying among restauranteurs is useful because it isn't just about food. Notice what In-N-Out's four-item board really is: a refusal. Every dish a kitchen doesn't make is prep it never stocks, training it never runs, and oven time it never loses — so the few it keeps, it makes faster, cheaper, and better than a rival drowning in options. This is the deepest practical lesson of cost: managed well, costs force ruthless prioritization. They make you name your most important job, pour everything into it, and let the rest go. The best restaurants have short menus for exactly this reason, and the logic reaches any operation with a binding constraint. The goal isn't to do less for its own sake — it's that doing the one thing that matters superbly is impossible until you stop trying to do everything.

Both readings depend on a word we've been using loosely. Before we can manage costs, we need to be precise about what economists actually mean by one.

Major concept 2

The Taxonomy of Cost

"Cost" is one word hiding four different meanings. Good thinking starts by knowing which one you're asking.

A firm that confuses kinds of cost makes bad decisions, so economists slice the word four ways — each slice a different question.

Does it change with output? A soda-producers factory cost is the same whether it makes one bottle of soda or a hundred thousand — a fixed cost . The syrup, lid, and carbonated water climb with every bottle and vanish at zero output — variable costs . Together they make total cost : TC = FC + VC This split governs the rest of the model, so hold onto it.

Is it paid in money? Wages, rent, a delivery of flavor are explicit costs — money that leaves the till. But quit a $60,000 job to run the soda shop and no one bills you for the salary you gave up, though you surrendered it as surely as you pay for aluminum cans. That foregone alternative is an implicit cost — the opportunity cost of using your own time or money here instead of its next-best use. This is the same idea the course opened with, now wearing a firm's clothes: economists count what was given up, not merely what was spent. Ch 1

Which costs are included? Economists like to say that accountants are wrong about cost. (That's a bit of grandstanding: accountants know plenty about costs!) But there is an important principle here: accountants tally the money that moved — which is called accounting cost . Economists insist on adding the opportunities forgone — economic (or opportunity) cost . The honest cost of a choice includes the door it closed. (Next chapter turns this into the difference between accounting and economic profit .) Ch 10

Can it still be changed? Money already spent and unrecoverable — the non-refundable equipment, the signed lease — is a sunk cost . Because nothing you do now can change it, it should play no part in deciding what to do next. For now, learn to spot sunk costs; we'll discuss more fully in a future chapter. Ch 12

Fixed and sunk answer different questions: "fixed" means unmoved by output (rent is fixed but still avoidable if you close); "sunk" means unrecoverable at all. A cost can be fixed this month yet not sunk, because next month you could drop the lease.

With the vocabulary straight, the two forces from Concept 1 take concrete shape. Some costs fall as a firm grows; others rise as it strains. We will take them in turn.

Major concept 3

Why Costs Fall Before they Rise

Two forces fight over every cost curve: meet the U-shaped cost curve.

A fixed cost — the $60-a-month lease, the factory, the movie that cost $200 million to shoot — doesn't change with output. So the more units you make, the thinner that same cost spreads. Average fixed cost, AFC = FC ÷ Q , only ever falls. Take the two columns from a small workshop's books and plot them; this is the easy curve, and it's the whole reason the first unit feels ruinous and the next feels cheaper.

What to notice: AFC never turns back up. As long as a cost is fixed, more output can only dilute it.

Now stretch the same logic from one plant to a whole operation, and you get the headline version: economies of scale . Here we step briefly out of the short run — this isn't about spreading one fixed cost over more units, but about a firm free to scale everything (bigger plants, specialized teams, better machines) and finding that its average cost keeps dropping as it grows. Where the up-front costs are enormous and the cost of one more copy is tiny, the effect is dramatic. A drug may cost a billion dollars to develop and pennies to manufacture; a film, a video game, a streaming catalog, a semiconductor fab — all sink huge fixed costs that the millionth user barely adds to. The first unit is a fortune; the millionth is nearly free. And scale pays off even without a giant up-front cost: Costco stocks only about 4,000 items, so it buys each in colossal volume, turns its entire inventory roughly twelve times a year, and squeezes suppliers in a way a corner store never could — sheer size converted straight into a lower cost per unit.

Economies of scale are not magic, and not (by themselves) a sign of villainy. They're arithmetic: a giant fixed cost shared across a giant quantity. Which clears up a common confusion about big firms.

Reflection · why big firms often have an edge

Once, in college, my business strategy professor asked us how it is that Wal-Mart so clearly dominates the marketplace. The young group of business students tried something that seemed plausible: "because... Wal-Mart is big?" It's tempting to assume large firms dominate because they're powerful. Often the causation runs the other way: they grew large because scale let them drive average cost down, and the low cost is what let them grow. Amazon's warehouses, a chipmaker's fab, a streaming service's catalog — each spreads a colossal fixed cost over enormous volume, so the per-unit cost a small rival faces is simply higher. Wal-Mart can win because it's big, but it didn't start that way: it found a way to revolutionize its supply chain in a way that saved costs; an immediate cost advantage that allowed them to become a major market player. This isn't a verdict, good or bad; sometimes that scale passes savings to customers, sometimes it walls out competitors, and untangling which is the work of later chapters. Ch 16 For now just see the mechanism: much of what looks like raw power is average cost doing its quiet arithmetic.

Quick check A software studio spends $5,000,000 building an app and almost nothing serving each new user. What happens to its average cost per user as users grow — and which force is it? ▸
Average cost per user falls steeply — the $5M is spread over more and more users. With near-zero cost per extra user, it keeps dropping. That's economies of scale (and, within a fixed plant, the same spreading that makes AFC fall).
Quick check Why is "average fixed cost falls" a short-run idea while "economies of scale" is a long-run one? ▸
AFC falls because one fixed cost is spread over more units with the plant held fixed — short run. Economies of scale lets every input grow (bigger plants, new machines) and asks what happens to average cost then — long run. Same direction, different time horizon.

If costs only ever fell, every firm would grow forever and the largest would make everything. They don't, because a second force pushes the other way. Picture a small kitchen with one oven, a fixed counter, one head chef. Hire a second cook and output jumps — one preps while the other cooks. A third specializes further; output leaps again. But keep going. The fifth cook is reaching across the fourth for the same pans; the sixth waits on oven time; the seventh is mostly in the way. Each still helps, but less and less — and to squeeze out those last few meals you're burning whole salaries. The cost of one more meal is climbing.

The culprit isn't the cooks. It's the oven. The kitchen has a bottleneck — a fixed input everything else has to funnel through — and once you've added enough of the things you can add, that fixed thing throttles the rest. Economists call the result diminishing returns : past some point, each extra worker (or machine, or dollar) adds less than the one before, because the resource that really limits output isn't the one you're adding. This is the deepest lesson of the concept, and it generalizes far past kitchens: the constraint that matters is usually not the resource you're piling on, but the one quietly capping everything else.

You see the same bottleneck everywhere once you look. Workers waiting on a single machine. Trucks queued at one loading dock. Diners filling a restaurant with a fixed number of tables. A class where the teacher's attention is the scarce thing, not the number of desks. In each, output can be pushed up for a while, then the fixed input bites and every additional unit gets costlier to produce. That — not a formula — is the reason marginal cost eventually rises. The market doesn't care how badly the kitchen wants to serve more; it responds to what serving more actually costs. The firms that win this game hunt the bottleneck down instead of throwing bodies at it: Toyota built its whole production system around finding the single constrained step on the line and widening it, treating every wasted motion as the real enemy — the exact opposite of hiring a seventh cook for a one-oven kitchen.

Put numbers to that workshop and the cost concepts fall out directly. Marginal cost is the cost of one more unit, MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ — it dips while specialization helps, then climbs as the bottleneck bites. The averages divide a total by the units sharing it: average variable cost AVC = VC ÷ Q and average total cost ATC = TC ÷ Q (which is just AFC + AVC ). Here is the whole workshop in one table.

Retrieval practice From memory: name the force that pushes costs down and the force that pushes them up, and the single shape that records the fight between them. ▸

Down: a fixed cost spread over more units lowers cost per unit (AFC falls), and scaled to the whole operation that becomes economies of scale. Up: forcing output through a fixed input creates a bottleneck, so added inputs hit diminishing returns and each extra unit costs more — marginal cost rises. The average-cost curve is U-shaped because the two trade off: spreading wins at low output, crowding wins at high. The slip to avoid is treating either force as mysterious — both are arithmetic, one dividing a fixed cost across more units, the other piling variable inputs onto a fixed one.

Major concept 4

The Shape of Cost

Four curves on one graph — and every bend is a story you've already heard.

If you want to understand cost curves, begin here: we assume a U-shaped (often checkmark shape) for a marginal cost curve. Plot the workshop's costs and the whole chapter shows up in a single picture. First, average costs fall (due to specialization, economies of scale, and spreading costs over more produced quantity). Then they begin to rise (due to diminishing returns to inputs, like having one stove and 25 workers). Are all industries shaped this way? No, it is a simplification. But it is a strong enough trend to be representative, and it can teach you a few things about microeconomics in the meantime!

Now let's learn about the rest of the curves you will likely see when we look at firms:

Fig 9.2 The workshop's cost family. MC dips while specialization helps, then rises as the bottleneck bites, passing through the bottom of AVC and then of ATC. The shrinking gap between ATC and AVC is average fixed cost.
What to notice: nothing here is arbitrary — each curve is one of the chapter's forces drawn in ink.

AFC falls forever — Concept 3, the fixed cost spreading thinner. It isn't drawn as its own line here; it's the closing gap between ATC and AVC.

AVC is U-shaped. Early on, specialization makes each unit cheaper to produce; later, the bottleneck from Concept 4 makes each costlier. Down, then up.

ATC is also U-shaped, but for a combined reason: it's a race. Falling AFC drags it down while rising AVC pushes it up. AFC wins at first (ATC falls), then AVC takes over (ATC rises), and the bottom — the efficient scale — is where they trade places. (Early note: it might be tempting to produce at the efficient scale, but it's not quite right. We'll cover this more later, but it's worth noticing for now.)

MC rises once the bottleneck bites, and it does one thing worth memorizing: it slices through the lowest point of both average curves. That's not a coincidence, just the logic of any average. While the next unit costs less than the running average, it pulls the average down; once it costs more , it tugs the average up; the two are equal exactly at the bottom. It's the same reason a test score above your GPA lifts it and one below drags it — the marginal pulls the average toward itself, and they meet at the turning point.

And now the payoff we promised at the start of the chapter: look again at that rising MC curve, because it is the supply curve in disguise. A producer makes a unit whenever the price covers what that unit costs — its marginal cost. At a low price, only the cheap early units clear the bar; raise the price and the costlier later units become worth making too. Read the MC curve as "what price would it take to make this unit worthwhile?" and you've drawn supply: upward-sloping, for exactly the reason this chapter has been building — because cost rises at the margin.

One last look back. The two thinkers from the opening live on this graph. A Baumol cost is the curve sitting stubbornly high because the bottleneck is real and won't move — the quartet's four players. A Christensen move is someone finding a way to drop the whole curve down — a cheaper process the incumbent never tried. The shape tells you what's true now; it can't tell you which costs are walls and which are dares. That judgment is the chapter's real lesson, and the curves are only its instrument.

Pull the chapter's examples into one view. Every one of these firms read a high cost not as a verdict but as an invitation — and every move maps back to a force on the graph you just read.

ATC is U-shaped because falling AFC pulls it down while rising AVC pushes it up; the minimum is where they trade off. Major concept 5

Where Cost Meets Demand

A cost builds the supply curve. A price needs two curves. That difference is the whole chapter.

In the previous eight chapters, you found the most important point on any market graph: the place where supply crosses demand, where the market clears and, in a competitive market, the total benefit to buyers and sellers is as large as it can be. Ch 6–8 But back then the supply curve was a stranger — a line that sloped up because we said it did. Now you can name it. The supply curve is the marginal-cost curve. I can make a few eggs just from the chicken in my backyard; to produce a thousand eggs would require significantly more spending, and for 100,000 eggs I would need to buy a factory. The supply curve rises because the cost of making one more unit climbs at the margin, exactly as the last two concepts showed; read that rising MC curve as the question "what would the price have to be to make this unit worth producing?" and you have drawn supply. Demand is a measure of a consumer's willingness to pay. The supply curve was a measure of a producer's willingness to sell; but a supplier is willing to sell whenever their costs are covered. The stranger from Unit 1 was cost all along.

Which is the moment to retire the tempting mistake from the beginning of the chapter: consumers often assume that cost and price are inextricably related. A thing's price is not its cost. Cost builds only half the picture: the supply curve, the producer's side. The price is where that supply curve meets demand — where the cost of one more unit is weighed against what a buyer will give for one more. Demand never left the room. So a high price is never, on its own, "a high cost made visible." It is a meeting: cost on one side, willingness to pay on the other, settling on the number where the market clears.

Seeing price as a meeting sorts every price increase into two clean cases. Sometimes the supply curve itself shifts up — the cost of one more genuinely rises: the 2 a.m. driver who must be coaxed out with a 2x fare from a ridesharing app; the congested lane; the plant fired up only at peak. Here the higher price really is the higher marginal cost showing through. Sometimes supply can't move at all, and demand surges against it. A vending machine holds a fixed stock of cans; a heat wave sends demand leaping while the cost of stocking a can stays exactly what it was. The price climbs anyway — not because cost rose, but because demand is now crowding a supply that cannot grow. The hot-day Coke is this second kind, which is exactly why calling it a "cost" rang false. It wasn't one.

Are there examples of supply-pricing? Absolutely: if a crop is decimated by a hurricane, you will see prices rise. Individual firms might want to sell at a given price, but simply be unable to because their crop has been destroyed: the supply curve shifts in (left) and the price spikes to compensate. Similarly, hotel rooms might be unavailable after a natural disaster not because of demand, but because some of the stock of available hotel rooms were damaged. When most people speak of "demand-pricing" they simply mean a more granular measure of demand: not the average demand for a day, but the demand for a good in real-time, minute-by-minute. Perhaps the best example of supply-pricing is an airline ticket when there are only three spots left: one the three spots are gone, the only way to increase supply is by contracting another full flight with all the cost of plane, fuel, and crew.

In the case of a Coke machine, however, are there increased costs during the hot part of the day? No. So what is the higher price doing if not reflecting a cost? It is rationing supply during a period of increased demand . There is something worse than a $10 bottle of Coke: an "out of stock" sign. There are only so many cold cans, and on a blazing day more people want one than there are cans to go around. Someone goes without — that is what scarcity means, and no pricing scheme, imposed or repealed, can change it. The only question is who , and how it gets decided. Let the price rise, and it decides: the can goes to whoever will give up the most for it. Forbid the price to move — pin it at the cool-morning number — and the price doesn't vanish; a different one takes over. The machine empties by noon, and the parched soul at three o'clock meets the one price higher than any surge: sold out . A shortage is a price too. It is just paid in queues, in luck, in showing up early — and it falls hardest on whoever has the least time to wait. Holding a price down does not abolish the rationing. It only changes the currency.

Economists may support demand-pricing more than the layperson, but it has nothing to do with cost. It's because demand rises during peak times, and demand-pricing is a good way to ration. Without such rationing, there is nothing to prevent a shortage just like we learned about in chapter 3.

Reflection · when the can is insulin

A cold Coke is a fine thing to ration by price, because going without one costs you almost nothing. Run the same logic on a good no one can do without — insulin, an inhaler, the only hospital bed for sixty miles — and "the price decides who goes without" stops sounding clever and starts sounding cruel. The machinery is identical; what changed is the stakes , and one thing we haven't named yet: how sharply buyers pull back when the price rises, which is very different for a luxury than for a necessity. That responsiveness has a name and a measure, and it is the hinge on which the fairness of price-rationing turns. We'll come back to the insulin machine when we reach elasticity — and we'll be able to say something precise, instead of merely uneasy.

Note: our example of a single Coke machine in a desert is an example of a stylized case: the kind that you read about in textbooks, but you likely won't encounter in real life. In real life you would likely see more buyers, more sellers, and more stock. We use this example only for teaching purposes. Here is what we can say: a price free to move rations a scarce thing by willingness to pay; a price held down rations it by shortage instead. Which of those you would rather live with — for a Coke, for a parking space, for a dose of insulin — is exactly the question the chapter leaves in your hands.

A company that is deciding whether to invest in more Coke machines, however, does make decisions based on a cost measure: opportunity cost. If they can make more money with Coke machines than they can by other means, then they will order more Coke machines — and give you more chances for consumer surplus.

Quick check A ride-share fare doubles at 2 a.m.; a vending-machine Coke doubles on a hot afternoon. One reflects a real cost and one doesn't. Which is which — and why? ▸
The 2 a.m. fare reflects a real cost: coaxing a driver out of bed genuinely costs more, so the supply curve has shifted up. The hot-day Coke does not: the can costs no more to stock, so supply is fixed and demand has surged against it. That price is rationing the scarce cans, not tracking a cost.
Quick check During a heat wave a city caps bottled-water prices at their normal level. The water doesn't become more plentiful. So what does the cap actually change? ▸
Not whether the water is rationed — it still is, because there isn't enough — only how . Instead of price deciding who goes without, a shortage does: shelves empty, and the water goes to whoever queued earliest or got lucky, not whoever needed it most. The cap swaps one rationing currency (money) for another (time and luck).
Quick check Fill in the chapter's last reveal: the supply curve is the ______ curve, and a market price is set where that curve meets ______. ▸
The marginal-cost curve; demand. Cost alone gives you supply; the price is the crossing of supply and demand, never the cost by itself.
Retrieval practice From memory: say what the supply curve really is, why a price is not the same thing as a cost, and the two currencies a scarce good can be rationed in. ▸

The supply curve is the marginal-cost curve — it slopes up because the cost of one more unit rises at the margin. A price is not a cost: it is where the supply curve (cost of one more) meets demand (value of one more), so demand is always half of it. And a scarce good is always rationed somehow — either by price (it goes to whoever will pay most) or, if the price is held down, by shortage (it goes to whoever queues, waits, or gets lucky). The error to avoid is thinking a price control removes the rationing, when it only changes the currency.

On the exam

The moves you can now make

Three questions at the level the exam actually asks — compute the costs, read the cost graph, and explain the crossing. Try each on paper first, then reveal. The trying is where the learning happens.

Type A · computation from a schedule

A firm has a fixed cost of $50. Its total cost is $90 at 2 units and $108 at 3 units. Find, for the third unit: marginal cost; and at Q = 3, average fixed cost, average variable cost, and average total cost. Show the steps.

Show worked solution ▸
  1. MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ = (108 − 90) ÷ 1 = $18 for the third unit.
  2. AFC = FC ÷ Q = 50 ÷ 3 = $16.67.
  3. Variable cost at Q = 3 is TC − FC = 108 − 50 = $58, so AVC = VC ÷ Q = 58 ÷ 3 = $19.33.
  4. ATC = TC ÷ Q = 108 ÷ 3 = $36 — check: AFC + AVC = 16.67 + 19.33 = $36. ✓
Third-unit MC $18; at Q = 3, AFC $16.67, AVC $19.33, ATC $36. The AFC + AVC check catches arithmetic slips before they cost you.

Type B · identify and read

Three short-run cost curves are drawn below, labeled only 1, 2, and 3. Identify which is marginal cost, which is average total cost, and which is average variable cost — and state how you can tell. Then mark the firm's efficient scale.

Three short-run cost curves — which is which?
Show worked solution ▸

Curve 1 is marginal cost : it is the one that crosses both of the others from below, and it crosses each at that curve's lowest point. Curve 2 is average total cost and curve 3 is average variable cost — the tells are that ATC sits above AVC everywhere (it carries the extra average fixed cost), and the two converge as quantity grows because that fixed-cost gap shrinks.

The efficient scale is the quantity at the bottom of curve 2 (ATC) — where MC slices through its minimum. To the left of it ATC is still falling; to the right it has turned up. A frequent error is to read the minimum of AVC (curve 3, slightly to the left) as the efficient scale; efficient scale is defined on total cost per unit, so it is the bottom of ATC.

Type C · explain the relationship

Explain why the marginal cost curve must intersect the average total cost curve at the minimum of average total cost. Do not just assert it — give the reasoning.

Show model answer ▸

It follows from how any average responds to the next value added to it. When the marginal (next) unit costs less than the current average, it drags the average down; when it costs more , it pulls the average up. So ATC falls exactly while MC is below it and rises exactly while MC is above it.

The turning point — the bottom of ATC, where it stops falling and starts rising — is therefore precisely the quantity where MC has just risen to equal ATC. Below that quantity MC < ATC (ATC falling); above it MC > ATC (ATC rising); at it, MC = ATC. That is why the curves must cross at the minimum, not before or after — the same logic that makes one low test score pull your grade down only while it sits below your average.

Capstone · free response

Put the moves together

AP-style long free response · ~9 points · graph required · model answer and scoring below

A small furniture workshop produces tables in the short run, with its workshop space fixed. Its fixed cost is $120. The schedule below gives total cost at each output.

Q (tables) 1 2 3 4 5
Total cost ($) 200 260 300 360 460
  1. Calculate the marginal cost of the 2nd table and of the 5th table. Show your work. (2 pt)
  2. Calculate average variable cost and average total cost at Q = 4. Show your work. (2 pt)
  3. On a correctly labeled graph, draw the firm's marginal cost, average variable cost, and average total cost curves, and mark the efficient scale. (3 pt)
  4. Identify the quantity at which average total cost is minimized, and state what must be true of marginal cost at that quantity. (1 pt)
  5. Explain why average total cost is U-shaped, referring to diminishing marginal product. (1 pt)
Reveal a full-credit response ▸

(a) MC of the 2nd table = (260 − 200) ÷ 1 = $60 . MC of the 5th = (460 − 360) ÷ 1 = $100 .

(b) At Q = 4: variable cost = TC − FC = 360 − 120 = $240, so AVC = 240 ÷ 4 = $60 . ATC = 360 ÷ 4 = $90 .

(c) The full schedule (FC = $120) works out to: MC 80, 60, 40, 60, 100; AVC 80, 70, 60, 60, 68; ATC 200, 130, 100, 90, 92. The model graph:

Model graph MC cuts AVC and then ATC at their minimum points; ATC bottoms out at Q = 4, the efficient scale.

(d) ATC is minimized at Q = 4 (ATC = $90, its lowest value). The rule is that marginal cost equals average total cost at the minimum of ATC — MC cuts ATC there from below. In this discrete schedule that crossing falls between the 4th and 5th tables: MC is $60 (below ATC) at the 4th and $100 (above ATC) at the 5th, which is exactly what pins the minimum at Q = 4.

(e) With the workshop fixed, adding variable inputs runs into diminishing marginal product: each added unit of input eventually yields less extra output, so marginal cost rises. ATC first falls (the fixed cost is being spread over more units, and early units are cheap to make), then rises once rising marginal cost outweighs the shrinking average fixed cost — producing the U.

Reveal the scoring rubric ▸
  • 1 pt (a) MC of 2nd table = $60 with work shown (ΔTC ÷ ΔQ).
  • 1 pt (a) MC of 5th table = $100 with work shown.
  • 1 pt (b) AVC at Q = 4 = $60 with work (VC = TC − FC).
  • 1 pt (b) ATC at Q = 4 = $90 with work.
  • 1 pt (c) Axes labeled (cost per unit on vertical, quantity on horizontal); all three curves drawn and labeled.
  • 1 pt (c) MC drawn cutting AVC and ATC at (or near) their minimum points, from below.
  • 1 pt (c) Efficient scale marked at the minimum of ATC (Q = 4).
  • 1 pt (d) States ATC minimized at Q = 4 AND that MC = ATC there.
  • 1 pt (e) Explains the U via diminishing marginal product driving MC up.

Nine independent points. A wrong number in (a) or (b) doesn't sink the graph points in (c) if the shapes and crossing are right for your own figures.

Reveal what loses points ▸
  • Conceptual Drawing MC through the downward slope of ATC, or above the minimum — MC must cut each average at its lowest point, from below. Drawing AVC above ATC also forfeits the point; AVC is always the lower of the two.
  • Precision Writing "MC = $60" with no ΔTC ÷ ΔQ setup. An AP "Calculate" prompt requires shown work; a bare correct number routinely earns nothing.
  • Exam craft Unlabeled axes or unlabeled curves on the graph — even a perfectly shaped diagram scores zero on the labeling points if the reader can't tell which curve is which or what the axes measure.
Market thesis

Costs are how scarcity reveals itself to producers — and how the market holds them honest. The firm that reads its costs squarely, confronting the real ones and reinventing the rest, stays in contact with reality; the firm that ignores them drifts into fantasy, until reality sends the bill.

If economists ruled the world…

prices would be free to move to wherever supply meets demand — surge fares, congestion tolls, peak-power and drought-water rates — so that scarce things were rationed out in the open, by price, instead of quietly, by shortage, queue, and luck.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Spreading a fixed cost over more units makes average cost fall; scaled to the whole operation, that's economies of scale.
  • Forced through a bottleneck, production hits diminishing returns, so marginal cost rises — and MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimums.
  • The rising marginal-cost curve is the supply curve: higher prices are needed to make costlier units worthwhile.
  • A price set where supply meets demand rations a scarce good by willingness to pay; hold the price down and a shortage rations it instead — by queue and luck. The rationing never disappears, only its currency.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • When an industry's costs keep climbing, is it missing competition (a Christensen opening) or running into cost disease (a Baumol wall)? The cure differs entirely.
  • Should we subsidize services whose costs are structurally high — care, teaching, the arts — or treat the high price as a signal to ration them?
  • When a price climbs because demand is crowding a fixed supply — a surge fare, a hot-day Coke, a scarce dose of insulin — is the seller rationing honestly or exploiting a captive buyer? The answer leans on how badly the buyer can do without. Ch 16
Takeaways
In your life

Every choice has a cost in roads not taken. Name it honestly — then ask whether it's a real wall or just the comfortable old way of doing things.

In your work

When output stalls, don't pile more onto the abundant input — find the bottleneck. The constraint that matters is the one quietly capping everything else.
