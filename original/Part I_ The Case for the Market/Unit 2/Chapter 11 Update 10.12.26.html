Unit 2 · The Logic of Competition

Firm Model III: The Long Run

Easy come, easy go

Chapter 11 ~ 26 min read

Try to name a company that has earned large profits for more than a hundred years without some kind of protection: a government license, a patent, a trade barrier, an exclusive franchise, a network nobody else can copy. It sounds easy. Big old companies are everywhere, and famous brands last for generations. But big profits that last are much harder to find than big companies, because profit attracts attention.

When one firm finds an easy way to make money, other firms notice. They copy it, enter the market, undercut the price, and improve on the original. What started as a brilliant opportunity becomes ordinary. The market doesn't let an easy profit sit quietly in a corner; it points at it.

Chapter 10 showed what a firm does with the plant it has, this month. But give it time and everything is on the table: it can build a bigger plant, sell the one it has, or leave the industry, and so can everyone else. So what happens to a profit once every rival is free to chase it? And what is left for the firm that found it first?

The big picture
Competition is the consumer's ally. In the long run, nothing is fixed: firms choose how big to build, enter markets where profit beckons, and leave markets that lose money. Free entry pushes economic profit toward zero and hands much of the gain to buyers, which is hard on firms and good for everyone else. Firms that want lasting profit have two choices: keep creating new value, or block the door. By the end you should be able to read the long-run average cost curve, trace the long-run adjustment on side-by-side graphs, state the long-run equilibrium condition and explain why it is efficient, and tell profit that was earned from profit that is protected.

Major concept 1 · Nothing is fixed

The Long Run and Economies of Scale

For two chapters, something has stayed fixed: the oven, the lease, the building. That is what made it the short run . Ch 9 The long run is long enough that every input can change: sign a bigger lease, buy a second oven, build a new kitchen, or sell the whole thing. Neither is a set number of months. A food truck's long run might be a few weeks; a chip factory's is years. The long run is however long it takes to change the slowest input.

One consequence follows directly: in the long run, there are no fixed costs. The rent you're locked into today becomes, at renewal, one option among several. That is the formal version of the "cost as opportunity" reading from Chapter 9: given enough time, a firm can rethink any cost it now treats as fixed.

Choosing a plant. Suppose the workshop is planning ahead and can choose among four sizes of shop. Once built, each has its own fixed cost and its own bottleneck, so each has its own short-run ATC curve — its own U. The small shop is the workshop from Chapter 9, which does best at 4 units. Bigger shops cost more to run at low output but can handle more before they jam. For whatever output it plans to produce, the firm picks the shop with the lowest average cost. Read down any column of the table: the lowest number is the long-run average cost of that output.

What to notice: the bottoms of the U's fall, hold level, then climb. With more shop sizes to choose from, the scallops smooth into the single U-shaped LRATC curve drawn on the exam.

Traced across every output, those lowest costs form the long-run average total cost curve (LRATC). Its shape has three parts.

Economies of scale. Where LRATC falls, bigger is cheaper per unit: the firm has economies of scale . A bigger operation can split work into narrower specialties, run larger and faster machines, and buy inputs in bulk. Costco stocks only about 4,000 items, so it buys each in enormous volume and negotiates with suppliers in a way a corner store never could. Where up-front costs are huge and each extra copy is cheap, the effect is dramatic: a drug may cost a billion dollars to develop and pennies to manufacture, and the same holds for a film, a video game, or a semiconductor fab.

Constant returns to scale. Where LRATC is flat, doubling every input roughly doubles output, so cost per unit holds steady and a firm can grow without getting cheaper or costlier per unit. The lowest output at which LRATC reaches its minimum is the minimum efficient scale — 8 units in the table. When that scale is small relative to the market, as for restaurants, salons, and contractors, small and large firms survive side by side.

Diseconomies of scale. Where LRATC rises, the firm has grown past its best size and bigger is costlier per unit: diseconomies of scale . The bottleneck returns in a new form. In the long run you can buy another oven, but you can't easily buy more of the founder's attention. As a firm adds layers, information passes through more hands, decisions wait on more approvals, and more of the payroll goes to coordinating the work rather than doing it.

Keep the two time horizons separate. A rising short-run curve comes from piling variable inputs onto a fixed one; a rising long-run curve comes from an organization too large to coordinate. Both raise costs, for different reasons. Your exam checks whether you can tell them apart.

Reflection · why big firms often have an edge

In college, my business strategy professor asked us why Walmart so clearly dominates its market. Our answer: "because… Walmart is big?" It's tempting to think large firms dominate because they're powerful. Often the causation runs the other way. Walmart didn't start big; it revolutionized its supply chain, and the cost advantage that produced is what let it grow. Amazon's warehouses and a chipmaker's fab work the same way: when minimum efficient scale is enormous, smaller rivals face a higher cost per unit, and only a few firms can survive. Whether that scale passes savings to customers or walls out competitors is the question Concept 4 starts to answer.

Retrieval practice From memory: define the short run and the long run, describe the three parts of the LRATC curve and what causes each, and explain why rising short-run marginal cost is not the same thing as diseconomies of scale. ▸

In the short run at least one input is fixed; in the long run every input can change. Neither is a set length of time. LRATC falls where there are economies of scale (specialization, bigger machines, bulk buying, huge up-front costs spread over huge output), is flat under constant returns (minimum efficient scale is where the flat part begins), and rises under diseconomies of scale (coordination costs of a too-large organization). Rising short-run MC comes from diminishing marginal returns — variable inputs piled onto a fixed one. Diseconomies of scale happen when nothing is fixed and the organization itself is the problem. A common slip is calling any rising cost "diseconomies of scale."

Choosing a plant is one long-run decision. The bigger one is whether to be in the industry at all, and that is where profit comes in.

Major concept 2 · Profit is a flare

Entry, Exit, and the Long-Run Adjustment

In the short run, profit is a scoreboard: it tells the firm whether it is beating its next-best alternative. In the long run, profit is also a flare. Everyone else can see it, and in the long run they can act on it.

Entry is what happens when profit draws in competitors: new firms start up and existing ones expand, so market supply rises. Exit is what happens when losses push firms out: they close, sell their equipment, and move their money and people elsewhere, so market supply falls. Exit is not the same as the short-run shutdown from Chapter 10. A firm that shuts down still owes its fixed costs and can reopen next week; a firm that exits has escaped them for good. Ch 10

Trace the profit case on two graphs at once. On the firm's graph, price is above ATC, so there is a profit rectangle. That profit draws in new firms, which shifts the market supply curve to the right. The market price falls, and since each firm takes that price as given, its horizontal price line falls too. Each step down shrinks the profit rectangle. Entry keeps going until the rectangle is gone: price has fallen to the bottom of ATC, and economic profit is zero.

Losses run the same story backward. Firms exit, market supply shifts left, the price rises, and the remaining firms' losses shrink until price is back at the bottom of ATC. Easy profits come and go for the same reason: anything easy to earn is easy to copy.

Fig 11.2 Profit invites entry. New firms shift market supply from S₁ to S₂, and the price falls from P₁ to P₂. Each firm's price line falls with it, and the profit rectangle shrinks until P₂ sits at the bottom of ATC.
What to notice: the firm produces less after entry (q₂ is left of q₁), even though the market produces more. More firms, each one smaller.
Fig 11.3 Losses push firms out. Exit shifts market supply from S₁ to S₂, and the price rises from P₁ to P₂. The surviving firms' loss shrinks to zero as the price climbs to the bottom of ATC.
What to notice: at P₁ these firms are above AVC, so in the short run they keep producing (Chapter 10). In the long run, a loss that never ends is a reason to leave.

Worked example Trace the long-run adjustment

Firms in a competitive industry are earning economic profit, and entry is open.

  1. Start on the firm's graph: at Q*, P > ATC, so firms earn economic profit.
  2. Profit attracts entry. On the market graph, supply shifts right.
  3. The market price falls. On the firm's graph, the horizontal P = MR line falls with it.
  4. Entry continues until price reaches the bottom of ATC, where economic profit is zero.
End state: P = MR = MC = min ATC. For losses, run every step in reverse: exit, supply shifts left, price rises.
Quick check A competitive firm is earning economic profit. Predict what happens in the long run if entry is open. ▸
New firms enter, market supply shifts right, the price falls, and each firm's profit shrinks until it reaches zero economic profit.
Quick check Restaurants in a town are losing money. In the long run, what happens to the number of restaurants and the price of a meal? ▸
Some restaurants exit. With fewer sellers, supply falls and the price of a meal rises until the survivors are back to zero economic profit.
Quick check After entry, the market sells more, but each firm sells less. How can both be true? ▸
There are more firms. The lower price moves each firm down its MC curve to a smaller quantity, but the extra firms more than make up for it.
Retrieval practice Explain the line "profit is a flare," and trace what entry does on both the market graph and the firm's graph. ▸

Profit rewards the firm that found value, but it also signals the opportunity to everyone else. If entry is open, rivals enter, market supply shifts right, and the price falls. Each firm's horizontal price line falls with it, shrinking the profit rectangle until price reaches the bottom of ATC and economic profit is zero. A common slip is to shift only the firm's graph; the price changes because the market supply curve shifts.

Major concept 3 · Where the flare goes out

Long-Run Equilibrium and Efficiency

Entry continues while firms earn economic profit, and exit continues while they take losses. The movement stops only when economic profit is zero. Every firm is then producing where price equals marginal cost (its profit-maximizing quantity) and where price equals average total cost (zero profit). Both can be true only at the bottom of ATC, where MC crosses it. That gives the long-run equilibrium condition for a perfectly competitive firm: P = MR = MC = min ATC .

Zero economic profit sounds like failure, but it is the normal profit from Chapter 10. Ch 10 The owner covers every cost, including what her time and money could earn elsewhere. She is doing exactly as well as her next-best option: well enough to stay, not well enough to draw in anyone new.

Fig 11.4 Long-run equilibrium in perfect competition. The price line touches the bottom of ATC, exactly where MC crosses it. The firm earns zero economic profit and has no reason to enter or leave.
On the exam: zero economic profit is not shutdown. The firm covers every cost, including normal profit. Shutdown is a short-run decision about price and AVC.

One more consequence. If new firms can enter with the same costs as the old ones, the industry can grow without its costs rising, so in the long run it will supply any quantity at the same price: the bottom of ATC. Its long-run supply curve is horizontal. Economists call that a constant-cost industry . If growth bids up the price of something the industry needs (land, specialized workers), each new firm costs a little more to run, and long-run supply slopes upward instead.

Worked example How many firms?

A competitive industry has constant costs. Each firm's ATC bottoms out at $12 when it produces 50 units. At a price of $12, buyers want 6,000 units.

  1. Long-run price: P = min ATC = $12 .
  2. Each firm produces where its ATC is lowest: 50 units .
  3. Number of firms = market quantity ÷ output per firm = 6,000 ÷ 50 = 120 firms .
120 firms, each making 50 units at $12. If demand rose so that buyers wanted 7,500 at $12, entry would continue until there were 7,500 ÷ 50 = 150 firms, and the price would end up back at $12.

That end point is good news for buyers. Economists don't usually respond to profit from a good idea by saying, "Cap it." They ask, "Can competitors enter?" If entry is open, the first firm's profit summons rivals, supply expands, and the price falls until buyers capture most of the gain. A firm wants its profit to last; consumers want the value to spread. Free entry is how a private discovery becomes a public gain.

Long-run perfect competition also hits two kinds of efficiency at once. Allocative efficiency means P = MC : the value buyers place on one more unit equals the cost of producing it, so no unit worth more than it costs goes unmade, and no unit is made that costs more than it is worth. Productive efficiency means producing at min ATC : each unit is made at the lowest cost per unit the technology allows.

Quick check In long-run equilibrium, P = ATC. Should firms enter, exit, or stay? ▸
Stay. P = ATC means zero economic profit: no extra profit to attract entry, and no loss to force exit.
Quick check Which efficiency condition is P = MC? Which is producing at minimum ATC? ▸
P = MC is allocative efficiency: the right amount gets made. Producing at minimum ATC is productive efficiency: it gets made at the lowest cost per unit.
Quick check Each firm's ATC is lowest at $8, at 200 units. At $8, buyers want 30,000 units. How many firms are there in long-run equilibrium? ▸
30,000 ÷ 200 = 150 firms , each producing 200 units at $8.
Retrieval practice State the long-run equilibrium condition for a competitive firm, and explain why zero economic profit is not failure. ▸

P = MR = MC = min ATC. Price equals MC because the firm maximizes profit; price equals ATC because entry and exit have pushed economic profit to zero; both hold only at the bottom of ATC. Zero economic profit is normal profit: the firm covers every cost, including the owner's opportunity cost, so the owner does as well as her next-best option. A common slip is to treat zero economic profit as zero accounting profit, or as a reason to shut down.

Major concept 4 · Create value or block entry

Two Ways to Keep Profit

If entry erodes profit, a firm that wants its profit to last has two broad options. The first is to keep creating value: lower costs, improve quality, earn trust, solve the next problem before anyone else does. The second is to make entry harder: lobby, license, restrict, exclude, and turn policy into a moat. To see the difference clearly, start with the businesses most often accused of creating nothing.

The wholesaler, the broker, the ride app, the reseller: they "make nothing," and the popular verdict is that they are parasites skimming a cut between the people who do the real work. "Cut out the middleman" is one of the most common pieces of business advice there is. The economist's reply is that the middleman is often the one creating the most value in the chain. You just can't see it, because what they produce isn't a thing.

The clearest account comes from the Duke economist Michael Munger, who argues that firms like Uber don't really sell rides. They sell reductions in transaction costs . A transaction cost is everything that stands between you and a deal, beyond the price itself, and Munger sorts it into three: triangulation (finding the right partner and agreeing on a price), transfer (actually paying and delivering), and trust (believing the other side will come through). Before Uber, a stranger's car was useless to you: you couldn't find it, couldn't price it, couldn't trust the driver. The app didn't build a car; it manufactured triangulation, transfer, and trust, and that is a real product. To a customer, a dollar of hassle removed is worth as much as a dollar off the price.

Which gives us the honest line. Picture two ways to make money on a river. One person dredges the channel so barges pass faster and cheaper: a real cost falls, value is created, and the fee is earned. Another stretches a chain across the water and charges boats to lower it: nothing moves faster and no cost falls; they have added an obstacle and rented out the key. Same fee collected, opposite economics. The first creates value; the second only extracts it, which economists call rent-seeking . The test for any middleman, any platform's cut, any toll: did a real cost fall because you're here, or did you just hang a chain across the river?

Blocking entry is the chain across the river, applied to a whole market. A barrier to entry lets economic profit last because the flare goes up but rivals can't answer it. Some barriers come from the economics itself: when minimum efficient scale is enormous (Concept 1), a newcomer would have to start huge to match the incumbents' costs. Others are written into law: licensing requirements, exclusive franchises, permits that take years, subsidies only incumbents can get, and patents. Precision matters here. Some protections are worth their cost: patents reward invention, and some licenses protect safety. The economist's question is not "rule or no rule?" It is "does this rule solve a real problem at a reasonable cost, or does it mainly protect insiders from competition?"

That points to the policy question. A contestable market is one that rivals can enter and challenge, and even the threat of entry keeps incumbents honest. So when profit persists, don't ask only "why are these firms greedy?" Ask "why hasn't entry happened?" Sometimes the industry is genuinely hard. Sometimes entry is blocked by law, paperwork, or privilege, and then the cure is to remove the block. Trade is one of the most powerful forms of entry: imports are competitors. A tariff (a tax on imports) or a quota (a limit on them) is a barrier to entry by another name. It protects domestic producers' profits from competition and makes buyers pay more. Trade gets its own chapter later in the course.

Policy rule · attack protected profit, not profit

If high profit comes from lower cost, better quality, or a newly discovered need, let it summon competitors. If high profit survives because entry is blocked, break the blockage: needless permits, exclusive privileges, incumbent subsidies, and trade barriers. The goal is not to punish value creation. It is to keep markets open enough that value spreads.

Rules of business success · Rule #5: build the habit, not the hit

One profitable idea will be copied. Become the kind of firm, or person, that keeps finding new value. And look where others won't: when everyone runs away from a hard, unglamorous need, run toward it. The problem others avoid may be the one customers most need solved.

Quick check A wholesaler buys from 200 farms and sells to 50 grocery stores, "making nothing." In transaction-cost terms, what is the wholesaler producing? ▸
Triangulation and transfer at scale. Instead of 200 × 50 separate searches, negotiations, and deliveries, each side deals once with the wholesaler. The cost that falls is all that matching and moving, and that reduction is the product.
Quick check Apply the river test: a ticket platform that instantly matches fans to verified resale seats, versus a scalper who buys up a whole show and resells at a markup. ▸
It depends on whether a real cost fell. Matching fans to verified seats lowers triangulation and trust costs: coordination. Buying up supply only to resell adds no matching or trust; if it is just cornering tickets to charge more, that is closer to the chain across the river. Reasonable people argue the line, which is the point.
Quick check Give one example of renewing value and one of protecting profit. ▸
Renewing value: a firm cuts delivery costs with better logistics. Protecting profit: a firm lobbies for a rule that keeps qualified rivals out.
Quick check Why can imports act like entry? ▸
Foreign producers compete with domestic firms, add to supply, and push the price down, limiting how much excess profit domestic firms can keep. Tariffs and quotas weaken that pressure.

The moves you can now make

Four questions at exam level: profit to entry, loss to exit, how many firms, and blocked entry. Try each on paper first, then reveal.

Type A · long-run adjustment from profit

A perfectly competitive firm is earning economic profit. Explain what happens in the long run, using both the market graph and the firm's graph.

Show model answer ▸

Profit attracts entry. On the market graph, supply shifts right and the price falls. On the firm's graph, the horizontal P = MR line falls with the market price, and the firm moves down its MC curve to a smaller quantity. Entry continues until price equals the bottom of ATC. In long-run equilibrium, P = MR = MC = min ATC, and firms earn zero economic profit.

Type B · long-run adjustment from loss

A perfectly competitive firm is suffering economic losses but covering its variable costs. What does it do in the short run, and what happens in the long run?

Show model answer ▸

In the short run it keeps producing, because price is above AVC (Chapter 10). In the long run, losses cause exit. On the market graph, supply shifts left and the price rises. On the firm's graph, the P = MR line rises. Exit continues until the remaining firms earn zero economic profit, where P = min ATC.

Type C · number of firms

A constant-cost competitive industry is in long-run equilibrium. Each firm's ATC reaches its minimum of $20 at 400 units. At $20, buyers want 48,000 units. Find the price, each firm's output, and the number of firms. Then demand rises so that buyers want 60,000 units at $20. What happens in the long run?

Show worked solution ▸
Worked example Price, output, and number of firms
  1. Long-run price = min ATC = $20 .
  2. Each firm produces where ATC is lowest: 400 units .
  3. Firms = 48,000 ÷ 400 = 120 .
  4. After the demand increase, the price rises briefly and firms earn profit, which draws in entry. Supply expands until the price is back at $20: 60,000 ÷ 400 = 150 firms .
In a constant-cost industry, demand changes the number of firms in the long run, not the price.

Type D · barriers to entry

A domestic industry earns economic profit year after year. Imports are limited by a quota, and new domestic firms need permits that take three years to get. Explain why profit persists, and what policy would make the market more contestable.

Show model answer ▸

Profit persists because entry is blocked. The quota limits foreign entry through imports, and the slow permits discourage domestic entry. Without new supply, the price stays above ATC. To make the market contestable, remove or loosen the quota and streamline needless permitting, while keeping rules that solve a real safety or information problem.

Reveal a full-credit response ▸

(a) The market graph shows supply and demand crossing at $40. On the firm's graph, a horizontal line at $40 is the firm's demand, MR, and AR; MC crosses it at 300 units. At 300, ATC ($53) is above the price and AVC ($37) is below it, and MC passes through the bottoms of AVC and ATC.

(b) Loss = (ATC − P) × Q = (53 − 40) × 300 = $3,900 .

(c) Produce. The price ($40) is above AVC ($37), so producing covers variable cost and pays part of fixed cost; shutting down would lose all of fixed cost, (53 − 37) × 300 = $4,800.

(d) Losses cause firms to exit . Market supply shifts left, and the market price rises .

(e) The price rises until it equals minimum ATC: $51 . Each remaining firm produces 400 units , where MC = min ATC.

(f) Zero economic profit. This is normal profit: the firm covers every cost, including the owner's opportunity cost, so there is no reason to enter or exit.

Market thesis

In free markets, the main ways to get rich are to provide value or to cheat the system. Competition is the consumer's ally: free entry turns one firm's discovery into everyone's lower price, and drives profit toward zero unless something blocks the door.

If economists ruled the world…

we would make markets easier to enter and let competition solve the profit problem. If entry is open, let profit call competitors in. If entry is blocked, break the blockage. And we'd stop calling middlemen parasites: we'd judge them by whether a real cost fell because they showed up.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Economic profit attracts entry; economic losses cause exit.
  • Long-run equilibrium in perfect competition is P = MR = MC = min ATC, with zero economic profit.
  • Free entry pushes firms toward allocative efficiency (P = MC) and productive efficiency (min ATC).
  • LRATC falls with economies of scale and rises with diseconomies of scale.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Which barriers to entry protect consumers, and which mainly protect incumbents?
  • When is a middleman a coordinator earning its keep, and when is it a gatekeeper charging a toll: the reseller, the platform's cut, the licensing board?
  • When should policy defend a temporary profit as the reward for a discovery, and when should it attack a profit that is protected?
Takeaways
In your life

When everyone runs away, run toward. Not toward danger for its own sake, but toward real needs others avoid because they are difficult, unglamorous, or uncomfortable.

In your work

Build the habit, not the hit. Don't expect one profitable position to stay untouched. Keep learning, improving, and finding the next need to fill.
