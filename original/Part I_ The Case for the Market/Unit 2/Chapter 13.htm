Unit 3 · How Markets Move Resources

Factor Markets I: Capital and Land

Where the future gets built

Chapter 13 ~ 41 min read

Two cities are growing. In one, more people want to live there and the city builds: apartments above shops, townhomes near transit, backyard cottages, taller buildings where the jobs are. In the other, more people want to live there and the city says no: zoning fights, parking mandates, height limits, neighborhood vetoes, endless hearings. Both cities receive the same economic signal. People want the place. Only one lets resources respond.

That is the central question of this chapter: when the world discovers a better use for a resource, can that resource move? A vacant lot can become housing. A warehouse can become a studio. Savings can become machines, software, tools, buildings, and research. A restaurant that fails can release its kitchen, workers, and equipment for something people value more. The economy is not a museum of current uses. It is a system for discovering better ones.

Capitalism's superpower is not profit by itself. It is profit and loss. Profit pulls resources toward uses consumers value; loss pushes resources away from uses that waste them. That movement can be uncomfortable. Joseph Schumpeter called it creative destruction : progress creates new value partly by destroying the market position of older, weaker uses. But if land cannot be built on, if permits take years, if incumbents can veto change, or if capital is rescued from every loss, the economy keeps feeding old ideas while better ones wait outside the gate.

The big picture
Factor markets are where the future gets assembled from scarce inputs. Firms do not merely sell pizzas, phones, apartments, and software; they buy ovens, trucks, buildings, code, land, and labor. Those inputs have prices because they have other possible uses. The whole chapter turns on one claim: capitalism's superpower is moving resources away from bad ideas and toward better ones. That power depends on two disciplines at once: profit must be allowed to pull capital and land toward higher-valued uses, and loss must be allowed to push them away from weaker ones.

Major concept 1

Factor Markets: Inputs Have Markets Too

The market does not stop at the cash register. It reaches backward into the ovens, buildings, trucks, land, and tools that make output possible.

In a product market, households demand goods and services while firms supply them. In a factor market , the roles flip around. Firms demand inputs, while households, investors, landowners, and other resource owners supply them. A bakery sells bread in the product market, but it buys flour, leases ovens, rents space, borrows funds, and hires workers in factor markets.

This matters because the chapter is about movement. Chapter 11 showed how firms compare price and cost in the short run. Chapter 12 showed how profit attracts entry and loss forces exit in the long run. But entry and exit are not magic words. For a new restaurant to open, someone has to finance equipment, rent space, and redirect labor. For a new apartment building to appear, land has to be allowed to change use. Factor markets are where the resources actually move.

The price of a factor is usually called a rental rate . For land, this is easy to picture: the rent paid for a location. For capital, it means the price of using a machine, building, truck, or piece of equipment for a period of time. Even if a firm owns the machine, using it still has an opportunity cost: the machine could have been leased, sold, or used for something else.

Quick check A coffee shop leases an espresso machine for $900 per month. In which market is the shop operating, and what is the $900? ▸
A factor market for capital services. The $900 is the rental rate: the price of using the machine for that month.
Retrieval practice In one sentence, contrast product markets and factor markets. ▸
In product markets, firms supply goods and households demand them; in factor markets, firms demand productive inputs and resource owners supply them.
Major concept 2

Derived Demand: Inputs Are Valuable Because Outputs Are Valuable

Nobody wants an oven for its own sake. The oven matters because people want pizza.

A firm demands an input because that input helps produce output consumers want. This is derived demand . Demand for delivery trucks comes from demand for delivered goods. Demand for downtown land comes from demand to live, work, and shop downtown. Demand for cloud servers comes from demand for apps and websites that run on them.

This is the first place students often slip. In ordinary speech, capital often means money. In economics, capital is not mainly cash; it is produced tools used to make other goods and services. The money used to buy those tools is financial capital . A pizza oven is capital. The loan used to buy it is financial capital.

The logic behind input demand is simple: an input is worth using when the extra value it helps create is greater than its cost. Chapter 14 will formalize this as marginal revenue product. Here, keep the intuition. A bakery wants another oven only if that oven lets it sell enough additional bread to justify the cost of the oven, the space, the maintenance, and the funds tied up in it.

Worked example Should the firm invest?

A food truck can buy new refrigeration equipment for $40,000. The equipment is expected to raise annual revenue, net of extra operating costs, by $46,000. The next-best use of the funds would earn $3,500.

  1. Expected net return before opportunity cost: $46,000 - $40,000 = $6,000.
  2. Compare that $6,000 to the opportunity cost of the funds: $3,500.
  3. Because $6,000 is greater than $3,500, the investment passes the market test.
Decision: invest, if the estimate is credible and risk is acceptable. Capital moves toward the higher-valued use.
Quick check If demand for electric vehicles rises, explain what happens to demand for battery factories, charging equipment, and engineers. ▸
Demand for those inputs rises because it is derived from demand for the output. Consumers want more EVs, so firms want more of the capital and labor needed to produce and support EVs.
Major concept 3

Profit and Loss: The Steering Wheel

Profit says, "more resources here." Loss says, "stop feeding this." The system needs both voices.

Capital is scarce. The economy cannot fund every startup, build every factory, buy every machine, preserve every old business, and develop every possible technology. Every dollar, building, acre, and machine used for one project is unavailable for another. So the economy needs a steering mechanism. In markets, that mechanism is profit and loss.

Profit is the pull. It tells owners and investors that consumers value this output more than the resources used to produce it. Loss is the push. It tells owners and investors that resources are being used in a way that fails the market test. The hard part is that loss feels like failure, embarrassment, and disappointment. But economically, loss is information. It releases capital from weak uses so it can move toward stronger ones.

This is why creative destruction is both beautiful and brutal. Streaming did not merely create a new way to watch movies; it destroyed much of the value of DVD rental stores. Better software does not merely help new firms; it makes old workflows obsolete. New housing does not merely create homes; it can replace low-density land uses that some people liked. The destruction is painful, but freezing the old use in place has a cost too: the better use never gets built.

Rules of business success · Rule #4: reallocate capital

Move capital away from weak uses and toward stronger ones. Do not keep funding a bad idea because you love it, already paid for it, or are embarrassed to quit. The discipline is not "never fail." It is "do not feed failure forever." The loss is the message.

AP calculation Expected return and opportunity cost

A manufacturer can buy a machine for $100,000. It expects the machine to add $115,000 in revenue after extra operating costs. The interest rate is 12%, so the funds have an opportunity cost of $12,000.

  1. Expected net return = expected revenue gain - cost of capital = $115,000 - $100,000 = $15,000.
  2. Opportunity cost of funds = $12,000.
  3. Because $15,000 exceeds $12,000, the machine is expected to create more value here than in the next-best use.
Invest. If the expected net return were below the opportunity cost, the firm should move the capital somewhere else.

One warning belongs here. Sometimes policymakers decide a firm is too big to fail . During a financial panic, emergency intervention may prevent a broader collapse. But economists worry when rescue becomes expected. If investors keep the upside while taxpayers absorb the downside, that creates moral hazard . Capital stops learning from loss, and the steering wheel gets loose.

Retrieval practice Explain why capitalism's steering mechanism is profit and loss, not profit alone. ▸
Profit pulls resources toward uses consumers value more than their cost. Loss pushes resources away from uses that waste scarce resources. Without loss, weak uses keep absorbing capital and better uses may never get funded.
Major concept 4

Land and Economic Rent: Location Is Scarce

You can build more chairs, trucks, and ovens. You cannot build another Manhattan.

Land is different from most capital because the physical supply of valuable locations is fixed or highly constrained. There are only so many acres in a city center, only so many lots near a train station, only so many beachfront parcels, only so much mineral-rich land. When demand for a location rises and supply cannot respond much, rent rises sharply.

That rising payment is often called economic rent . The word does not mean "apartment rent" only. It means a payment above what is necessary to keep a factor in its current use. Land is the classic case because a fixed location does not need a higher payment in order to keep existing physically. The land is there either way, so demand pressure shows up as a higher price.

The important nuance is this: the physical land is fixed, but the usable services from land are not completely fixed. A city cannot create another downtown parcel, but it can allow a taller building on that parcel. It can legalize duplexes, apartments, mixed-use buildings, backyard cottages, and transit-oriented development. Building upward or using land more intensively increases the effective supply of housing or commercial space.

Fig 13.2 A fixed land supply. When demand for a location rises from D1 to D2, the quantity of physical land cannot rise, so rent rises.
AP watch: with a vertical supply curve, a demand increase raises price/rent but does not increase the fixed physical quantity.
Quick check If a coastal city becomes more popular but the supply of buildable coastal land is fixed, what happens to land rent? ▸
Land rent rises. Demand shifts right, supply is fixed or highly inelastic, so the higher demand shows up mainly as a higher rental rate.
Major concept 5

NIMBYism: When Better Uses Are Made Illegal

When people want more of a place, the compassionate answer is often to let more of that place exist.

NIMBYism is the land-market version of blocking reallocation. Demand says, "more people want to live here." The rules say, "no." Zoning may limit apartments, ban mixed-use buildings, require large lots, impose parking mandates, cap building height, or make permits slow and uncertain. The result is a housing supply curve that is steeper, less responsive, or capped below the quantity people want.

Do not make the local concerns look silly. Traffic can be real. School capacity can be real. Infrastructure and environmental questions can be real. Neighborhood change can feel like loss. The economist's objection is not that every project is perfect. The objection is that blocking nearly all new building is a costly and exclusionary way to handle those concerns. It protects some insiders while pushing renters, newcomers, young families, workers, and future residents out.

If economists ruled the world, housing policy would default toward allowing more homes where people want to live: more legal density, faster permitting, fewer excessive parking mandates, apartments near jobs and transit, and land-use rules that let resources move toward higher-valued uses. In plain English: NIMBYism is bad. Build, baby, build.

Fig 13.3 Housing supply restrictions. When supply is made less elastic, demand pressure turns into higher prices and too little quantity.
AP watch: if demand rises while supply is restricted, price rises more and quantity rises less than it would under flexible supply.
Policy translation What "build" actually means
  • Legalize more homes in high-demand places, especially near jobs, schools, and transit.
  • Let land move from lower-valued to higher-valued uses instead of freezing current uses by default.
  • Handle real congestion and infrastructure problems directly rather than using them as a blanket reason to ban new housing.
  • Remember who pays for scarcity: renters, newcomers, workers, young families, and people who never get to move where opportunity is.
Retrieval practice Explain why restricting housing supply raises rents when demand rises. ▸
When demand rises, people want more housing at each price. If supply is restricted by zoning, permitting, parking mandates, or height limits, quantity cannot expand much. The pressure is absorbed mainly by higher rent or price rather than by more homes.
AP Micro practice

Factor markets, land, and capital discipline

Use the tabs for quick AP-style practice, then try the FRQ. Graph labels matter.

Concept explanation

A city experiences a surge in demand for restaurant meals. Explain how this affects demand for commercial kitchen space, ovens, and downtown restaurant locations.

Reveal answer ▸

Demand for those inputs rises because factor demand is derived demand. Consumers demand more restaurant meals, so firms demand more of the inputs that help produce those meals: kitchen space, ovens, and valuable locations.

Calculation

A firm can buy equipment for $80,000. It expects the equipment to generate $91,000 in additional revenue after operating costs. The next-best use of the funds would return $14,000. Should the firm invest? Show your work.

Reveal answer ▸

Expected net return = $91,000 - $80,000 = $11,000. The opportunity cost is $14,000. Since $11,000 is less than $14,000, the firm should not invest; the funds have a higher-valued alternative use.

Graph move

Draw a land market for a fixed downtown location. Demand for downtown offices increases. Show the effect on rent and quantity of land.

Reveal answer ▸

Draw rent on the vertical axis and quantity of land on the horizontal axis. Draw a vertical supply curve for land. Shift demand right. Rent rises; the fixed quantity of physical land does not increase.

Reveal scoring guide ▸
  • 1 pt Defines derived demand as input demand that comes from demand for output, such as demand for buildable land derived from demand for housing.
  • 1 pt Correct axes and labels: rent/price on vertical, quantity of housing on horizontal, demand and restricted supply labeled.

Reveal common mistakes ▸
  • Calling capital "money" without distinguishing financial capital from physical capital.
  • Drawing a product market graph but forgetting that the housing graph's vertical axis is rent or price and the quantity is housing.
  • Saying "land supply increases" when the graph is about fixed physical land. Usable space can increase through denser building, but the physical land curve is vertical.
  • Blaming high rent only on greed while ignoring restricted supply. Greed does not explain why prices rise more in places where building is illegal or slow.
Market thesis

Capitalism's superpower is moving resources away from bad ideas and toward better ones. Profit pulls capital and land toward uses consumers value; loss pushes them away from uses that waste scarce resources. The system works only when resources are allowed to move and when both profit and loss are allowed to speak.

If economists ruled the world...

housing policy would default toward allowing more homes where people want to live. We would be much less impressed by rules that preserve low-density neighborhoods while pushing rents beyond reach. NIMBYism is bad. Build, baby, build.

  • How much local control should incumbent residents have over who gets to live nearby?

Takeaways
In your life

Your time, attention, skills, and savings are capital too. Do not pour them endlessly into commitments that no longer create value just because quitting feels like admitting defeat. Sometimes the brave move is releasing resources for the better use.

In your work

Move capital away from weak uses and toward stronger ones. The sunk cost is gone. The forward-looking question is where the next dollar, hour, room, or tool can create the most value.
