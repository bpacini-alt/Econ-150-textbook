Unit 2 · The Logic of Competition

Profit Maximization II: The Long Run

Easy Come, Easy Go

Chapter 12 ~ 46 min read

Find a company that has earned large profits for more than 100 years without enjoying some kind of government protection, legal barrier, network privilege, patent shelter, trade restriction, exclusive privilege, or durable moat. It sounds easy. Big old companies exist. Famous brands exist. But sustained large economic profits are harder to find than people expect, because profit attracts attention.

If one firm discovers an easy way to make money, other firms notice. They copy, enter, imitate, invest, undercut, improve, and expand. What was once a brilliant opportunity becomes normal. The market does not let an easy profit sit quietly in the corner. It points at it.

Profit is a flare. In Chapter 11, profit told the firm, "You found something consumers value." In Chapter 12, profit tells everyone else, "Look over here." Entry is the market's way of spreading the gain. The first firm may have created the opportunity, but competition is what hands much of the value to consumers.

The big picture
Competition is the consumer's ally: free entry drives profit toward zero and hands the gains to buyers. Profit rewards the firm that found value in the short run, then summons competitors in the long run. That process is hard on firms and good for consumers. Firms that want long-run profit must either renew value again and again, or protect profit by blocking entry. The chapter's policy test is simple: attack protected profit, not profit.

Major concept 1

Profit Is an Invitation

The Chapter 11 scoreboard is public. Other firms can see it too.

In the long run , firms can do more than adjust output. They can enter a market, leave it, build capacity, sell equipment, hire, fire, copy, and expand. That changes the meaning of profit. Short-run profit says, "This firm found value." Long-run profit says, "Everyone, look over here."

Entry is what happens when profit attracts competitors. New firms enter, existing firms expand, and supply rises. Exit is what happens when losses push firms out. Firms leave, capacity shrinks, and supply falls.

The intuition is brutal and beautiful: easy profits are easy come and easy go because easy profits invite imitation. A firm may deserve the first reward for discovering value. But once the opportunity is visible, competition turns the discovery into a wider consumer benefit.

Fig 12.1 Short-run profit attracts entry. Entry shifts market supply right, lowers market price, and shrinks the individual firm's profit rectangle.
Fig 12.2 Short-run losses cause exit. Exit shifts market supply left, raises market price, and shrinks the remaining firms' losses toward zero economic profit.
Quick check A competitive firm is earning economic profit. Predict what happens in the long run if entry is open. ▸
New firms enter, market supply increases, price falls, and the firm's profit shrinks toward zero economic profit.
Retrieval practice Explain the line "profit is a flare" in your own words. ▸
Profit rewards the firm that discovered value, but it also signals the opportunity to competitors. If entry is open, rivals enter or expand, supply rises, price falls, and consumers capture much of the gain.
Major concept 2

Zero Economic Profit Is Not Failure

Zero economic profit means the firm is covering every opportunity cost. It does not mean the owner earns nothing.

In long-run perfect competition, entry continues while firms earn economic profit. Exit continues while firms suffer economic losses. The process stops at zero economic profit , also called normal profit .

This is the point students often misread. Zero economic profit does not mean "the owner earns nothing." It means the firm covers explicit costs and implicit opportunity costs. The owner is earning enough to keep the resources in this use, but not enough to attract new firms in.

The long-run competitive condition is:

P = MR = MC = min ATC

That single line carries a lot. Price equals marginal cost. The firm produces at the bottom of average total cost. And price equals ATC, so economic profit is zero.

Fig 12.3 Long-run equilibrium in perfect competition. The firm earns zero economic profit, produces at minimum ATC, and has no incentive to enter or exit.
AP warning: zero economic profit is not shutdown. It means all opportunity costs are covered.
Worked example Why zero is not nothing

A firm earns $500,000 in revenue, pays $430,000 in explicit costs, and uses owner time and capital worth $70,000 in their next-best use.

  1. Accounting profit = 500,000 - 430,000 = $70,000.
  2. Economic profit = 500,000 - 430,000 - 70,000 = $0.
  3. The owner is not unpaid. The owner's opportunity cost is fully covered.
Zero economic profit means normal profit. The firm is doing as well as its next-best alternative.
Quick check If P = ATC in long-run perfect competition, should firms enter, exit, or stay? ▸
Stay. P = ATC means zero economic profit: no excess profit to attract entry and no loss to force exit.
Retrieval practice Why is zero economic profit not the same as no accounting profit? ▸
Economic cost includes implicit opportunity costs. A firm can earn accounting profit while earning zero economic profit, because the accounting profit is just enough to cover the owner's foregone alternatives.
Major concept 3

Competition Hands the Gains to Consumers

The first firm discovers the value. Entry spreads it.

Economists do not usually respond to value-producing profit by saying, "Cap it." They usually ask, "Can competitors enter?" If entry is open, profit calls in rivals. Rivals expand supply, price falls, quality improves, and consumers inherit much of the original discovery.

That is why competition is uncomfortable for firms and wonderful for consumers. A firm wants the profit to last. Consumers want the value to spread. Free entry is the mechanism that turns a private discovery into a public gain.

Long-run perfect competition produces two efficiency results. Allocative efficiency means P = MC : the value consumers place on one more unit equals the cost of producing it. Productive efficiency means the firm produces at min ATC : output is produced at the lowest average cost.

Translation What the equations mean in English
  • P = MC: society is not leaving mutually valuable units unmade, and not making units that cost more than consumers value.
  • P = min ATC: firms are pushed to produce at the lowest average cost they can reach.
  • Zero economic profit: consumers receive much of the gain through lower prices instead of firms keeping excess profit forever.
Fig 12.4 Competition is hard on firms and good for consumers. Entry turns private discovery into lower prices, more output, and normal profit.
Quick check Which efficiency condition is P = MC? Which is producing at minimum ATC? ▸
P = MC is allocative efficiency. Producing at minimum ATC is productive efficiency.
Retrieval practice Why might economists prefer open entry to profit caps when profits come from value creation? ▸
Open entry lets profit summon competitors. Competitors expand supply, lower price, and pass much of the gain to consumers, while preserving the incentive for firms to discover new value in the first place.
Major concept 4

Two Ways to Keep Profit

Renew value, or protect profit. One helps consumers. The other often blocks them.

If entry erodes profit, firms that want profit to last face two broad strategies. The first is to keep creating value: innovate, lower cost, improve quality, earn trust, learn faster, solve the next problem. The second is to make entry harder: lobby, restrict, protect, license, exclude, subsidize incumbents, or turn policy into a moat.

A barrier to entry can let economic profit persist because the flare goes up but rivals cannot answer it. Barriers include regulatory walls, tariffs and quotas, exclusive franchises, certificate-of-need laws, zoning restrictions, incumbent subsidies, complex permitting, licensing walls, patents, and legal privileges.

Precision matters. Some protections can be defensible. Patents can encourage invention. Licensing can protect safety. Some rules reduce fraud or severe information problems. The economist's question is not "rule or no rule?" It is: does this rule solve a real problem at reasonable cost, or mainly protect insiders from competition?

Life and business · when everyone runs away, run toward
Mike Rowe's line, "when everyone runs away, run toward," fits this chapter because profit often appears where other people refuse to look: dirty work, awkward work, hard logistics, unglamorous repairs, boring compliance, neglected customers, and problems nobody wants to own. In markets, entry usually rushes toward easy profit. But long-run opportunity often belongs to the person or firm willing to run toward the hard problem others avoid. The rule is not recklessness. It is attention: when everyone flees a real need because it is difficult, low-status, or uncomfortable, there may be value waiting for the person disciplined enough to serve it well.
Business rule Build the habit, not the hit
  • Do not bet only on the horse. One profitable idea will be copied.
  • Bet on the jockey. Become the kind of person or firm that repeatedly finds new value.
  • Run toward real needs. The problem others avoid may be the problem consumers most need solved.
Quick check Give one example of value renewal and one example of protected profit. ▸
Value renewal: a firm lowers delivery costs through better logistics. Protected profit: a firm lobbies for a rule that prevents qualified rivals from entering.
Retrieval practice Why is "protect profit" different from "renew value"? ▸
Renewing value means earning profit by solving problems better. Protecting profit means keeping rivals out so the firm can keep excess returns without continuing to improve. The first tends to help consumers; the second often transfers wealth from consumers to incumbents.
Major concept 5

Contestability: Break Barriers and Let Entry Work

If entry is the mechanism, the policy question is what blocks entry.

A contestable market is one where potential rivals can enter and challenge incumbents. Contestability matters because even the threat of entry can discipline existing firms. If incumbents know rivals can enter, they have less room to keep prices high and quality low.

When profit persists, do not ask only, "Why are firms greedy?" Ask, "Why has entry not happened?" Sometimes entry is hard because the industry is genuinely difficult. Sometimes it is blocked by law, politics, paperwork, or privilege. If profit survives because entry is blocked, attack the barrier.

International trade is one of the most powerful forms of entry. Imports are competitors. A tariff and a quota are barriers to entry by another name. They may protect domestic producers, but they also protect domestic profits from the discipline of competition and make consumers pay more.

Fig 12.5 If entry is blocked, the long-run adjustment cannot finish. Price can remain above ATC, and consumers do not receive the full benefit of competition.
Policy rule · attack protected profit, not profit
If high profit comes from lower cost, better quality, or a discovered need, let it summon competitors. If high profit survives because entry is blocked, break the blockage. Reduce needless permitting, exclusive privileges, incumbent subsidies, regulatory capture, and trade barriers. The goal is not to punish value creation. The goal is to make markets contestable enough that value spreads.
Quick check Why can imports act like entry? ▸
Foreign producers can compete with domestic firms, increase supply, lower prices, and reduce domestic firms' ability to keep excess profit. Tariffs and quotas limit that entry pressure.
Retrieval practice What is the economist's test for a barrier to entry? ▸
Ask whether the rule solves a real consumer or safety problem at reasonable cost, or mainly protects incumbents from competition. The first may be defensible; the second is protected profit.
Practice — trace the long-run adjustment

The essential AP move is the adjustment story. Start with profit or loss. Predict entry or exit. Shift market supply. Show the price change. Move the firm's horizontal price line. End at P = MR = MC = min ATC

On the exam

The moves you can now make

Three AP-style moves: profit-entry, loss-exit, and barriers to entry. Try each before revealing.

Type A · long-run adjustment from profit

A perfectly competitive firm is earning economic profit. Explain what happens in the long run, using both the market and firm graphs.

Show model answer ▸

Profit attracts entry. In the market graph, entry shifts supply right and lowers price. In the firm graph, the horizontal P = MR line falls. Entry continues until price equals ATC at the firm's profit-maximizing output. In long-run equilibrium, P = MR = MC = min ATC and firms earn zero economic profit.

Type B · long-run adjustment from loss

A perfectly competitive firm is suffering economic losses. Explain what happens in the long run.

Show model answer ▸

Losses cause exit. In the market graph, exit shifts supply left and raises price. In the firm graph, the horizontal P = MR line rises. Exit continues until remaining firms earn zero economic profit, where P = ATC at Q*.

Type C · barriers

A domestic industry earns economic profit, but imports are limited by quotas and new domestic firms face complex permits. Explain why profit may persist and what policy would make the market more contestable.

Show model answer ▸

Profit may persist because entry is blocked. Quotas limit foreign entry through imports, and complex permits discourage domestic entry. If new supply cannot enter, price may stay above ATC. The contestability policy is to reduce or remove unnecessary quotas and permitting barriers while keeping rules that solve genuine safety or information problems.

Capstone · free response

Put the moves together

AP-style long free response · ~10 points · graph and explanation required

Firms in a perfectly competitive market are earning positive economic profit in the short run. Entry is legally open.

  1. On side-by-side market and firm graphs, show the short-run profit condition. (2 pt)
  2. Explain what happens to market supply as firms respond. (1 pt)
  3. Show the effect on market price and on the individual firm's horizontal price line. (2 pt)
  4. State the long-run equilibrium condition for a perfectly competitive firm. (2 pt)
  5. Explain why zero economic profit is not failure. (1 pt)
  6. Explain how a tariff could interrupt this adjustment if foreign producers are the likely entrants. (2 pt)
Reveal a full-credit response ▸

(a) The market graph begins at a price that gives the representative firm P > ATC at Q*, so the firm earns economic profit.

(b) Profit attracts entry, so market supply shifts right.

(c) The increase in supply lowers market price. The firm's horizontal P = MR line falls with the market price.

(d) Entry continues until P = MR = MC = min ATC. At that point firms earn zero economic profit.

(e) Zero economic profit means the firm covers all explicit and implicit opportunity costs, including normal profit. It does not mean the owner earns nothing.

(f) A tariff raises the cost of imports and reduces foreign competition. If foreign producers would have entered and expanded supply, the tariff blocks entry pressure and can let domestic firms keep economic profit longer while consumers pay higher prices.

Reveal the scoring rubric ▸
  • 1 pt Market and firm graphs correctly labeled.
  • 1 pt Firm graph shows P > ATC at Q*.
  • 1 pt States entry occurs because of economic profit.
  • 1 pt Shows market supply shifting right and price falling.
  • 1 pt Shows the firm's price/MR line falling.
  • 1 pt States P = MR = MC = min ATC.
  • 1 pt Connects long-run equilibrium to zero economic profit.
  • 1 pt Explains normal profit / opportunity cost.
  • 1 pt Explains tariff as a barrier to entry by foreign competitors.
  • 1 pt Connects blocked entry to persistent profit or higher consumer prices.
Reveal what loses points ▸
  • Graph Forgetting to shift market supply and only moving the firm's graph.
  • Concept Saying zero economic profit means the firm earns no money.
  • Policy Calling every rule bad. The better test is whether the rule protects consumers or mainly protects incumbents.
Market thesis

Competition is the consumer's ally: free entry drives profit toward zero and hands the gains to buyers. Profit is the flare that rewards discovery and summons rivals; entry is the mechanism that spreads value.

If economists ruled the world...

we would make markets easier to enter and let competition solve the profit problem. If entry is open, let profit call competitors in. If entry is blocked, break the blockage.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Economic profit attracts entry; economic losses cause exit.
  • Long-run perfect competition ends at P = MR = MC = min ATC.
  • Free entry pushes firms toward productive and allocative efficiency.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Which barriers protect consumers, and which mainly protect incumbents?
  • When should policy defend temporary profit as a reward for discovery, and when should it attack protected profit?
Takeaways
In your life

When everyone runs away, run toward. Not toward danger for its own sake, but toward real needs others avoid because they are difficult, unglamorous, or uncomfortable.

In your work

Build the habit, not the hit. Do not expect one profitable position to stay untouched. Keep learning, improving, and finding the next need to fill.
