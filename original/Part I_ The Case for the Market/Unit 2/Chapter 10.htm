Unit 2 · The Logic of Competition

Firm Model II: Production Decisions

The firm as problem-solver

Chapter 10 ~ 31 min read

Picture the most essential business you can. Billions of people fly every year; whole cities and supply chains depend on it; the planes are marvels and the brands are household names. By any measure of size, reach, or sheer usefulness, the airline industry is a titan. And across its entire history, the money it has returned to the people who funded it is, roughly, nothing — a bottomless pit that has swallowed capital since the Wright brothers. Warren Buffett put it best: a farsighted capitalist standing at Kitty Hawk in 1903 would have done future investors an enormous favor by shooting Orville down.

How can something so big, so busy, so plainly needed be a money-loser? Because big is not the same as profitable, and busy is not the same as winning. The airlines fill every seat and still hand their owners less than a savings account would. And the lesson shrinks to fit a single storefront: the café owner who clears $50,000 after a year of seventy-hour weeks looks successful — until you learn she walked away from a $60,000 salary to do it. The till says she made money. Did she?

So what is the goal, if it isn't to be the biggest, or the busiest, or even to "turn a profit" in the ordinary sense? How can a business that books a profit still be a mistake — and how does a clear-eyed firm actually decide what, and how much, to make?

The big picture
There are rules to the game of business, and the market rewards the players who follow them. Chapter 9 gave you the first rule — costs matter: a firm that ignores them loses to one that doesn't. Ch 9 Here are the next two. One is how much to make : produce every unit whose extra revenue covers its extra cost, and stop there — at MR = MC. The other is how to keep score : profit isn't the cash in the till, it's what you earn above what your money, time, and effort could have made anywhere else. Get both right and you can be smaller than your rivals, spend more than the cheapskate down the street, and still win. By the end you should be able to compute accounting and economic profit, find the profit-maximizing quantity with MR = MC, and explain how a firm can create real value without making a single physical thing.
Rules of business success · Rules #2 and #3

Rule #2: Profit means economic profit. A business is not truly profitable just because money is left over after paying explicit bills. It earns economic profit only when revenue exceeds both explicit costs and implicit opportunity costs.

Rule #3: Decide at the margin. A profit-seeking firm should keep producing as long as the next unit adds more revenue than cost. In short: produce where marginal revenue is at least marginal cost; in smooth models, this is summarized as MR = MC.

Major concept 1

Economic and Accounting Profit

The books and the economist can look at the same year and disagree about whether you won.

Profit is the simplest idea in business and one of the most misread. It is what's left of revenue after costs: Profit = TR − TC The trouble is the second term. Which costs? Chapter 9 already split them for us — explicit costs paid in money, and implicit costs paid in opportunities given up. Ch 9 Count only the money that left the till and you get accounting profit , the number on the tax return. Subtract the opportunities you gave up as well, and you get economic profit — the honest one, the one that asks not "did money come in?" but "did this beat what I could have done instead?"

Return to the café owner. Her year looks like this.

Fig 10.1 $120k revenue, less $70k in explicit costs, leaves a $50k accounting profit — then the $60k salary she gave up drags the economic result to −$10k.
What to notice: the same year reads as a $50k success on the books and a $10k loss to an economist. Both are "right" — they're answering different questions. Only the second asks whether the café beat her next-best alternative.

That gap is the whole point of the hook. The airlines and the café share a diagnosis: an accounting profit that quietly hides an economic loss, because the capital (or the owner's own labor) could have earned more elsewhere. And it gives us the most useful benchmark in the chapter: an economic profit of exactly zero, which we call normal profit . Zero economic profit is not failure — it means you're doing exactly as well as your next-best option, no better, no worse. Anything above that is a genuine reason to keep doing this rather than something else. (Hold onto normal profit; in Chapter 13 it becomes the resting point that competition pushes every firm toward.) Worked: accounting profit vs. economic profit ▸
Worked example Did the café actually pay off?

Revenue $120,000. Explicit costs (rent, supplies, an employee): $70,000. To run it, the owner gave up a $60,000 salary.

  1. Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs = 120,000 − 70,000 = $50,000 . The books look great.
  2. Implicit cost = the foregone $60,000 salary — a real cost, though no check is ever written for it.
  3. Economic profit = accounting profit − implicit cost = 50,000 − 60,000 = −$10,000 .
Accounting profit +$50,000; economic profit −$10,000. The café "made money" and still cost her $10,000 against simply taking the salary — and that's before counting the interest her savings could have earned elsewhere, which only deepens the loss. The economic number is the one that answers "was it worth it?"
Quick check A consultant earns $90,000 in revenue with $20,000 of explicit costs. She left a job paying $80,000. Accounting profit? Economic profit? ▸
Accounting profit = 90,000 − 20,000 = $70,000. Economic profit = 70,000 − 80,000 (foregone salary) = −$10,000 . Profitable on paper, an economic loss in fact — she'd be richer back at the old job.
Quick check A firm is earning exactly zero economic profit. Is it failing? What is this called? ▸
No — it's earning normal profit . Zero economic profit means it's doing exactly as well as its next-best alternative: covering every cost, including opportunity cost. Perfectly fine; just no better than the alternative.
Retrieval practice Without looking, state how accounting and economic profit differ, and why a firm can have one positive and the other negative. ▸

Accounting profit subtracts only explicit (money) costs; economic profit subtracts implicit (opportunity) costs as well. A firm shows positive accounting profit but negative economic profit whenever the owner's foregone alternatives — a salary, the return on tied-up capital — exceed what's left after the money costs. The books say "money came in"; the economist says "less came in than you'd have earned elsewhere." The common slip is to forget that the owner's own time and capital have a price even when no check is written for them.

Major concept 2

The Output Rule: MR = MC

Not the most, not the cheapest — the quantity that opens the widest gap.

Now the firm's actual decision: how much to make. Two tempting answers are both wrong. Minimize cost? The cheapest possible operation makes nothing — zero cost, zero business. Maximize output? Chapter 9 showed that the last units cost a fortune as marginal cost climbs; pile on production and you spend yourself broke. Ch 9 The goal is neither total: it's to maximize the gap between revenue and cost. And there's a clean rule for finding the top of that gap.

Start with revenue. Total revenue is TR = P × Q The extra revenue from one more unit is marginal revenue . We'll make one simplifying assumption this chapter: the firm is a price taker — small enough that selling one more unit doesn't budge the market price. Sell the tenth unit or the ten-thousandth, each fetches the same going price. So for a price taker, MR = P : every extra unit brings in exactly the price.

Lay marginal revenue against the rising marginal cost from Chapter 9 and the rule writes itself. As long as the next unit brings in more than it costs (MR > MC), making it widens the gap — do it. The moment a unit would cost more than it brings in (MC > MR), it shrinks the gap — stop. The profit-maximizing quantity is the last one where revenue still covers cost: MR = MC , which for a price taker is P = MC . Notice the chapter's whole personality in that line: you decide at the margin , on the next unit, never on the average or the total.

Worked: find the quantity and the profit ▸
Worked example MR = MC, then the profit rectangle

Price taker at P = $50, using the schedule above (fixed cost $36).

  1. Compare P = MR = $50 to MC at each unit: $20, $24, $30, $40, $50, $64. Every unit through the 5th clears the price; the 6th (MC $64) does not.
  2. Profit-maximizing quantity: Q* = 5 , where P = MC = $50.
  3. Total revenue = P × Q = 50 × 5 = $250. Total cost at Q = 5 = $200.
  4. Profit = TR − TC = 250 − 200 = $50 . (Check via the rectangle: ATC at Q = 5 is 200 ÷ 5 = $40, so profit = (50 − 40) × 5 = $50.) ✓
Produce 5 units, earn $50 economic profit. Stopping earlier leaves gap on the table; going to 6 makes a unit that costs $64 to earn $50 — shrinking the gap by $14.
Misconception "To make more money, cut your costs to the bone — or just get bigger." ▸

Both instincts optimize the wrong thing. Cutting cost to the minimum drives you toward producing nothing — the lowest-cost firm is the one that's closed. Chasing size pushes you up the MC curve past the point where units still pay for themselves, burning the gap you'd already built. Neither cost nor size is the target; the gap is, and MR = MC is its peak.

This is exactly the airline trap from the hook: an industry that maximized scale — more routes, more planes, more passengers — while the marginal flight barely cleared its cost, leaving almost no gap for investors. Big and busy, optimizing everything except the one quantity that matters.

One quiet but important point ties this back to Concept 1. The cost curves we're using already include opportunity cost — they're economic costs, the way Chapter 9 built them. Ch 9 So the profit in that rectangle is economic profit: a positive rectangle means the firm is beating its next-best alternative, not merely showing black ink. And here is the thread the next chapters pull: that the firm lands exactly where price equals marginal cost is the hinge of everything to come — why that point is good for the whole market is Chapter 11's marginal buyer, and what happens to this tidy profit rectangle once rivals are free to enter is Chapter 13. Ch 11 Ch 13 For now: decide at the margin, and keep score in economic profit.

Quick check A price taker sells at $30. The next unit would cost $26 to make. Should it produce that unit? What if the next costs $34? ▸
Yes to the $26 unit (MR $30 > MC $26 — it widens the gap). No to the $34 unit (MC > MR — it shrinks the gap). Stop where MC reaches $30.
Quick check Why doesn't the firm produce at the quantity where average total cost is lowest? ▸
Because it cares about the gap , not the lowest per-unit cost. Past the bottom of ATC, units cost more than the average but can still cost less than the price — each one still adds profit. It keeps going until MC reaches P.
Quick check For a price taker, why is marginal revenue equal to the price? ▸
It's too small to move the market price, so every unit — including the next one — sells at the same going price. The extra revenue from one more unit is therefore just that price: MR = P.
Retrieval practice State the profit-maximization rule and explain, from the gap idea, why it lands there and not at maximum output. ▸

Produce until MR = MC (for a price taker, P = MC). The reason: every unit with MR > MC widens the revenue-minus-cost gap, so you make it; every unit with MC > MR shrinks the gap, so you don't. The top of the gap is exactly where the two are equal. Maximum output overshoots — it keeps making units whose rising marginal cost has passed the price, each one subtracting from profit. The classic error is to chase the largest quantity or the lowest average cost; the rule is a statement about the margin , not the total or the average.

Major concept 3

Value Without a Product

"Cut out the middleman" assumes the middleman did nothing. Usually the opposite is true.

We've taught a firm to choose output and grade itself. Now the surprising case — the firms that follow the very same rules while seeming to break the spirit of them. The wholesaler, the broker, the ride app, the reseller: they "make nothing," and the popular verdict is that they're parasites skimming a cut between the people who do the real work. Cut out the middleman is one of the most common pieces of business advice there is. The economist's reply is that the middleman is often the one creating the most value in the chain — you just can't see it, because what they produce isn't a thing.

The clearest account comes from the Duke economist Michael Munger, who argues that firms like Uber don't really sell rides — they sell reductions in transaction costs. A transaction cost is everything that stands between you and a deal, beyond the price itself, and Munger sorts it into three: triangulation (finding the right counterparty and agreeing a price), transfer (actually paying and delivering), and trust (believing the other side will come through and not rob you). Before Uber, a stranger's car was useless to you — you couldn't find it, couldn't price it, couldn't trust the driver. The app didn't build a car; it manufactured triangulation, transfer, and trust, and that is a real product. To a customer, a transaction cost is as real as a price: cut the hassle of a deal by a tenth and you've done as much good as cutting its price by a tenth.

This sits on a deeper idea, from Ronald Coase: the reason firms and middlemen exist at all is that using markets is itself costly, and someone who lowers that cost has created genuine value. So the intermediary is a firm like any other — running MR = MC on a service (matching, moving, timing, vouching), earning economic profit on a genuinely scarce kind of coordination. "Makes nothing" was always the wrong test. The right test is whether a real cost fell because they showed up.

Which gives us the honest line, and the one to carry forward. Picture two ways to make money on a river. One person dredges the channel so barges pass faster and cheaper — a real cost falls, value is created, and the fee they charge is earned. Another simply stretches a chain across the water and charges boats to lower it — nothing moves faster, no cost falls; they've added an obstacle and rented the key. Same fee collected; opposite economics. The first creates value by reducing a transaction cost; the second merely extracts it, capturing a payment for a problem they invented — the seed of what we'll later call rent-seeking . Unit 3 The test for any middleman, any platform's cut, any toll, is exactly this: did a real cost fall because you're here, or did you just hang a chain across the river? (Whether the buyer comes out ahead too — the other half of the value story — is Chapter 11.) Ch 11

Quick check A wholesaler buys from 200 farms and sells to 50 groceries, "making nothing." In transaction-cost terms, what is the wholesaler actually producing? ▸
Triangulation and transfer at scale: instead of 200 × 50 separate searches, negotiations, and deliveries, each side deals once with the wholesaler. The real cost that falls is all the matching and moving — that reduction is the product.
Quick check Apply the river test: a ticket platform that instantly matches fans to verified resale seats vs. a scalper who buys up a show and resells at a markup. Coordinator or toll-collector? ▸
It depends on whether a real cost fell. Matching fans to verified seats lowers triangulation and trust costs — coordination. Buying up supply only to resell adds no matching or trust; if it's just cornering tickets to charge more, that's closer to the chain across the river. (Reasonable people argue the line — which is the point.)
Retrieval practice Name Munger's three transaction costs a middleman can reduce, and give the one-line test for whether a middleman creates value or extracts it. ▸

Triangulation (finding and pricing a counterparty), transfer (paying and delivering), and trust (believing the deal will hold). The test: did a real cost fall because the middleman is there? If yes, they created value (dredged the channel); if they only collect a fee on a deal that's no easier than before, they're extracting it (a chain across the river). The common error is to treat "produces no physical good" as "produces no value" — coordination is production.

On the exam

The moves you can now make

Three questions at exam level — score the venture, find the quantity, and explain the rule. Try each on paper first, then reveal. The trying is where the learning happens.

Type A · accounting vs. economic profit

A web designer brings in $110,000 a year. She pays $25,000 in software, rent, and contractors. To do this she left a salaried job paying $78,000 and stopped renting out a spare room that earned $6,000. Find her accounting profit and her economic profit, and say whether she should keep going.

Show worked solution ▸
  1. Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs = 110,000 − 25,000 = $85,000 .
  2. Implicit costs = foregone salary + foregone rent = 78,000 + 6,000 = $84,000.
  3. Economic profit = 85,000 − 84,000 = +$1,000 .
Economic profit is positive (just barely), so yes — keep going. She's beating her next-best alternative by $1,000. Note how close it is: the venture looks like an $85k winner on the books but only clears its true opportunity cost by $1,000.

Type B · MR = MC and profit

A price-taking farm sells wheat at $8 a bushel. Its marginal cost for each additional bushel is, in order: $3, $4, $6, $8, $11. Total cost of producing 4 bushels is $27. Find the profit-maximizing quantity and the firm's profit at that quantity.

Show worked solution ▸
  1. P = MR = $8. Compare to MC: $3, $4, $6, $8, $11. Units 1–4 each clear $8 (the 4th exactly); the 5th (MC $11) does not.
  2. Profit-maximizing quantity: Q* = 4 , where P = MC = $8.
  3. TR = 8 × 4 = $32. TC at 4 bushels = $27.
  4. Profit = 32 − 27 = $5 .
Produce 4 bushels for $5 profit. The 4th bushel (MC = P = $8) is the break-even-at-the-margin unit you still make; the 5th would cost $11 to earn $8, so it's left ungrown.

Type C · explain

A student claims, "A profit-maximizing firm should produce at the quantity where average total cost is lowest, to be as efficient as possible." Explain why this is wrong, and state where the firm should produce instead.

Show model answer ▸

The firm doesn't maximize efficiency-per-unit; it maximizes the gap between total revenue and total cost. At the minimum of ATC, marginal cost is still below the price, so additional units — though they raise average cost above its minimum — each still bring in more than they cost, widening the gap. Refusing to make them leaves profit unclaimed.

The firm should produce where MR = MC (for a price taker, where P = MC), which lies to the right of minimum ATC. It willingly accepts a higher average cost per unit in exchange for a larger total profit. Lowest average cost and highest profit are simply different targets.

  2. Using the MR = MC logic (for whole units, produce while MC is no greater than MR), determine the profit-maximizing quantity. Show your reasoning. (2 pts)

Reveal a full-credit response ▸

(a) MR = $22 . The studio is a price taker — too small to move the market price — so each additional vase sells at the same $22, making the extra revenue per vase equal to the price.

(b) Marginal cost per vase (ΔTC, starting from the $20 fixed cost at Q = 0) is $18, $14, $12, $16, $20, $28. Produce each whole vase while MC ≤ P = $22: the 5th vase (MC $20) clears it, the 6th (MC $28) does not. Q* = 5.

(c) TR = 22 × 5 = $110. TC at 5 = $100. Accounting profit = 110 − 100 = $10 .

(d) The $15 foregone job is an implicit cost. Economic profit = accounting profit − implicit cost = 10 − 15 = −$5 . The studio is not economically worth running — the owner would be $5 better off taking the job, despite the books showing a $10 profit.

Model graph MC is below the $22 price line at the 5th vase and above it at the 6th, so the best whole-number output is Q = 5. ATC at Q = 5 is $20, so the books show profit — but the graph can't see the $15 implicit cost that makes economic profit negative.
Reveal the scoring rubric ▸
  • 1 pt (a) MR = $22 AND because the firm is a price taker (each unit sells at the market price).
  • 1 pt (b) Computes MC per unit from the schedule (ΔTC).
  • 1 pt (b) Identifies Q* = 5 as the last unit with MC ≤ P.
  • 1 pt (c) TR = $110 (22 × 5) shown.
  • 1 pt (c) Accounting profit = $10 (110 − 100).
  • 1 pt (d) Subtracts the $15 implicit cost: economic profit = −$5.
  • 1 pt (d) States the studio is not economically worth running (would do better at the job).
  • 1 pt (e) Labeled graph: axes, a horizontal P = MR line, an MC curve that rises through price after Q = 5, and Q* = 5 marked as the last whole unit before MC exceeds price.

Eight independent points. A wrong Q* in (b) doesn't sink (c) if the revenue and cost arithmetic is right for the quantity chosen.

Reveal what loses points ▸
  • Conceptual Stopping at the lowest-ATC quantity instead of MR = MC, or forgetting the implicit cost in (d) and calling the studio profitable — the whole point of the question.
  • Precision Reporting Q* or profit with no work — AP "Calculate" and "Determine" prompts require the steps (MC schedule, TR, TC), not just a number.

Market thesis

There are rules to the game, and the market rewards those who follow them: make every unit whose extra revenue beats its extra cost, and judge the venture by economic profit — what you cleared above your next-best alternative — not by the cash in the till.

If economists ruled the world…

we'd stop calling middlemen parasites. The wholesaler, the rideshare app, the reseller "make nothing" — and create real value by matching buyers to sellers, moving goods, and manufacturing the trust a deal needs. We'd judge them by whether they earn economic profit on a genuinely scarce service, not by whether they make a physical thing.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • A firm maximizes profit at MR = MC — not at maximum output, and not at minimum cost.
  • Economic profit subtracts opportunity cost, so a firm can show an accounting profit and an economic loss at once.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • When is an intermediary a coordinator earning its keep, and when a gatekeeper charging a toll — the reseller, the platform's cut, the licensing board?
  • If a venture only "profits" by never counting the owner's foregone wage, who decides whether it should exist?
Takeaways
In your life

The real cost of a path is the best one you passed up. Grade your choices against your next-best alternative, not against zero.

In your work

Don't ask "should we do this?" — ask "is the next unit worth its cost?" Produce, hire, and invest up to MR = MC, never to the max.
