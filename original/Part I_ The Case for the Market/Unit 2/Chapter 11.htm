Profit Maximization I: The Short Run

The Scoreboard

Chapter 11 ~ 44 min read

Businesses love visible signs of success: full seats, long lines, famous brands, packed schedules, rising revenue, and constant motion. Markets are colder than that. They do not grade firms on how impressive they look from the outside. They grade firms on whether the value created exceeds the full opportunity cost of creating it.

Think about airlines. The industry is huge, technically miraculous, socially essential, and never still: planes in the air, terminals packed, routes across continents, status tiers, mergers, billion-dollar fleets. From the outside, it looks like bigness itself. And yet airlines have often been a brutal business for investors. They can fill planes, move millions of people, and still struggle to earn sustained economic profit because fuel, labor, aircraft, gates, maintenance, delays, and competition eat the gap.

So what should the firm maximize? Not output. Not revenue. Not market share. Not being busy. Not applause. Not even accounting profit. The firm should maximize economic profit : the gap between what consumers are willing to pay and the full opportunity cost of providing it.

The big picture
Profit is the scoreboard, not the game. In free markets, the primary ways to get rich are to provide value or cheat the system. This chapter celebrates the first and names the second. In the competitive-firm model, profit and loss are short-run signals: profit says resources are creating more value here than elsewhere; loss says the strategy may be failing. The AP task is to read those signals with precision: take price as given, choose Q* where MR = MC, compare price with ATC for profit or loss, and compare price with AVC for shutdown.

Major concept 1

Profit Is the Scoreboard, Not the Game

The right question is not "profit or no profit?" It is "how was the profit earned?"

Chapter 10 described the firm as a machine for solving consumer problems. Chapter 11 adds the short-run test: did that machine create value greater than the full opportunity cost of the resources it used? That is what economic profit measures.

Profit = TR - TC

Because this is economic profit, total cost includes explicit and implicit costs. A firm that earns positive economic profit has done more than pay its bills. It has created enough value to beat the next-best use of its resources. A firm that earns a loss has not.

But do not turn that into a moral shortcut. Profit can come from value-production : better food, faster delivery, cleaner software, safer payment, a service people wanted and could not easily get. Profit can also come from cheating the system : fraud, coercion, political favoritism, barriers to entry, deception, or rules written to protect insiders. A healthy market order should defend the first and attack the second.

Rules of business success · Rule #3: maximize the right thing
Position strategically, then maximize economic profit rather than vanity metrics. Know what problem you solve, who needs it, and why you can provide it better than the alternatives. Then do not confuse profit with size, popularity, revenue, output, or busyness.
Synthesis Two ways profit can happen
  • Value-producing profit: consumers willingly pay more than the opportunity cost of the resources used.
  • System-cheating profit: the firm gains through deception, coercion, barriers, favoritism, or rent-seeking.
  • The economist's test: did the firm create value, or did it block, deceive, extract, or manipulate?
Quick check A firm earns high profit after inventing a cheaper medical device. Another earns high profit because a licensing board blocks rivals. Which is value-production and which is rent-seeking? ▸
The cheaper medical device is value-production if it genuinely lowers cost or improves care. Blocking rivals through a protected licensing rule is rent-seeking if the barrier protects insiders rather than consumers.
Retrieval practice Explain why economists treat profit as meaningful without treating every profit as morally innocent. ▸
Profit can signal that consumers value a resource use more than its opportunity cost. But the signal is trustworthy only when competition, honest information, property rights, and open entry are working. Profit from value creation deserves defense; profit from cheating or protected barriers deserves attack.
Major concept 2

The Competitive Firm Is a Price Taker

The market sets the price. The firm chooses quantity.

A perfectly competitive firm is tiny relative to the market and sells a product buyers see as identical to everyone else's. One wheat farmer, one shrimp boat, one seller of a standardized commodity cannot raise price just because it wants more profit. If the market price is $8 and the firm asks $9, buyers walk away. If the firm sells at $8, it can sell all it wants at that price.

That is why the individual firm's demand curve is horizontal. The firm does not face the whole market demand curve. The market's demand and supply determine price; the individual firm is a price taker .

For a competitive firm:

P = MR = AR

Price equals marginal revenue because each extra unit sells for the same market price. Price equals average revenue because total revenue divided by quantity is just the price per unit.

Fig 11.1 The market sets price with demand and supply. The individual competitive firm then faces a horizontal demand curve at that price.
AP warning: do not draw the individual competitive firm's demand curve downward sloping. In perfect competition, the firm is a price taker.
Quick check If a competitive firm sells each unit for $14, what are MR and AR? ▸
MR = $14 and AR = $14. Each added unit sells for the same price, and total revenue divided by quantity also equals price.
Retrieval practice Explain the difference between the market graph and the competitive firm's graph. ▸
The market graph has downward-sloping demand and upward-sloping supply, which determine the market price. The individual firm takes that price as given, so its demand, MR, and AR are horizontal at the market price.
Major concept 3

MR = MC: The Output Rule

Make the units that widen the gap. Stop before the units that shrink it.

The competitive firm cannot choose price, but it can choose quantity. The rule is marginal: compare the next unit's added revenue with the next unit's added cost.

If MR > MC , the next unit adds more revenue than cost. Producing it increases profit. If MC > MR the next unit adds more cost than revenue. Producing it reduces profit. The profit-maximizing quantity is where the firm stops leaving profitable units unmade and stops making unprofitable units.

General rule: MR = MC

Competitive firm rule: P = MC

Worked: find Q* from the schedule ▸
  1. Because the firm is competitive, P = MR = $20.
  2. Produce units as long as MR is at least MC: units 1, 2, 3, and 4 pass.
  3. The 5th unit has MC = $26, above MR = $20, so it would shrink profit.
  4. At Q = 4, profit = TR - TC = 80 - 64 = $16 .
The profit-maximizing output is Q* = 4. The firm is not maximizing revenue or output; it is maximizing profit.
Fig 11.2 The competitive firm chooses Q* where the horizontal P = MR = AR line meets MC.
AP warning: Q* is not automatically the minimum of ATC. Efficient scale and profit-maximizing output are different ideas.
Quick check If P = $30 and the MC values are $12, $18, $25, $31, and $40, what quantity should the firm produce? ▸
Three units. The first three have MC less than price; the fourth costs $31 and brings in only $30, so it would reduce profit.
Retrieval practice Explain why a firm should not maximize output, revenue, or market share. ▸
Output, revenue, and market share can rise even when profit falls. A firm should produce units that add more revenue than cost and stop before units that cost more than they bring in. The goal is the largest gap between total revenue and total cost.
Major concept 4

Profit, Loss, and the Rectangle

After choosing Q*, compare price with ATC. That tells you whether the scoreboard is positive, negative, or zero.

The graph has two separate jobs. First, use MR = MC to find Q*. Second, at Q*, compare price with average total cost . Price is revenue per unit. ATC is cost per unit. Their difference is profit or loss per unit.

Profit per unit = P - ATC

Total profit = (P - ATC) x Q

If price is below ATC, reverse it:

Loss per unit = ATC - P

Total loss = (ATC - P) x Q

Fig 11.3 Profit: at Q*, price is above ATC. Total profit is (P - ATC) x Q.
Worked example Read the profit rectangle

At Q* = 400, price is $18 and ATC is $14.

  1. Profit per unit = P - ATC = 18 - 14 = $4.
  2. Total profit = (P - ATC) x Q = 4 x 400 = $1,600 .
Use ATC for profit. AVC is not the profit line.
Fig 11.4 Three scoreboard outcomes: loss but producing, break-even, and shutdown. The AP trick is knowing which curve answers which question.
Quick check At Q* = 200, P = $9 and ATC = $12. Calculate the loss. ▸
Loss per unit = ATC - P = 12 - 9 = $3. Total loss = 3 x 200 = $600.
Retrieval practice How do you tell profit, loss, and break-even from the graph? ▸
First find Q* where MR = MC. Then read ATC at Q*. If P > ATC, the firm earns profit. If P < ATC, the firm has a loss. If P = ATC, the firm earns zero economic profit, also called normal profit.
Major concept 5

Losses and the Shutdown Rule

A loss is bad news. It is not always a reason to close tonight.

One of the easiest mistakes in this chapter is to say, "The firm has losses, so it should shut down." Not in the short run . The short run is the period when some costs are fixed. If the restaurant owes tonight's rent whether it opens or not, that fixed cost does not decide whether opening the doors makes the loss better or worse.

The operating decision compares price with average variable cost . If price covers variable cost, producing helps pay something toward fixed cost. The firm may still lose money, but shutting down would lose even more. If price does not cover variable cost, every unit makes the loss worse, so the firm shuts down.

Produce in the short run if: P >= AVC

Shut down in the short run if: P < AVC

That is why the competitive firm's short-run supply curve is the marginal cost curve above the minimum of AVC. Below that point, the firm does not supply output in the short run.

Worked example The restaurant tonight

A restaurant owes $1,000 in rent tonight no matter what. If it opens, food and wages cost $700. Expected dinner revenue is $850.

  1. If it closes, it loses the fixed rent: -$1,000 .
  2. If it opens, it earns $850 but pays $700 in variable cost and $1,000 in fixed cost: 850 - 700 - 1,000 = -$850 .
  3. Opening still loses money, but it loses $150 less than closing.
The firm should produce in the short run because price/revenue covers variable cost. This is not sustainable forever; Chapter 12 handles exit.
Fig 11.5 The competitive firm's short-run supply curve is the MC curve above the minimum of AVC. Below AVC, the firm shuts down.
AP warning: ATC tells profit or loss. AVC tells whether to shut down.
Quick check At Q*, P = $10, ATC = $14, and AVC = $8. Profit, loss, or shutdown? ▸
Loss but producing. Since P < ATC, the firm has a loss. Since P > AVC, it should keep producing in the short run.
Retrieval practice Why can a firm rationally produce at a loss in the short run? ▸
Because fixed costs are already unavoidable in the short run. If price covers average variable cost, production contributes something toward fixed cost and reduces the loss. The firm shuts down only when price is below AVC, because then producing makes the loss worse.
Practice — make the graph automatic

The practice priority is simple: draw and interpret the perfectly competitive firm graph until profit, loss, break-even, and shutdown feel automatic. The sequence is always the same: draw P = MR = AR, find Q* at MR = MC, read ATC for profit or loss, and read AVC for shutdown.

On the exam

The moves you can now make

Three AP-style moves: choose output, calculate profit or loss, and apply the shutdown rule. Try each before revealing.

Type A · schedule

A competitive firm sells each unit for $25. Its marginal costs for units 1-6 are $10, $16, $22, $25, $29, and $36. What quantity maximizes profit?

Show worked solution ▸

Because the firm is competitive, P = MR = $25. Produce each unit whose MC is less than or equal to $25. Units 1, 2, 3, and 4 pass. Unit 5 has MC = $29, so it would lower profit. Q* = 4 units .

Type B · calculation from graph values

At Q* = 800, the market price is $11 and ATC is $8. Calculate the firm's economic profit.

Show worked solution ▸

Profit per unit = P - ATC = 11 - 8 = $3. Total profit = 3 x 800 = $2,400 . Use ATC, not AVC, for the profit rectangle.

Type C · shutdown decision

At Q*, P = $7, ATC = $10, and AVC = $6. Should the firm produce in the short run, shut down, or exit the industry?

Show worked solution ▸

The firm has a loss because P < ATC. But it should produce in the short run because P > AVC. Exit is a long-run decision; shutdown is the short-run decision. Here, producing reduces the loss compared with shutting down.

Capstone · free response

Put the moves together

AP-style long free response · ~10 points · graph and calculations required

A perfectly competitive firm sells its product for $18. At the profit-maximizing output of 500 units, ATC is $15 and AVC is $11.

  1. Draw a correctly labeled firm graph showing P = MR = AR, MC, ATC, AVC, and Q*. (3 pt)
  2. Calculate the firm's total revenue. (1 pt)
  3. Calculate the firm's economic profit or loss. (2 pt)
  4. State whether the firm should produce or shut down in the short run. (1 pt)
  5. Explain why the firm is not simply maximizing revenue or output. (1 pt)
  6. Explain why positive profit can be a market signal but is not automatically proof of moral innocence. (2 pt)
Reveal a full-credit response ▸

(a) The graph should show dollars per unit on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, a horizontal P = MR = AR line at $18, MC crossing that line at Q* = 500, U-shaped ATC and AVC curves, and ATC below price at Q*.

(b) TR = P x Q = 18 x 500 = $9,000 .

(c) Profit per unit = P - ATC = 18 - 15 = $3. Total profit = 3 x 500 = $1,500 .

(d) The firm should produce. Price is above AVC, and in fact it is also earning positive economic profit.

(e) The firm maximizes profit by comparing marginal revenue and marginal cost. More output or revenue can reduce profit if extra units cost more than they bring in.

(f) In a competitive market, profit can signal that resources are creating value consumers want. But profit can also come from cheating, deception, barriers, favoritism, or rent-seeking. The policy question is how the profit was earned.

Reveal the scoring rubric ▸
  • 1 pt Correct axes and labels on the firm graph.
  • 1 pt Horizontal P = MR = AR line and Q* where MR = MC.
  • 1 pt ATC and AVC correctly placed, with ATC below price at Q*.
  • 1 pt TR = $9,000.
  • 1 pt Profit per unit = $3.
  • 1 pt Total profit = $1,500.
  • 1 pt Correctly says produce in the short run because P > AVC.
  • 1 pt Explains MR = MC logic rather than revenue/output maximization.
  • 1 pt Explains profit as a possible signal of value creation.
  • 1 pt Names why profit can also be distorted by cheating, barriers, or rent-seeking.
Reveal what loses points ▸
  • Graph Drawing the firm's demand curve downward sloping in perfect competition.
  • Calculation Using AVC instead of ATC to calculate profit or loss.
  • Concept Saying the firm shuts down whenever it has losses. Shutdown depends on P and AVC.
Market thesis

In free markets, the primary ways to get rich are to provide value or cheat the system. The competitive-firm model treats profit and loss as signals, but the signal deserves trust only when competition, honest information, property rights, and open entry are doing their jobs.

If economists ruled the world...

we would stop treating competitive profits as suspicious by default. We would celebrate and defend profit from value-production, while attacking cheating, fraud, political favoritism, rent-seeking, and barriers to entry.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • A competitive firm takes price as given, so P = MR = AR.
  • The firm chooses output where MR = MC, then compares P to ATC for profit or loss.
  • In the short run, a firm shuts down only if P is below AVC.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Which profits came from value creation, and which came from cheating, barriers, favoritism, or rent-seeking?
  • How do we protect the profit signal without protecting deception or insider privilege?
Takeaways
In your life

Do not confuse motion with progress. Being busy, visible, praised, or exhausted is not the same as doing valuable work.

In your work

Ask what problem you solve, for whom, and why you are best positioned to solve it profitably. Then optimize for that, not the vanity metric that feels good this week.
