Factor Markets II and Unit Capstone: Labor Markets

Work, wages, and dignity

Chapter 14 ~ 43 min read

Who pays the highest marginal tax rate in America?

One of the key ideas of "supply-side" neoliberal thinkers is that taxes harm economic growth. Economists recognize that this is only true on the margin: no one cares about how much total money will be taxed. Ronald Reagan once recalled that he was tempted to make a third movie in a single year, but that's when the tax rate would jump to near 90%. He said, " Why should I have done a third picture, even if it was Gone with the Wind ? What good would it have done me? "

The basic logic of marginal taxation is just this simple: people respond to the incentives to work and not work, and the higher the marginal tax, the less they will respond by working.

So the question naturally presents itself: which group of Americans suffer the negative incentive effects of marginal tax rates the most?

The answer: the very poor.

The well-intended welfare scheme operates as a diffuse network of government programs that require permissions and interviews and social worker sign offs and work requirements. One of the biggest problems, however, is the welfare cliff.

Imagine you are very, very poor. You get a few welfare benefits, but it's not enough for a decent living. You decide to go out and work. You make $100. Then you get an update from the various programs, do some tabulating, use your spreadsheet, and realize that your $100 cost you $400.

Labor markets are subject to the same rules as any other markets: supply curves slope up; when people are paid at higher rates, they will work more. People think on the margin; marginal taxes affect billionaires and the poor the same. And, most important of all, people respond to incentives.

Now, an important note: wages obey the laws of supply and demand, but that does not mean that they are an indication of fundamental worth. Why do some people work incredibly hard and earn very little, while other people earn a lot for work that may not look harder? Effort matters. Skill matters. Luck matters. Power matters. But wages do not simply line up with sweat, exhaustion, or moral deservingness.

The labor-market question is more precise: what determines the value of a worker's next hour? In the competitive model, a worker's wage is connected to the extra revenue a firm can earn by hiring one more unit of labor. That value depends on skill, effort, tools, technology, organization, demand for the output, and the worker's alternatives. It is a marginal question, not a total-person question.

A wage is a market price, not a measurement of a person's value. A liberal society treats people as equal in dignity even when markets pay different prices for different kinds of work. The model explains why wages differ. It does not tell us who matters.

The big picture
Labor is a factor of production, and workers are people. That double truth makes this chapter the capstone of the firm unit. The competitive model says firms hire workers when the worker's next hour adds at least as much revenue as it costs, and wages tend to move toward the value of the worker's marginal contribution. But real labor markets are full of search costs, mobility barriers, monopsony power, discrimination, credentials, licensing rules, bargaining, and institutions. Learn the model because it is powerful. Keep the guardrail because wages are prices for labor services, not measures of human worth.

Major concept 1

Labor Demand Is Derived Demand

A firm hires workers because customers value what those workers help produce.

A labor market is a factor market. Firms demand labor; workers supply it; the wage is the price. But demand for labor is not floating in space. It is derived demand : firms demand workers because workers help produce goods and services consumers value.

A restaurant hires cooks because people want meals. A hospital hires nurses because patients value care. A software firm hires engineers because users, advertisers, or clients value the product. If demand for the output rises, demand for the labor that helps produce it often rises too. If customers stop wanting the output, the demand for that labor can fall even if the workers are hardworking and decent.

This model tells us something about why firms hire, and what they're willing to pay. (As mentioned earlier, it does not explain virtue, intelligence, effort, kindness, courage, or full social contribution.) This question is narrow: how much extra revenue does this worker's next hour help create for this firm in this market?

AP watch: the vertical axis is the wage, not the product price. The horizontal axis is quantity of labor.

Major concept 2

Marginal Product and Marginal Revenue Product

Chapter 10 said inputs become output. Now ask the sharper question: how much value does one more worker add?

The value of an additional worker depends on two pieces. First, how much extra output does the worker help produce? That is marginal product . Second, how much revenue does the firm receive from selling that extra output? Multiply them together and you get marginal revenue product .

The formulas are the AP muscle of the chapter:

MP = change in total product / change in labor

MRP = MP x MR

In a perfectly competitive product market, MR = P , so MRP = MP x P . If each smoothie sells for $5, and the next worker adds 18 smoothies per day, that worker's MRP is $90 per day.

Calculating MP and MRP when product price is $5
Workers Total product MP Price / MR MRP
0 0 - $5 -
1 20 20 $5 $100
2 38 18 $5 $90
3 52 14 $5 $70
4 62 10 $5 $50
5 68 6 $5 $30
6 70 2 $5 $10

Notice the pattern. Total product rises as more workers are added, but marginal product falls after a while. This is diminishing marginal product . The counter gets crowded. The blender becomes the bottleneck. The manager cannot coordinate everyone perfectly. When marginal product falls, MRP falls too, so the firm's demand for labor slopes downward.

Worked example Find MP and MRP

When the third worker is hired, total product rises from 38 to 52 smoothies. Each smoothie sells for $5.

  1. MP = 52 - 38 = 14 smoothies.
  2. MRP = MP x price = 14 x $5 = $70.
  3. The third worker adds $70 of revenue per day.
AP habit: show the change in total product before multiplying by price.
Retrieval practice Using the table, calculate the MP and MRP of the fifth worker. ▸
The fifth worker raises total product from 62 to 68, so MP = 6. With price/MR = $5, MRP = 6 x $5 = $30.
Major concept 3

The Hiring Rule: Hire Until MRP = MRC

Chapter 11 gave firms MR = MC for output. For inputs, the matching rule is MRP = MRC.

A profit-maximizing firm does not hire workers because "more labor is always good." It hires as long as the next worker adds at least as much revenue as the worker costs. The added cost of hiring one more unit of a resource is marginal resource cost .

The rule is simple:

Hire if MRP > MRC.

Do not hire if MRP < MRC.

Profit-maximizing input choice: MRP = MRC , or as close as possible without hiring a worker whose cost exceeds added revenue.

In a competitive labor market, the individual firm is a wage taker. It can hire as many workers as it wants at the market wage, so MRC = wage . If the wage is $50 per day, the firm hires workers whose MRP is at least $50 and stops before hiring a worker whose MRP is below $50.

Fig 14.2 The individual competitive firm's hiring graph. The firm takes the wage as given, so MRC is horizontal. It hires where MRP equals MRC.
AP watch: do not confuse this with the market graph. The market sets the wage; the individual competitive firm takes the wage.
Hiring decision How many workers at a $50 wage?

Use the MRP column from the smoothie table: $100, $90, $70, $50, $30, $10.

  1. Workers 1, 2, and 3 all have MRP greater than the $50 wage.
  2. Worker 4 has MRP equal to the $50 wage.
  3. Worker 5 has MRP of $30, which is below the wage.
Hire 4 workers. Stop before hiring the fifth.

Major concept 4

Competitive Labor-Market Equilibrium

Wages coordinate two searches: firms looking for productive labor and workers looking for worthwhile opportunities.

In the market labor graph, wages and employment are determined by labor supply and labor demand . Labor demand shifts when output demand changes, product price changes, worker productivity changes, technology changes, or complementary capital becomes more valuable. Labor supply shifts when population, immigration, training, alternative wages, working conditions, preferences, licensing rules, credentials, or mobility costs change.

Capital and technology can cut both ways. A machine can replace some workers, reducing demand for that kind of labor. But better tools can also raise the marginal product of workers who use them, increasing demand for those workers. A nurse with better diagnostic technology, a carpenter with better tools, or a programmer with stronger software can create more value per hour.

This is the market-allocation thread. Labor markets pull workers toward jobs, firms, industries, and places where their skills create more value. The more options workers have, the better that process works. A worker with three competing employers has a stronger position than a worker with one employer and no realistic exit.

Demand shifts right when...
Output demand rises, product price rises, worker productivity rises, workers gain better complementary capital, or technology makes a skill more valuable.
Supply shifts right when...
More workers enter, immigration expands the workforce, training lowers entry costs, alternative jobs become less attractive, or barriers to entry fall.
Quick check New software makes paralegals much more productive. What happens to demand for paralegals if firms still need humans to use the software well? ▸
Labor demand can increase because the software is complementary capital. It raises paralegals' marginal product and therefore their MRP.

Major concept 5

The Competitive Wage Model Is Powerful and Incomplete

The benchmark says wages tend toward MRP. Reality asks whether competition and worker options are actually there.

The clean model is powerful because it says something precise: in competitive labor and product markets, employers cannot long pay workers more than the value they add, because the firm will lose money. They also cannot long pay workers much less than the value they add if other employers are free to bid those workers away. Competition pushes wages toward MRP.

But "toward" matters. Real labor markets can depart from the benchmark because of search costs, bargaining power, discrimination, unions, credentials, licensing, immigration restrictions, noncompete agreements, information problems, regional immobility, and employer market power. A wage can be lower than a worker's MRP if the worker has few options. A wage can be higher than MRP for a while because of institutions, contracts, or mistakes. The model is a benchmark, not a moral verdict.

There is, of course, a limitation: monopsony . A monopsonist faces an upward-sloping labor supply curve. To hire more workers, it must raise the wage, often for all workers, so its MRC curve lies above labor supply. It hires where MRP = MRC, then pays the wage from the labor supply curve at that employment level. Compared with a competitive market, monopsony usually means lower employment and lower wages.

Fig 14.3 Monopsony benchmark. The employer hires where MRP = MRC, then pays the wage workers require from the labor supply curve. This usually reduces both wages and employment compared with competition.
AP watch: in monopsony, the wage is read down from the hiring quantity to the labor supply curve, not from the MRC curve.
Model meets reality How economists analyze a wage gap

A wage gap is not solved by one slogan. Economists ask which mechanisms are operating: human capital, occupation and industry choice, hours and flexibility, caregiving constraints, discrimination, monopsony or bargaining power, workplace norms, networks, and institutions. Some mechanisms affect MRP. Some affect labor supply. Some affect bargaining conditions or access to opportunities. The point is not to settle every case in one chapter. The point is to ask better questions.

If economists ruled the world · Make it easy for people to work

Default away from occupational licensure unless the public-safety case is strong, and even then design it narrowly. Doctors, pilots, and electricians are not the same as low-risk service work. Use certification, inspections, bonding, insurance, disclosure, ratings, or targeted training when those solve the problem with less damage. Do not make it illegal for people to work unless the public case is strong.

Major concept 6

The Welfare Cliff

How welfare can harm welfare: what happens when good intentions ignore economic laws.

The safety net has a moral goal: reduce poverty, narrow inequality, and raise social welfare . The word does double duty. "Welfare" names both the programs that transfer resources and the human flourishing those programs exist to protect. Most of this chapter has treated the wage as the price that clears a labor market. Here we ask what happens when government tops up low incomes — because the way it does so runs straight into the laws of incentives we have been building all unit.

Almost every means-tested program shares one design feature: benefits shrink as earned income rises. That is reasonable, since help should flow to those who need it most. But shrinking a benefit as earnings rise is, mathematically, a tax on earning more. Economists measure it with the effective marginal tax rate : the fraction of one additional dollar of earnings that is lost to taxes and to benefits that phase out. If earning $1 more costs 40 cents in taxes and forgone benefits, the effective marginal tax rate is 40 percent, whatever the official bracket says.

Now stack several programs together — food assistance, housing, child care, health coverage. Their phase-outs pile on top of one another, and the effective marginal tax rate on a low-income worker can climb past 60, 80, even 100 percent. When it crosses 100 percent, earning more leaves a family with less. That is a welfare cliff : a threshold where a single extra dollar of earnings ends a benefit worth far more than a dollar, so total resources drop (Fig 14.4).

Fig 14.4 The welfare cliff. As earnings rise, benefits phase out, so total resources climb more slowly than earnings (the gap to the dashed line). At the cliff, crossing an eligibility limit ends a benefit outright and total resources fall — earning more has left the family worse off.
Watch: the drop is the whole point. A worker would have to earn far past the cliff just to get back to where they stood before it.

A worker facing a cliff who turns down a raise, declines extra hours, or refuses a promotion is not being lazy. They are responding rationally to the incentive the policy built, exactly as this unit's labor-supply logic predicts: people supply more labor when the net return to working rises and less when it falls. A cliff makes the net return to crossing it negative. The tragedy is that a program meant to raise well-being can trap a family below the cliff, discourage advancement, and lower the output and mobility that well-being ultimately rests on. Well-meant welfare can, through incentives alone, harm welfare.

$30,000 $9,000 $39,000
$31,000 $0 $31,000
Worked example The effective marginal tax rate at a cliff

A worker earning $30,000 keeps $9,000 in stacked benefits, for $39,000 in total resources. A $1,000 raise pushes them just over an eligibility limit and ends the entire $9,000 benefit.

  1. Extra earnings from the raise: $31,000 − $30,000 = $1,000.
  2. Benefits lost by crossing the limit: $9,000.
  3. Change in total resources: +$1,000 − $9,000 = −$8,000.
  4. Effective marginal tax rate = resources lost ÷ extra earned = $8,000 ÷ $1,000 = 800%.
The raise leaves the family $8,000 poorer. An 800% effective tax on those earnings makes turning the raise down the rational choice. The incentive is broken, not the worker.
A transfer that phases out too fast taxes effort; a transfer that rewards effort expands it. Same goal, opposite results, because incentives are not optional.

The fix is not to abandon the goal but to redesign the incentive. The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) is the clearest example of a transfer that works with incentives instead of against them, and it is among the largest anti-poverty programs in the United States. Its defining feature is that you must earn to receive it, and it is built in three stages.

In the phase-in range, the credit rises with every dollar earned: for each dollar of wages the worker keeps the wage and receives an extra share on top. That is a negative marginal tax rate on work — the government pays you to earn. Across a middle plateau the credit holds at its maximum. Then a gradual phase-out shrinks it slowly to zero. The phase-out is smooth by design: a modest tax on additional earnings, never a cliff (Fig 14.5).

Fig 14.5 The EITC obeys incentives. The credit grows as earnings rise (a subsidy to work), holds at a maximum, then tapers gently to zero. Compare Fig 14.4: there is no vertical drop anywhere, so working always leaves a household better off.

Because the phase-in raises the reward to the first hours of work, the EITC pulls people into the labor force — the extensive margin of labor supply. Research consistently finds it raised employment among low-income single mothers, especially after the expansions of the 1990s, and it lifts millions above the poverty line each year by rewarding work rather than replacing it. It is not flawless: the phase-out still adds a modest tax on additional earnings, which can trim hours at the intensive margin; it does little for those who cannot work; and it arrives once a year in a tax refund rather than in each paycheck. But measured against a cliff, a gentle slope is a triumph. The EITC obeys the laws of incentives, and that is precisely why it shows real promise.

If economists ruled the world · Reward work, never punish it

Keep the effective marginal tax rate on low-income workers well below 100 percent, always. Coordinate benefit phase-outs so they do not stack into cliffs, replace abrupt eligibility limits with gradual tapers, and prefer earnings subsidies like the EITC that pay people for working over transfers that withdraw sharply the moment they do. The goal is not smaller help. It is help designed so that working is never the losing choice.

Retrieval practice Why does a welfare cliff reduce work incentives, and how does the EITC avoid one? ▸
A cliff pushes the effective marginal tax rate above 100 percent, so earning more leaves a worker with fewer total resources — making it rational to not earn more. The EITC instead subsidizes the first dollars earned (a negative marginal tax rate) and phases out gradually, so working always pays.
AP Micro practice

MP, MRP, hiring, and labor-market shifts

This is the exam muscle of the chapter: table to graph to hiring decision.

MP and MRP

A firm sells output for $8. When labor rises from 3 to 4 workers, total product rises from 54 to 64 units. Calculate MP and MRP for the fourth worker.

Reveal answer ▸

MP = 64 - 54 = 10 units. MRP = MP x price = 10 x $8 = $80.

Hiring rule

A wage-taking firm faces a wage of $80. Its workers have MRP values of $140, $118, $96, $80, $62, and $35. How many workers should it hire?

Reveal answer ▸

Hire 4 workers. The first three have MRP greater than the wage, the fourth has MRP equal to the wage, and the fifth has MRP below the wage.

Market graph shift

Demand for home health care rises as the population ages. In a competitive labor market for home health aides, what happens to labor demand, wage, and employment?

Reveal answer ▸

Labor demand shifts right because demand for the output rises. Equilibrium wage rises and equilibrium employment rises, assuming labor supply slopes upward.

Market thesis

Markets allocate labor by pulling workers toward the places where their work creates the most value. In competitive markets, wages tend toward marginal revenue product: not because people are worth only what they earn, but because firms bid for labor services according to the value those services help create.

If economists ruled the world...

we would make it easy for people to work, change jobs, enter occupations, move across places, and bargain from real options. We would be skeptical of rules that sound protective but mostly keep outsiders out.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Labor demand is derived from demand for output.
  • Firms hire until MRP = MRC, or as close as possible without hiring a worker whose MRP is below wage.
  • Productivity, output demand, human capital, tools, and worker options all affect wages.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Which licensing rules genuinely protect the public, and which protect insiders from competition?
  • How should institutions respond when monopsony, discrimination, or immobility weakens worker options?
Takeaways
In your life

Specialize in skills that raise the value of your marginal contribution, then keep enough options that someone has to compete for it. Look for the place where your next hour creates more value.

In your work

Take good care of people by raising their productivity, options, and dignity. Better tools, training, processes, teams, and management can raise MRP without treating labor as disposable.
