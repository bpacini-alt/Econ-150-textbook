Imperfect Competition I:
Monopolistic Competition

A little power, competed away

Chapter 23 ~ 46 min read

Walk down any busy street and count the coffee shops. There are a dozen within a few blocks, and not one of them is a price taker selling an identical bean at a single market price. Each has its own roast, its own couches, its own playlist, its own regulars who would grumble if it closed. In that small way, every one of them is a little monopolist — the only seller of this exact cafe.

And yet none of them is getting rich. The differentiation is real, the loyalty is real, and the long-run profit is, stubbornly, about zero. Open a wildly profitable cafe and within a year three more appear down the block.

So which is it — are these tiny monopolists with real pricing power, or are they competitors with none? The honest answer is both, and holding both at once is the whole point of this chapter. It is also where Unit 4 begins its turn back toward the competitive market we started with.

The big picture
Product differentiation hands each firm a sliver of pricing power — a downward-sloping demand curve of its own. But free entry competes the profit away, until firms break even while still charging above marginal cost and running below full capacity. By the yardstick of perfect competition that looks inefficient. But the markup is buying something perfect competition can't: variety. Whether that trade is worth it is a question the model cannot answer for us.

Major concept 1

Many Firms, but None Quite the Same

Take perfect competition and change one thing: let the products differ.

Monopolistic competition is the structure that borrows from both sides of the book. From perfect competition it takes many firms and free entry and exit : nobody needs permission to open a cafe, a salon, a food truck, or an app. From monopoly it takes product differentiation : the products are close substitutes but not identical, so each firm has a few customers who prefer it specifically.

That one change — differentiation — is everything. In perfect competition every firm sells the same thing, so no one can charge a cent above the going price without losing every customer. Here, because your cafe is not quite the same as the one next door, you can raise your price a little and keep most of your regulars. You face your own downward-sloping demand curve. You are, in a small way, a price searcher rather than a price taker.

Quick check Two markets each have hundreds of sellers. In one, a firm that raises price loses every customer instantly; in the other, it loses only a few. What separates them? ▸
Differentiation. Identical products (perfect competition) mean any price above the market clears out every buyer — demand is horizontal. Differentiated products (monopolistic competition) mean some buyers prefer this seller, so it keeps most of them after a small price rise — demand slopes down.
Major concept 2

A Sliver of Pricing Power

In the short run, a differentiated firm looks a lot like a tiny monopolist.

Because each firm has its own downward-sloping demand curve, it has its own marginal-revenue curve lying below that demand — the same logic as a monopolist, for the same reason: to sell one more cup you shave the price a little. So the firm follows the familiar rule, producing where MR = MC and reading its price up on the demand curve. If demand is strong relative to cost, the firm can earn short-run economic profit, the shaded rectangle below.

This is the “monopolistic” half of the name. For a while, a genuinely beloved cafe can charge above cost and pocket the difference. The difference from a true monopoly is just one thing — and it is the thing the next concept turns on: nothing is stopping anyone else from opening a cafe too.

Fig 24.1 Short run. The firm produces where MR = MC, prices up on its demand curve at P*, and because P* exceeds ATC, earns the green profit rectangle. Its demand is more elastic (flatter) than a monopolist's, because close substitutes sit right next door.
Quick check Why does a monopolistically competitive firm's marginal revenue lie below its price, just like a monopolist's? ▸
Because it faces a downward-sloping demand curve. To sell one more unit it must lower the price, and the lower price applies to all units, so the revenue from one more unit is less than its price. Differentiation, not size, is what gives it a downward-sloping demand curve.
Major concept 3

Entry Competes the Profit Away

Free entry is the competitive half of the name — and it does its work relentlessly.

Short-run profit is a signal, and in a market with free entry the signal is answered. New cafes open. Every entrant pulls a few customers away from each existing firm, so each firm's demand curve drifts leftward and grows more elastic — with more substitutes nearby, your regulars have more places to defect to. Entry continues as long as profit is positive, and it stops only when profit reaches zero.

That resting point has a precise geometry. Economic profit is zero exactly when price equals average total cost — when the firm's demand curve has slid down until it is just tangent to its ATC curve. The firm still produces where MR = MC, and at that quantity the demand curve touches ATC at a single point: P = ATC , no profit, no loss. This is the long-run equilibrium, and it is the chapter's central graph.

Fig 24.2 Long run. Entry slides demand left until it is tangent to ATC at Q*, so P = ATC and profit is zero. But notice two things at that point: P still sits above MC, and Q* lies to the left of minimum ATC — the gap to Qmin is excess capacity.
AP watch: long-run tangency gives zero profit (P = ATC), and P > MC, and output below minimum-ATC (excess capacity), all at once.
Retrieval practice Walk the chain: short-run profit → what happens → long-run resting point. ▸

Short-run profit attracts entry. New firms pull customers away, so each existing firm's demand shifts left and becomes more elastic. Entry continues until profit is zero, which happens where demand is tangent to ATC: P = ATC. The firm still produces at MR = MC, now earning only normal profit.

Major concept 4

The Verdict Is Genuinely Split

Measured against perfect competition, it fails two tests. Measured against a world with no variety, it might be a bargain.

Stand at the long-run equilibrium and judge it by the competitive benchmark, and monopolistic competition comes up short twice. First, P > MC : there are customers who value a cup above what it costs to make, but who won't pay the marked-up price, so some mutually beneficial trades don't happen — the allocative inefficiency we first met under monopoly, in miniature. Second, the firm produces at excess capacity : output sits left of minimum ATC, so each firm is smaller and pricier per unit than it could be. A single giant coffee factory could brew each cup more cheaply.

And yet. That single giant factory would serve one coffee. The markup and the excess capacity are the price of having a dozen different cafes instead of one efficient monolith — the price of variety . The competitive benchmark counts the lost trades and the wasted capacity, but it puts no value on the fact that you can choose the roast, the room, and the vibe you actually want. Differentiation is not a trick played on consumers; much of it is something consumers genuinely want and willingly pay for.

So is monopolistic competition inefficient, or is it efficiently delivering variety? Economists can measure the markup and the excess capacity precisely. What they cannot do is tell you how much variety is worth — that is a value judgment, not a calculation. This chapter's verdict is meant to stay open. If it felt like it resolved cleanly in either direction, the resolution was smuggled in.

Misconception “Excess capacity and P > MC prove monopolistic competition is wasteful and should be fixed.” ▸

Both are real, and both are measured against a benchmark — perfect competition — that assumes the product is identical. The moment you value variety, the “waste” is partly the cost of choice. There's no free lunch where you keep the variety and erase the markup; the two come together. Calling it simply wasteful imports a judgment the economics doesn't make.

AP Micro practice

The moves you can now make

The long-run tangency graph and the three results that hold at it — zero profit, P > MC, excess capacity — are core AP. Try each before revealing.

Identify

A market has 200 firms, free entry, and products that are similar but not identical. Name the structure, and the one feature that distinguishes it from perfect competition.

Reveal answer ▸

Monopolistic competition. The distinguishing feature is product differentiation, which gives each firm a downward-sloping demand curve instead of the horizontal one a perfect competitor faces.

Graph reasoning

In long-run equilibrium, what is the relationship between the demand curve and the ATC curve, and what does it imply about economic profit?

Reveal answer ▸

Demand is tangent to ATC at the profit-maximizing quantity, so P = ATC. Economic profit is therefore zero — the firm earns only a normal profit. Entry competed away anything more.

Evaluation

Give one sense in which long-run monopolistic competition is inefficient, and one reason that inefficiency might be worth accepting.

Reveal answer ▸

Inefficient: P > MC (some valued trades don't happen) and output is below minimum ATC (excess capacity). Worth accepting: the markup and excess capacity are the cost of product variety, which consumers value and which a single efficient producer could not provide.

Market thesis

Differentiation buys each firm a sliver of pricing power, but free entry competes the profit away — leaving variety bought at the price of a markup and excess capacity, and a welfare verdict the model is honest enough to leave open.

If economists ruled the world…

they would measure the markup and the excess capacity to the penny — and then admit that the number they can't compute, how much the variety is worth to you, is the one that decides the case.

Positive
what economists agree on
  • Differentiation gives each firm a downward-sloping demand curve and a short-run chance at profit.
  • Free entry competes that profit to zero: long-run demand is tangent to ATC, so P = ATC.
  • At that point P > MC and output is below minimum ATC — a markup and excess capacity remain.
Normative
what citizens must decide
  • Is the lost efficiency worth the variety it buys?
  • Is perfect competition even the right yardstick for a market built on difference?
  • Can there be too much variety — choice that costs more than it's worth?
Takeaways
In your life

When you pay a premium for the brand, the room, or the vibe over a near-identical cheaper option, you're buying variety, not being fooled. Just know that's the trade you're making, and decide when it's worth it.

In your work

You can earn a margin by being different, but free entry erodes it. The real question for any differentiated business is whether your difference is one rivals can copy by next year — or one they can't.

Next
Monopolistic competition had so many firms that none had to watch any other. The last structure does the reverse: strip the market down to a few large rivals, each big enough that its every move lands on the others. Then the question stops being “what does the graph predict?” and becomes “what will they do if I move first?” That is the world of strategy: oligopoly.
